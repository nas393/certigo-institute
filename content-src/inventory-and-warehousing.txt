## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain the five main reasons a business holds inventory;
- identify the elements of holding cost, ordering cost and stock-out cost;
- calculate the annual holding cost of an item's average inventory;
- compare two ordering policies using total inventory cost.

## Key terms

::: terms
Cycle stock: the stock used up between one delivery and the next
Safety stock: extra stock held to protect against higher demand or late deliveries
Holding (carrying) cost: the cost of keeping stock for a period, usually a percentage of its value per year
Ordering cost: the cost of placing and receiving one order, regardless of its size
Stock-out: a customer asks for an item and you cannot supply it from stock
:::

## 1. Why inventory exists

Every bag of rice or carton of oil in a warehouse is money the business has spent but not yet recovered. Stock is still necessary, because supply and demand rarely move at the same speed. Inventory serves five functions:

- Cycle stock: you buy in batches (a container, a truck) but customers buy a little every day. On average, cycle stock equals half the order quantity.
- Safety stock: a buffer against demand above forecast and late suppliers.
- Pipeline (in-transit) stock: goods on a ship, at the port or on a truck to Huambo. Often already paid for, but not yet sellable.
- Anticipation stock: built before a known peak, such as the festive season, a promotion or an expected price rise.
- Decoupling stock: held between stages, for example between a central warehouse in Luanda and a branch in Lubango, so a problem at one stage does not stop the next.

Ask of every item: which job is this stock doing? Stock that does none of them is excess.

::: quiz
? A distributor builds extra stock of sugar in October because a price increase is announced for January. What type of stock is this?
- Cycle stock || Cycle stock covers normal demand between deliveries, not a planned build-up.
* Anticipation stock || Correct. It is built ahead of a known event, here a price increase.
- Pipeline stock || Pipeline stock is goods in transit, not goods built up in the warehouse.
- Decoupling stock || Decoupling stock separates two stages of the supply chain.
:::

## 2. Holding cost: what it really costs to keep stock

Many managers treat stock as free once the supplier is paid. In fact it carries costs every day. Build your own carrying cost rate in four steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Capital cost | Money in stock could reduce an overdraft or be invested elsewhere | Use your cost of borrowing or required return
SLIDE Step 2: Storage cost | Rent or depreciation of the warehouse, electricity, racking and security | Include warehouse labour linked to storage
SLIDE Step 3: Risk and shrinkage | Insurance, damage, pests, humidity and expiry | Add theft and admin errors
SLIDE Step 4: Add up and divide | Total yearly cost ÷ average stock value = carrying rate | Example: 15% + 5% + 1% + 3% = 24% per year
:::

Calculate your own rate rather than copying one from a book. Then:

- Average inventory = (order quantity ÷ 2) + safety stock
- Annual holding cost = average inventory × unit cost × carrying rate

## 3. Ordering cost and stock-out cost

Ordering cost covers each order's approvals, import documents and clearing, port handling, transport, unloading, inspection and invoice matching. It is roughly the same for a large or small order, so it is treated as a fixed amount per order.

Stock-out cost includes lost margin, emergency purchases, extra transport, key account penalties and customers who leave for good. It is often ignored because it is hard to see. Estimate it from the customer orders you could not fill, multiplied by your gross margin per unit.

::: quiz
? You order half the quantity, twice as often. What happens to annual ordering cost?
- It halves || Ordering cost depends on the number of orders, which has doubled.
- It stays the same || Each order costs about the same, so more orders cost more.
* It doubles || Correct. Twice as many orders means twice the ordering cost, while holding cost falls.
- It depends on the unit price || Ordering cost is a fixed cost per order, not linked to unit price.
:::

## 4. The total cost trade-off

Large, rare orders mean low ordering cost but high holding cost. Small, frequent orders mean the opposite. Cutting safety stock lowers holding cost but raises stock-out risk. The aim is not the lowest stock, but the lowest total cost at the service level the business has chosen.

::: diagram steps | Comparing two ordering policies
List options: for example order monthly or every two months
Holding cost: average inventory × unit cost × carrying rate
Ordering cost: orders per year × cost per order
Stock-out cost: estimate it if service levels differ
Compare totals: choose the lowest total at the required service level
:::

## Worked example: rice at a food distributor in Luanda

A food distributor in Luanda sells 60,000 bags of 25 kg rice per year. Each bag costs 18,000 Kz landed. The carrying rate is 24%, each import order costs about 450,000 Kz, and safety stock is 2,000 bags.

Current policy: 10,000 bags every two months (6 orders).

- Average inventory = 10,000 ÷ 2 + 2,000 = 7,000 bags
- Holding cost = 7,000 × 18,000 × 24% = 30,240,000 Kz
- Ordering cost = 6 × 450,000 = 2,700,000 Kz
- Total = 32,940,000 Kz per year

Proposed policy: 5,000 bags every month (12 orders).

- Average inventory = 5,000 ÷ 2 + 2,000 = 4,500 bags
- Holding cost = 4,500 × 18,000 × 24% = 19,440,000 Kz
- Ordering cost = 12 × 450,000 = 5,400,000 Kz
- Total = 24,840,000 Kz per year

The monthly policy saves about 8.1 million Kz per year and frees 45 million Kz of working capital. Before deciding, the commercial director checks that the supplier can ship monthly and that port delays will not require more safety stock.

::: case | A discount offer on cooking oil in Luanda
Sofia, a buyer in Luanda, normally orders one month of cooking oil at a time. A supplier offers a 2% discount if she buys three months at once. The company's carrying rate is 24% per year, which is 2% per month, and the warehouse is almost full.
? What should Sofia do?
- Accept at once, because a discount always saves money || The discount is only part of the picture. Extra stock also carries holding cost and needs space.
* Compare the discount with the extra holding cost and check space before deciding || Correct. Average stock rises by about one month of sales for three months, an extra holding cost of about 2% of the order value, which cancels the discount, and space is a real constraint.
- Refuse, because holding more stock is always wrong || Anticipation stock can be right. The decision should be based on total cost, not a rule.
- Accept and store the extra oil outside the warehouse || This adds damage and theft risk and hides the true holding cost.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
Annual consumption value: annual quantity sold or used multiplied by unit cost
Pareto principle: a small share of causes often accounts for a large share of results
SKU (stock keeping unit): one specific item tracked separately, such as one brand of 25 kg rice
Standard deviation: a measure of how far values spread around their average
Coefficient of variation (CV): standard deviation divided by the mean
:::

## 1. ABC analysis: ranking by value

A distributor may carry hundreds of SKUs, and no team can control all of them with the same care. ABC analysis applies the Pareto principle to inventory: a few items usually account for most of the money. In many businesses roughly 20% of SKUs represent about 80% of annual value, although the split varies. Follow five steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Annual value | For each SKU: annual units × unit cost | Use cost, not selling price
SLIDE Step 2: Sort | Rank SKUs from highest to lowest annual value | One list for the whole range
SLIDE Step 3: Cumulative % | Calculate each SKU's share of total value and the running total | A spreadsheet does this in minutes
SLIDE Step 4: Cut-offs | A up to about 80% of value, B up to about 95%, C the rest | Write your rule down
SLIDE Step 5: Assign and record | Put the class in your system and review every 6 to 12 months | Classes change as prices and ranges change
:::

A items deserve accurate forecasts, frequent review, tight safety stock and frequent counting. C items can be managed with simple rules and larger, less frequent orders. One exception: a low-value item can still be critical, such as a spare part that stops a line or a product a key account insists on. Flag these as critical regardless of class.

::: quiz
? An item sells 2,000 units per year at a cost of 5,000 Kz. Another sells 50,000 units at 100 Kz. Which ranks higher in ABC analysis?
* The first item, with 10,000,000 Kz of annual value || Correct. ABC ranks by annual consumption value, and 10 million Kz is larger than 5 million Kz.
- The second item, because it sells more units || ABC uses value, not units. Ranking by units puts cheap fast movers on top.
- They rank equally || Their annual values are 10 million Kz and 5 million Kz, so they differ.
- It depends on the selling price || ABC uses cost, so the selling price is not needed.
:::

## 2. XYZ analysis: ranking by demand stability

ABC shows how much an item matters, but not how hard it is to plan. XYZ adds that dimension by measuring demand stability:

- CV = standard deviation of demand ÷ average demand

Use monthly or weekly sales for at least 6, preferably 12, periods. In Excel, use AVERAGE and STDEV.S. A common set of cut-offs is:

- X: CV below 0.5. Stable and easy to forecast.
- Y: CV from 0.5 to 1.0. Variable, often seasonal or promotion-driven.
- Z: CV above 1.0. Erratic, with zero months or occasional large orders.

Some companies use tighter limits; choose yours and apply them consistently. Remove or explain one-off events such as a single large tender first, otherwise a stable item looks erratic.

::: quiz
? Average monthly demand is 400 units and the standard deviation is 300. What is the XYZ class?
- X, because demand is above zero every month || Class depends on the CV, not on whether demand is zero.
- Z, because the standard deviation is large || 300 looks large, but relative to the mean of 400 the CV is 0.75.
* Y, because the CV is 0.75 || Correct. 300 ÷ 400 = 0.75, which falls between 0.5 and 1.0.
- It cannot be classified without unit cost || XYZ uses only demand data. Cost is used in ABC.
:::

## 3. The ABC-XYZ matrix and stock policies

Combining both analyses gives nine cells. The four corners show the logic:

::: diagram matrix | ABC-XYZ corners | Demand variability (X stable → Z erratic) | Annual value (C low → A high)
AX: high value, stable – replenish often in small lots, low safety stock, weekly review
AZ: high value, erratic – avoid speculative stock, buy against confirmed orders
CX: low value, stable – simple min-max, larger orders, rare review
CZ: low value, erratic – stock little or nothing, order on demand or delist
:::

The middle cells follow the same logic. AY items need careful forecasts with sales input and planning for seasons and promotions. B items use the A policies with less frequent reviews. CY items work well on min-max with slightly higher safety stock, which is cheaper than close monitoring.

## Key terms

::: terms
Lead time: the time from placing an order to having the goods available for sale
Reorder point (ROP): the stock level at which a new order must be placed
Service level: the probability of not running out during one replenishment cycle
Inventory position: stock on hand plus stock on order, minus stock reserved for customers
Economic order quantity (EOQ): the order size that minimises annual ordering plus holding cost
:::

## 1. The reorder point

In a reorder point system, you watch the inventory position and place a fixed-size order whenever it falls to the reorder point:

- ROP = (average daily demand × lead time in days) + safety stock

Three practical rules:

- Measure lead time to goods available for sale, including supplier processing, transit, port, clearance and receiving.
- Compare the ROP with the inventory position, not shelf stock, or you will reorder goods already on the way.
- Keep units consistent: daily demand with lead time in days.

::: quiz
? Daily demand is 50 units, lead time 6 days and safety stock 120 units. Shelf stock is 300 and 200 units are on order. Should you order now?
- Yes, because shelf stock of 300 is below the ROP of 420 || You must compare the inventory position, not shelf stock.
* No, because the inventory position of 500 is above the ROP of 420 || Correct. 300 on hand + 200 on order = 500, which is above 50 × 6 + 120 = 420.
- Yes, because safety stock has been used || Safety stock is still intact. The position is above the ROP.
- No, because the ROP is 300 || The ROP is 50 × 6 + 120 = 420 units.
:::

## 2. Safety stock and service level

When daily demand varies and lead time is fixed, the standard formula is:

- Safety stock = z × σd × √L

σd is the standard deviation of daily demand, L the lead time in days and z the factor matching your service level: 90% = 1.28, 95% = 1.65, 98% = 2.05, 99% = 2.33. Moving from 95% to 99% raises z, and safety stock, by over 40%. Choose service levels by class, for example 98% for AX items sold to key accounts and 90% for C items.

When lead time also varies, which is common for imports, use the extended formula:

- Safety stock = z × √(L × σd² + d² × σL²)

Here d is average daily demand and σL the standard deviation of lead time in days. Port and clearance delays can make lead time variation the biggest driver of safety stock.

## 3. The economic order quantity

The EOQ model balances the two costs from lesson 1. The formula, usually credited to Ford W. Harris (1913), is:

- EOQ = √(2 × D × S ÷ H)

D is annual demand, S the cost per order and H the holding cost of one unit for one year (unit cost × carrying rate). At the EOQ, annual ordering cost equals annual holding cost. The model assumes steady demand and no quantity discounts, so treat it as a guide. Its total cost curve is flat near the EOQ, so you can round to full trucks or supplier minimums at little extra cost. Apply it in four steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Collect data | Annual demand D, cost per order S, unit cost and carrying rate | Use last 12 months of data
SLIDE Step 2: Calculate H and EOQ | H = unit cost × carrying rate | EOQ = √(2DS ÷ H)
SLIDE Step 3: List practical options | Full pallets, trucks or containers near the EOQ | Include supplier minimums
SLIDE Step 4: Compare total cost | (D ÷ Q) × S + (Q ÷ 2) × H for each option | Choose the lowest practical option
:::

::: diagram steps | Setting replenishment parameters
Classify: confirm ABC-XYZ class and service level
Measure demand: average and standard deviation per day
Measure lead time: average and standard deviation in days
Safety stock and ROP: apply the formulas
Order quantity: EOQ rounded to practical sizes
Review: recalculate at least every quarter
:::

::: quiz
? The EOQ is 894 units but trucks carry 600. Total cost at 1,200 units is about 4% above the EOQ cost. What does this show?
- EOQ is wrong and should be ignored || EOQ is a useful guide. It shows the lowest cost point.
* The cost curve is flat, so rounding to full trucks costs little || Correct. Near the EOQ, total cost changes slowly, so practical rounding is sensible.
- You should always order exactly the EOQ || Partial trucks would add transport cost not in the model.
- Holding cost is zero at 1,200 units || Holding cost rises with order size.
:::

## Worked example: sugar at a distributor in Luanda

A distributor in Luanda buys 50 kg sugar bags from a supplier in Benguela province: d = 80 bags per day, σd = 20 bags, L = 9 days, 300 selling days, unit cost 30,000 Kz, carrying rate 24%, S = 120,000 Kz, target service level 95% (z = 1.65).

- Safety stock = 1.65 × 20 × √9 = 99 bags
- ROP = 80 × 9 + 99 = 819 bags

The supplier's lead time actually varies, with σL = 2 days:

- L × σd² = 9 × 400 = 3,600 and d² × σL² = 6,400 × 4 = 25,600
- Safety stock = 1.65 × √29,200 = 1.65 × 170.9 = 282 bags, so ROP = 1,002 bags

Lead time uncertainty almost triples safety stock, so the planner discusses a fixed weekly delivery slot with the supplier.

Order quantity: D = 24,000 bags, H = 7,200 Kz.

- EOQ = √(2 × 24,000 × 120,000 ÷ 7,200) = √800,000 = about 894 bags
- 600 bags (one truck): 4,800,000 + 2,160,000 = 6,960,000 Kz
- 1,200 bags (two trucks): 2,400,000 + 4,320,000 = 6,720,000 Kz, against about 6,440,000 Kz at the EOQ

The planner chooses two full trucks, ordering roughly every 15 selling days.

## 1. The six-step warehouse flow

Every warehouse, from a depot in Malanje to a distribution centre in Viana, follows the same flow. Errors made early travel through to the customer, so each step needs controls.

::: diagram steps | Warehouse flow
Receiving: check, count and accept goods against the purchase order
Put-away: move goods to a confirmed location
Storage: keep goods safe, accessible and recorded
Picking: collect the right items for each order
Packing and checking: confirm item, quantity and batch
Dispatch: stage, load, seal and deliver with proof
:::

Receiving is where you take responsibility for the goods. Follow a standard procedure:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Prepare | Book a delivery slot | Dock, staff and equipment ready
SLIDE Step 2: Check documents | Delivery note or container papers against the purchase order | Check seal numbers on containers
SLIDE Step 3: Count and inspect | Pallets, then cartons per pallet | Damage, wet bags, pests, expiry dates
SLIDE Step 4: Record and sign | Write every discrepancy on the delivery note before signing | Photograph damage
SLIDE Step 5: Label and enter | Label item, batch, expiry and quantity | Enter the receipt in the system the same day
:::

Put-away should be directed: the system or supervisor names the location, and the operator confirms it after placing the pallet, by scanning or writing it on the put-away sheet. Without this, stock is "somewhere in the warehouse".

::: quiz
? A driver delivering 400 bags of flour is in a hurry. The clerk counts 385. What should she do?
- Sign the note and report the shortage later by phone || Once signed without remarks, the shortage is hard to claim.
* Write the 15-bag shortage on the delivery note, then sign || Correct. Recording discrepancies before signing protects the company's claim.
- Refuse the whole delivery || Usually unnecessary. Accept what arrived and record the shortage.
- Sign "subject to count" || This vague remark rarely supports a claim and hides the real figure.
:::

## 2. Storage: locations and equipment

Fixed locations give each SKU the same place, which is easy to learn but wastes space when stock is low. Random (dynamic) locations put a pallet in any free suitable location, which uses space far better but needs an accurate system. Many warehouses combine fixed picking locations at ground level with random reserve locations above.

Selective pallet racking makes every pallet accessible. Drive-in racking stores many pallets of few SKUs densely, but works last in, first out, a problem for products with expiry dates. Block stacking is cheap but limited by safe stacking height.

## 3. Picking, packing and dispatch

Picking is often the largest share of warehouse labour, and most of it is walking. The main methods are:

- Discrete picking: one picker, one order. Simple and accurate for large orders.
- Batch picking: one picker collects several orders in one trip, then sorts them. Less travel for many small orders.
- Zone picking: each picker works one area and orders pass between zones.
- Wave picking: orders are released in timed groups, for example by route or truck departure.

Checking confirms items, quantities and batches before wrapping. At dispatch, stage orders by route, load in reverse drop order, check the load against documents, seal the truck, record the seal number and collect signed proof of delivery.

::: quiz
? A warehouse has 150 small retail orders a day, all on four delivery routes. Which picking approach fits best?
- Discrete picking, one order at a time || This means 150 separate walks through the warehouse.
- Zone picking with one picker per aisle || Possible in large sites, but it adds hand-overs for small orders.
* Batch picking by route, released in waves || Correct. Grouping orders by route cuts walking and matches truck departures.
- Picking whatever orders arrive first || Without grouping, travel and missed departures increase.
:::

## 4. Slotting

Good slotting reduces travel, speeds up picking and improves safety:

- Rank SKUs by pick frequency (order lines per month), not by sales value.
- Place the fastest movers near dispatch on the main aisle.
- Use the golden zone, between waist and shoulder height, for the most-picked cartons.
- Store heavy items such as 50 kg bags at floor level.
- Group products often ordered together and separate similar-looking items.
- Review slotting every quarter.

## Worked example: re-slotting a food warehouse in Viana

A food warehouse in Viana handles about 400 order lines per day. Pickers complain of long walks, and two pasta brands with similar cartons are often confused. The supervisor ranks three months of order lines: 20 SKUs generate about 60% of lines, but eight sit at the far end because space happened to be free there.

She takes four actions:

- Moves the 20 fastest SKUs to ground-level locations in the two aisles nearest dispatch.
- Separates the two pasta brands and adds large location labels.
- Introduces directed put-away with the location written on each pallet label and in the system.
- Switches small retail orders to batch picking by route, released at 07:00 and 11:00 to match truck departures.

She then measures walking distance on sample orders and picking errors at checking each week.

## Key terms

::: terms
Cycle counting: counting a small part of the inventory on a regular schedule
Inventory record accuracy (IRA): % of counted locations where system and physical quantity match within tolerance
FIFO: first in, first out, stock received first is issued first
FEFO: first expired, first out, the earliest expiry date is issued first
Shrinkage: the gap between system stock value and physical stock value
:::

## 1. Cycle counting

Every decision in lessons 1 to 3 assumes the system quantity is right. If the system says 800 bags but the shelf holds 650, the reorder point triggers too late. An annual stock take stops the warehouse and finds errors months after they happened. Cycle counting counts a few locations every day, so causes can still be traced. Build it in five steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Set frequency by class | A monthly, B quarterly, C twice a year | Extra counts for high-theft items
SLIDE Step 2: Calculate workload | 100 A × 12 + 300 B × 4 + 600 C × 2 = 3,600 counts a year | About 14 per working day
SLIDE Step 3: Count blind | Counters do not see the system quantity | They cannot simply confirm it
SLIDE Step 4: Recount differences | Second person recounts anything above tolerance | Freeze movement during counts
SLIDE Step 5: Find the root cause | Unrecorded receipt, wrong location, wrong unit, unbooked damage, theft | Fix the process, then adjust
:::

::: quiz
? Why are cycle counts done "blind"?
- To make counting faster || Blind counts are not faster. Their purpose is accuracy.
* So counters record what they see instead of confirming the system figure || Correct. Seeing the system number tempts people to agree with it.
- Because the system quantity is secret || It is not secret. It is hidden only during the count.
- So no recount is needed || Differences above tolerance are still recounted.
:::

## 2. Measuring inventory record accuracy

A location is accurate when the count is within the agreed tolerance: tighter for A items (for example zero difference), wider for low-value C items.

- IRA = locations within tolerance ÷ locations counted × 100

Track it weekly and by cause. The goal is reliable records that planners trust, not numbers that match because the system was adjusted.

## 3. FIFO and FEFO for food products

Food, beverages, dairy and medicines have expiry dates. FIFO issues the oldest received stock first, which works when every batch has the same shelf life. FEFO issues the earliest expiry date first. It is the safer rule for food, because a container that arrives later may contain older production than goods already in stock.

::: diagram steps | FEFO expiry control
Receive: check minimum remaining shelf life agreed in the contract
Label: batch and expiry on every pallet and in the system
Store: earlier-expiry pallets reachable first
Pick: picking list names the batch, checked at dispatch
Report: weekly list of stock with less than 90 days left
Act: promote, transfer or return early, and quarantine expired goods
:::

Selective racking supports FEFO; drive-in racking and deep floor stacks make it hard. Move expired or damaged food at once to a marked, locked quarantine area and dispose of it under your company procedure and the applicable regulations, so it can never ship by mistake.

::: quiz
? Batch A arrived in March with expiry in June. Batch B arrived in April with expiry in May. Under FEFO, which is picked first?
- Batch A, because it arrived first || That is FIFO. FEFO looks at expiry, not arrival.
* Batch B, because it expires first || Correct. FEFO issues the earliest expiry date first.
- Whichever is nearer the aisle || Convenience picking causes expiry losses.
- Both at the same time || Mixing batches makes rotation and traceability harder.
:::

## 4. Shrinkage control

Shrinkage comes from theft, unrecorded damage, administrative errors and short deliveries signed for.

- Shrinkage % = (book value − physical value) ÷ book value × 100

Match controls to sources:

- Segregation of duties: people who handle stock cannot adjust records.
- Restricted access, visitor log and CCTV at docks and high-value cages.
- Two-person checks and sealed trucks for high-value loads.
- Damage booked the same day with a reason code.
- Approval levels for adjustments and a monthly review by person and item.

## Worked example: cycle counting at a distributor in Lobito

A food distributor near Lobito port held 850 million Kz of stock in the system, but the annual stock take found 838 million Kz: 12 million Kz, or 1.4%, shrinkage. Also, 2 million Kz of tomato paste had expired in a back row.

The new manager starts cycle counting. In month one, 176 of 200 blind-counted locations are within tolerance: IRA = 88%. The 24 errors have three causes: 10 receipts entered as cartons instead of trays, 8 picks from the wrong location, and 6 damaged bags thrown away unbooked.

He adds a unit-of-measure check at receiving, location barcode scanning at picking and a daily signed damage register. Tomato paste is re-stacked by expiry with a weekly near-expiry report. After four months, IRA reaches 97% and no stock has expired for two months.

## Key terms

::: terms
KPI (key performance indicator): a measure with a clear definition and target that shows whether an objective is met
Order line: one SKU on one customer order, regardless of quantity
Dock-to-stock time: time from arrival at the dock until goods are in a recorded location and available to pick
Inventory turns: how many times average inventory is sold and replaced in a year
Capacity plate: the forklift plate showing the maximum load at a given height and load centre
:::

## 1. Four core warehouse KPIs

Four indicators give a strong picture of accuracy, speed and stock health.

- Inventory record accuracy = locations within tolerance ÷ locations counted × 100
- Picking accuracy = (lines picked − lines with errors) ÷ lines picked × 100
- Dock-to-stock time = time receipt and location are confirmed − arrival time
- Inventory turns = annual cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory at cost, and days of inventory = 365 ÷ turns

Measure picking accuracy by line, because one order may have 30 lines. Errors include wrong item, quantity, batch or damaged goods. Report errors found at checking and errors found by customers separately; the second kind is the expensive one. Long dock-to-stock time means stock sits invisible to sales and can trigger needless emergency orders. Higher turns free cash, but turns that rise because of stock-outs are not a success, so read turns with service level.

::: quiz
? 4,000 order lines were picked. Checking found 28 errors and customers reported 8 more. What is picking accuracy?
- 99.3%, using only the checking errors || Customer-found errors must be included.
* 99.1%, because (4,000 − 36) ÷ 4,000 = 99.1% || Correct. All 36 errors count.
- 99.8%, using only customer errors || Errors found at checking are still picking errors.
- 90.0% || This would mean 400 errors.
:::

## 2. Making KPIs work

For each KPI, write down the definition, data source, a target based on your own performance and business needs, and an owner. Hold a short weekly stand-up at a board near dispatch: show eight weeks of trend, discuss the biggest cause of misses and agree one or two actions with names and dates.

## 3. Forklift safety basics

Forklifts are essential and a major cause of serious injuries. Apply these rules with your company procedures and the applicable local regulations:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Authorised drivers only | Trained and authorised operators | Keep a list with training dates
SLIDE Step 2: Pre-use check every shift | Brakes, steering, horn, lights, forks, chains, tyres, leaks, seatbelt | Tag out and report any failure
SLIDE Step 3: Lift within capacity | Read the capacity plate | Capacity falls as the load goes higher or further out
SLIDE Step 4: Travel safely | Forks low, mast tilted back, seatbelt on, speed limits | Horn at corners, no passengers
SLIDE Step 5: Separate people | Marked walkways, barriers, pedestrian-free dock zones | Eye contact before crossing
:::

The hierarchy of controls shows which measures work best against forklift and pedestrian collisions:

::: diagram pyramid | Hierarchy of controls for forklift traffic
Elimination: plan layouts so pedestrians never need to enter forklift areas
Substitution: use pedestrian pallet trucks in busy picking aisles
Engineering controls: barriers, separate walkways and doors
Administrative controls: speed limits, one-way routes, training
PPE: high-visibility vests and safety shoes
:::

## 4. Racking safety basics

Pallet racking holds tonnes above people's heads. Key rules:

- Display a load notice on every rack run and never exceed it. Do not change beam heights without checking with the supplier.
- Protect uprights at aisle ends with guards and barriers.
- Never climb racking or stand on forks.
- Place pallets squarely and never rack damaged pallets.
- Report every impact, even with no visible damage, and arrange regular inspections by a trained person plus an expert inspection at least once a year.

The European standard EN 15635 uses a traffic-light classification. Green: damage within limits, record and monitor. Amber: hazardous, unload the area and repair soon, with no new loads. Red: very serious, unload and isolate immediately until repaired.

::: quiz
? An inspection classes a damaged upright as amber. What should happen?
- Keep using it but reduce speed nearby || Amber damage must not carry new loads.
- Nothing until the annual inspection || Amber needs action within a short period.
* Unload it, put no new loads on it and repair it soon || Correct. Amber means hazardous damage needing prompt repair.
- Evacuate the whole warehouse || That response fits a collapse, not a single amber upright.
:::

## Worked example: a KPI and safety review in Luanda

A distribution warehouse in Luanda prepares its first monthly report:

- Turns = 2,400 million Kz cost of goods sold ÷ 300 million Kz average inventory = 8, days of inventory = 365 ÷ 8 = about 46.
- Picking accuracy = (4,000 − 36) ÷ 4,000 = 99.1%.
- Dock-to-stock time averages 26 hours because afternoon containers are entered the next day.
- Inventory record accuracy: 95%.

The receiving supervisor introduces same-shift system entry and books containers before 13:00; dock-to-stock reaches the new 8-hour target in two months. The safety officer reports two rack impacts in aisle 3; one upright is amber, so the bay is unloaded and the upright replaced within the week. Two forklifts had no pre-use checklists, so supervisors now check forms at 07:30 daily.
