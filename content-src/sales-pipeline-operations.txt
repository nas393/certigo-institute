## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain why pipeline stages must describe the buyer's progress, not the seller's activity;
- write exit criteria for each stage as verifiable buyer actions;
- apply BANT and MEDDIC as qualification gates;
- calculate stage probabilities from your own closed deals.

## Key terms

::: terms
Sales pipeline: all open sales opportunities, each placed in the stage it has reached
Stage: a step in the buying and selling process, such as Qualified or Offer submitted
Exit criterion: the verifiable event that must happen before a deal can move to the next stage
Qualification: checking that an opportunity is real and worth the sales team's time
Stage probability: the share of deals at a stage that were eventually won, based on past results
:::

## 1. Stages that follow the buyer

Weak pipelines often use stages that describe what the salesperson did, such as "Visit made" or "Price list sent". A rep can do both while the customer decides nothing. Such a pipeline looks full and forecasts badly.

A healthy pipeline uses stages that mark buyer progress. Design it in five steps:

- Map how your customers buy. A supermarket chain in Talatona may need a listing meeting, a category manager's approval and a supplier registration. A family wholesaler in Kikolo may decide in one conversation with the owner.
- Choose five to seven stages. Fewer hides problems; more creates confusion.
- Write one exit criterion per stage as a buyer action with evidence.
- Set a stage probability from your own history (section 3).
- Publish a stage dictionary so every rep and manager uses the same definitions.

::: diagram steps | A B2B distributor pipeline with exit criteria
Identified: outlet fits the target profile and a contact is known
Qualified: BANT confirmed in a meeting with the decision-maker
Needs confirmed: buyer agrees the range, volumes and service needed
Offer submitted: buyer has the written offer and a review date is set
Terms agreed: price, credit and delivery terms accepted in writing
Won or Lost: first order received and account opened, or a clear no
:::

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Name the buyer action | Not "I sent the offer" but "the buyer agreed a review date" | Start from what the customer does
SLIDE Step 2: Name the evidence | Email, signed form, meeting note or order number | Evidence must be visible to the manager
SLIDE Step 3: Name who checks | Rep self-checks daily and manager spot-checks weekly | Unchecked criteria are soon ignored
SLIDE Step 4: Test it | Give two reps the same deal and ask which stage it is in | Same answer means a clear criterion
:::

## 2. Qualification as the first gate

The most important exit criterion is the one into Qualified, because every later metric depends on it. Two widely used checklists help.

BANT asks four questions: Budget (can the customer pay, in cash or on terms you accept?), Authority (are you talking to the person who decides?), Need (is there a real problem you can solve?) and Timing (when will they buy?).

MEDDIC, which came out of enterprise technology sales in the 1990s, is deeper and suits larger or longer deals, such as a B2B services contract or a supermarket chain listing: Metrics (the measurable gain for the customer), Economic buyer (who controls the money), Decision criteria (how options will be judged), Decision process (the steps and people involved), Identify pain (the business problem) and Champion (a person inside the customer who actively supports you).

Use BANT for small, fast deals such as a new cantina. Use MEDDIC above a value you define.

::: quiz
? A hotel's purchasing officer loves your new cooking oil range, but the general manager signs every supplier contract and has not met you. Which MEDDIC element is missing?
- Identify pain || The hotel may well have a problem to solve. The bigger gap is who controls the money.
* Economic buyer || Correct. The general manager controls the decision and the budget, and you have not met him.
- Champion || The purchasing officer may become your champion. That is a strength, not the gap.
- Metrics || Metrics matter, but you cannot agree them without the economic buyer.
:::

## 3. Stage probabilities from your own history

A stage probability tells you how likely a deal at that stage is to be won. Do not copy percentages from a book or another company. Calculate them from your own closed deals:

- Stage probability = deals won that passed through the stage ÷ all closed deals (won plus lost) that passed through the stage × 100

Use only closed deals, at least twelve months of data, and review every quarter. Example: 60 closed deals reached Qualified and 15 were won, so Qualified = 25%. 30 reached Offer submitted and 12 were won, so Offer submitted = 40%.

::: quiz
? Last year 50 closed deals reached Offer submitted. 20 were won and 30 were lost. Ten more are still open. What is the stage probability?
- 33% || This divides 20 by 60 and wrongly includes the open deals.
- 50% || There is no basis for 50%. Use your own results.
* 40% || Correct. 20 won ÷ 50 closed = 40%. Open deals are excluded.
- 60% || This is the loss rate, not the win probability.
:::

## Worked example: the HoReCa pipeline in Luanda

Rui manages HoReCa sales for a food distributor in Luanda. His team's pipeline had eight stages, including "Visit made" and "Samples delivered", and showed 140,000,000 Kz, but the team won only a small part of it each quarter.

Rui rewrites the stages using the six-stage model above. He adds exit criteria: a deal is Qualified only after a meeting with the person who signs supplier contracts, and Terms agreed needs a signed credit application. He then checks every open deal against the new criteria.

- 18 deals worth 52,000,000 Kz had no meeting with a decision-maker and move back to Identified.
- 6 deals worth 21,000,000 Kz had no contact for 90 days and are closed as lost.
- The real pipeline is 67,000,000 Kz.

The number is smaller but true: Rui needs more qualified hotels, not more samples.

? What is the most accurate answer?
- Terms agreed, because the administrator liked the price || Liking a price is not acceptance. There is no written agreement.
* Needs confirmed, because the offer has no agreed review date yet || Correct. The offer was sent, but the exit criterion for Offer submitted needs a review date. Her next step is to get one.
- Won, because the building has no other supplier || No order or contract exists, so the deal cannot be won.

## Key terms

::: terms
Single source of truth: one agreed place where all pipeline data is kept and from which all reports are produced
Required field: information that must be filled in before a record is accepted
Data dictionary: a short document that defines every field, its format and its allowed values
Stale opportunity: an open deal with no recorded customer activity for a set number of days
Close date slippage: moving a deal's expected close date later, often more than once
:::

## 1. The minimum viable opportunity record

Metrics, forecasts and reviews are only as good as the data behind them. Many Angolan sales teams work from WhatsApp messages and one Excel file per rep. A well-built spreadsheet can work as well as a CRM. The problem is data that is incomplete, inconsistent or kept in private lists.

Start with one rule: one source of truth. All opportunities live in one shared file or CRM, and every report comes from it.

Every opportunity then needs a minimum set of fields:

- Account name and segment (wholesaler, supermarket, HoReCa, corporate).
- Main contact and their role in the decision.
- Value, and how it was calculated (for example, monthly volume × price × 12).
- Stage and the date the deal entered that stage.
- Expected close date.
- Next step and its date.
- Owner (the responsible rep) and lead source.
- Created date, and for closed deals, the result and the lost reason.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: One row per opportunity | Each deal is one row and each field is one column | No merged cells or blank rows
SLIDE Step 2: Lock the lists | Use data validation drop-downs for stage, segment, owner and lost reason | Free text creates ten spellings of one stage
SLIDE Step 3: Format the dates | Close date, stage-entry date and next-step date in real date format | Dates you can sort and calculate with
SLIDE Step 4: Keep a history tab | Each time a stage changes, add a row with deal ID, old stage, new stage and date | History lets you calculate conversion and cycle time later
:::

## 2. Data quality dimensions and hygiene rules

Six data quality dimensions are useful for a pipeline, each checked with a simple rule:

- Completeness: are all required fields filled? Rule: no deal enters Qualified without value, close date and next step.
- Accuracy: does the record match reality? Rule: managers spot-check three deals per rep weekly.
- Timeliness: is it up to date? Rule: update within 24 hours of a customer contact.
- Consistency: is the same thing recorded the same way? Rule: values always in Kz, annual or monthly as defined.
- Validity: do values follow the allowed format? Rule: stage comes from the drop-down.
- Uniqueness: is each deal recorded once? Rule: search for the account before creating a deal.

Add three hygiene rules: a past close date must be updated or the deal closed, 30 days without customer activity marks a deal stale, and a close date moved more than twice is discussed in the weekly review.

A simple way to track progress is a completeness score:

- Completeness score = records with all required fields ÷ total open records × 100

::: quiz
? A pipeline has 80 open deals. 62 have all required fields filled. What is the completeness score?
- 62% || This uses the number of complete records as a percentage without dividing by the total.
* 77.5% || Correct. 62 ÷ 80 × 100 = 77.5%.
- 22.5% || This is the share of incomplete records.
- 81% || Check the division: 62 ÷ 80 = 0.775.
:::

## 3. The hygiene routine

Clean data comes from small, regular habits, not a big clean-up.

::: diagram cycle | The pipeline hygiene cycle
Capture: rep records every customer contact within 24 hours
Validate: required fields and drop-downs block bad entries
Review: weekly exception report shows stale, past-due and incomplete deals
Correct: owners fix or close the flagged deals before the review meeting
:::

Daily, reps update each deal after a visit or call. Weekly, a coordinator sends an exception report the day before the pipeline review, and reps fix flagged deals before the meeting. Monthly, the manager removes duplicates, checks lost reasons and archives closed deals.

::: quiz
? A rep's deal shows a close date of 15 March. Today is 2 April and there is no order. What does the hygiene rule require?
- Leave it, because the customer may still order || A past close date makes the forecast wrong. It must be acted on.
- Move the date to the end of the year to be safe || A random date hides the problem and damages accuracy.
* Update the close date with the customer's real timing, or close the deal as lost || Correct. The record must reflect reality, based on the latest customer contact.
- Delete the record || Deleting removes history that is needed for win rates and lost reasons.
:::

## Worked example: an IT services firm in Luanda

An IT services firm in Luanda has four account managers, each with their own Excel file. The commercial director merges them and finds:

- 112 open deals, but 9 were duplicates entered by two people.
- Values mixed monthly fees and annual contract values.
- 41 deals had a close date in the past.
- Only 58 of the 103 unique deals had a next step with a date.

She creates one shared spreadsheet with a data dictionary: values are annual contract value in Kz, stages come from a drop-down, and next step and date are required from Qualified onwards. Fourteen past-due deals are closed as lost, with reasons.

After four weeks, completeness rises from 56% (58 ÷ 103) to 91%, and the pipeline falls from 410,000,000 Kz to a trustworthy 265,000,000 Kz.

Joaquim sells to supermarkets for a food distributor. A chain in Luanda has been in his pipeline at Offer submitted for five months. He has moved the close date forward four times. Each time he says the buyer is "about to decide". The exception report flags the deal.
? What should his manager do?
- Accept the new date because Joaquim knows the customer best || Four delays without new evidence suggest the deal is not progressing.
- Remove the deal from Joaquim's targets || This does not solve the data or the deal problem.
* Ask what the buyer has done since the last date and agree to move it back a stage or close it unless there is new evidence || Correct. Stage and date must be based on buyer actions. Repeated slippage is a warning sign.
- Raise the stage probability to make up for the delay || Probability comes from history, not from the manager's wish.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
Pipeline coverage ratio: open pipeline value expected to close in a period divided by the target still to be achieved
Win rate: the share of closed opportunities that were won
Stage conversion rate: the share of deals entering a stage that move on to the next stage
Sales cycle length: the average number of days from creating an opportunity to winning it
Average deal size: the average value of won deals
Sales velocity: the value of sales a pipeline produces per day
:::

## 1. Pipeline coverage

Coverage tells you whether there is enough pipeline to reach the target.

- Coverage ratio = open pipeline value with close dates in the period ÷ target still to be achieved in the period

Example: the quarterly target for new business is 90,000,000 Kz. 30,000,000 Kz is already won, so 60,000,000 Kz remains. Open deals closing this quarter total 150,000,000 Kz. Coverage = 150 ÷ 60 = 2.5x.

Many teams use a 3x rule of thumb. Better: calculate the coverage your own win rate requires, required coverage ≈ 1 ÷ win rate (by value). At 25% you need about 4x, at 50% about 2x. Count only deals that passed the Qualified exit criterion.

::: quiz
? A team wins 20% of its pipeline value. The remaining target this quarter is 40,000,000 Kz. About how much qualified pipeline does it need?
- 80,000,000 Kz || This assumes a 50% win rate.
- 120,000,000 Kz || This uses the 3x rule of thumb, not the team's own win rate.
- 48,000,000 Kz || This adds 20% to the target instead of dividing by the win rate.
* 200,000,000 Kz || Correct. Required coverage = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5x, and 5 × 40,000,000 = 200,000,000 Kz.
:::

## 2. Win rate and stage conversion

Win rate measures how often you win once a deal is decided:

- Win rate (by count) = deals won ÷ (deals won + deals lost) × 100
- Win rate (by value) = value won ÷ (value won + value lost) × 100

Never include open deals. Calculate both versions: a 40% count win rate with a 20% value win rate means you win small deals and lose large ones.

Stage conversion rates show where deals get stuck:

- Stage conversion = deals that reached the next stage ÷ deals that entered this stage × 100

If Qualified to Needs confirmed is 70% but Offer submitted to Terms agreed is 30%, the problem is the offer or negotiation. Fix the weakest stage first.

## 3. Sales cycle length and sales velocity

Calculate sales cycle length from won deals only, and by segment: a mini-market may take 10 days, a supermarket chain listing 120 days.

Sales velocity combines four metrics into one number:

- Sales velocity = (number of qualified opportunities × win rate × average deal size) ÷ average sales cycle length in days

The result is the value of sales your pipeline produces per day.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Count qualified opportunities | Example: 40 open qualified deals in the supermarket segment | Only deals that passed the Qualified gate
SLIDE Step 2: Find win rate and deal size | Win rate by count 30% and average won deal 6,000,000 Kz | Use the last 12 months
SLIDE Step 3: Find cycle length | Average 60 days from created to won | Won deals only
SLIDE Step 4: Calculate | 40 × 0.30 × 6,000,000 ÷ 60 = 1,200,000 Kz per day | About 36,000,000 Kz per 30 days
SLIDE Step 5: Test the levers | Win rate 30% to 35% gives 1,400,000 Kz per day | Each lever can be modelled the same way
:::

::: diagram steps | The four levers of sales velocity
More qualified opportunities: prospect more and improve lead sources
Higher win rate: qualify harder and improve offers
Larger deal size: sell a wider range or more volume per account

:::

::: quiz
? A rep has 20 qualified opportunities, a 25% win rate, an average deal of 4,000,000 Kz and a 50-day cycle. What is her sales velocity?
* 400,000 Kz per day || Correct. 20 × 0.25 × 4,000,000 ÷ 50 = 400,000 Kz per day.
- 1,600,000 Kz per day || This forgets to apply the 25% win rate.
- 20,000,000 Kz per day || This forgets to divide by the 50-day cycle.
- 100,000 Kz per day || Check the calculation: 20 × 0.25 = 5 deals, 5 × 4,000,000 = 20,000,000, ÷ 50 = 400,000.

## Worked example: the supermarket channel

Helena runs the modern trade team for an FMCG distributor in Luanda. Her new business target for the quarter is 120,000,000 Kz and nothing is won yet. Her metrics from the last 12 months:

- Closed deals: 24 won, 56 lost, so the win rate by count is 24 ÷ 80 = 30%.
- Value won 96,000,000 Kz, value lost 384,000,000 Kz, so the win rate by value is 96 ÷ 480 = 20%.
- Average won deal = 96,000,000 ÷ 24 = 4,000,000 Kz.
- Average cycle = 75 days.
- Open qualified pipeline closing this quarter: 300,000,000 Kz in 45 deals.

Coverage is 300 ÷ 120 = 2.5x, but her value win rate of 20% means she needs about 5x, or 600,000,000 Kz. Velocity is 45 × 0.30 × 4,000,000 ÷ 75 = 720,000 Kz per day, or about 65,000,000 Kz in a 90-day quarter, well short of 120,000,000 Kz.

The gap between count and value win rates shows she loses large deals. Lost reasons point to credit terms for big wholesalers, so she agrees a clear credit policy with finance and asks the team for more qualified opportunities.

## Key terms

::: terms
Sales forecast: an estimate of the sales that will be achieved in a defined period
Weighted pipeline: the sum of each open deal's value multiplied by its stage probability
Forecast category: a judgement label for each deal, such as Commit, Best case or Pipeline
Commit: deals the owner is highly confident will close in the period, backed by evidence
Best case: deals that could close in the period if things go well
Base business: repeat sales from existing customers that do not pass through the new business pipeline
:::

## 1. Two ways to forecast the pipeline

The weighted pipeline method multiplies each deal's value by its stage probability (from lesson 1) and adds the results. It is objective and quick. It works well when you have many deals of similar size, such as new mini-market accounts. It works badly with a few large deals: a 50,000,000 Kz supermarket listing at 40% adds 20,000,000 Kz to the forecast, but the real result will be 0 or 50,000,000 Kz.

The forecast category method adds the judgement of the person closest to the deal. Many CRM systems use similar labels:

- Closed: already won in the period.
- Commit: the owner is highly confident it will close in the period, and there is evidence.
- Best case: it could close in the period if things go well.
- Pipeline: open, but unlikely to close in this period.
- Omitted: excluded from this period's forecast.

Commit must follow strict rules, otherwise it becomes a list of hopes. A deal is Commit only if the buyer has confirmed the timing, the decision-maker is engaged, the order or contract process is known (for example, the purchase order steps and supplier registration) and there are no open blockers such as credit approval.

::: diagram pyramid | Forecast categories by certainty
Closed: already won and booked
Commit: high confidence with evidence
Best case: could close if things go well
Pipeline: open but unlikely this period
:::

::: quiz
? A hotel buyer says he "will probably order" your new rice range this month, but the credit application is not yet approved. Which category fits?
- Closed || No order has been received.
- Commit || An open blocker, credit approval, means it fails the Commit rules.
* Best case || Correct. It could close this month if credit is approved, but it is not certain.
- Omitted || The deal is active and could still close in the period.
:::

## 2. Base business plus new business

For a distributor, most monthly sales come from existing customers who reorder: wholesalers, supermarkets and HoReCa accounts. These sales do not pass through the new business pipeline, so forecast them separately.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Base business | Average monthly sales from existing accounts over the last three months | Adjust for known seasonality and lost accounts
SLIDE Step 2: Known changes | Add confirmed price changes or promotions and remove accounts that stopped buying | Use facts, not hopes
SLIDE Step 3: New business | Closed + Commit + a share of Best case from the pipeline | Use your own history for the share
SLIDE Step 4: Total and range | Base + new business, shown as floor, most likely and ceiling | Explain the main risks
:::

Example for the month: base business averaged 400,000,000 Kz over the last three months. One wholesaler that bought 20,000,000 Kz a month has moved to a competitor, so the base forecast is 380,000,000 Kz.

## 3. Building the range

Present the new business forecast as a range rather than one number:

- Floor = Closed + Commit
- Most likely = Closed + Commit + Best case × your historical Best case conversion
- Ceiling = Closed + Commit + all Best case

Then compare it with the weighted pipeline. If the two differ a lot, find out why: often weak Commit deals or old deals sitting in late stages.

::: quiz
? Closed is 10,000,000 Kz, Commit 25,000,000 Kz and Best case 30,000,000 Kz. Historically, 40% of Best case closes. What is the most likely new business forecast?
- 35,000,000 Kz || This is the floor: Closed plus Commit only.
- 65,000,000 Kz || This is the ceiling: it assumes all Best case closes.
* 47,000,000 Kz || Correct. 10 + 25 + 30 × 0.40 = 47,000,000 Kz.
- 26,000,000 Kz || This applies 40% to all categories, including Closed and Commit.
:::

## Worked example: the monthly forecast for the B2B and key accounts channel

Nuno leads the B2B and key accounts channel for an FMCG distributor in Luanda. He must send the monthly forecast to the commercial director on the third working day.

Base business: existing accounts averaged 250,000,000 Kz a month over the last three months. A supermarket chain has confirmed a promotion that historically adds about 10,000,000 Kz, and one HoReCa group worth 6,000,000 Kz a month has stopped buying. Base forecast = 250 + 10 − 6 = 254,000,000 Kz.

New business, from his team's categories:

- Closed: 8,000,000 Kz.
- Commit: 22,000,000 Kz (three deals, each with the buyer's confirmed date and credit approved).
- Best case: 40,000,000 Kz. Historically 35% of Best case closes.

New business range: floor 30,000,000 Kz, most likely 30 + 14 = 44,000,000 Kz, ceiling 70,000,000 Kz.

The weighted pipeline for deals closing this month is 52,000,000 Kz, higher than the most likely figure. Nuno checks and finds two old deals sitting at Terms agreed with no activity for 60 days. He moves them back, and the weighted figure falls to 45,000,000 Kz, close to the category forecast.

His forecast: 284,000,000 Kz floor, 298,000,000 Kz most likely, 324,000,000 Kz ceiling. Main risk: the large Best case deal with a hotel group in the Ilha that depends on a tasting next week.

Teresa, a key account manager, puts a 60,000,000 Kz first order from a new wholesaler in Benguela into Commit for this month. The owner said "yes, this month" by phone, but no purchase order exists, the credit limit is not approved, and nobody has met the owner's partner, who co-signs large purchases.

## Key terms

::: terms
Pipeline review: a regular meeting that checks the health of the whole pipeline: size, coverage, movement and stuck deals
Deal review: a coaching discussion on how to win one specific opportunity
Forecast call: a short meeting to agree the expected result for the period
Pipeline waterfall: a reconciliation showing how the pipeline changed from the start to the end of a period
Slipped deal: a deal whose close date moved out of the current period
:::

## 1. Three meetings, three purposes

Many sales meetings try to do everything at once: reps tell long stories and nobody knows what will change. Separate three conversations:

- The forecast call answers one question: will we reach the number this period? It uses the categories from lesson 4 and takes 15 to 30 minutes.
- The pipeline review checks the health of the whole pipeline: is there enough, is it moving, and where is it stuck? It looks at the pipeline as a system.
- The deal review is coaching on one large or difficult deal: who decides, what do they need, and what is our plan?

A weekly pipeline review can include a short forecast check and one or two deal reviews, but each part needs its own time box.

## 2. The weekly review: preparation and agenda

Good reviews are prepared. Agree a data deadline, for example Thursday at 12:00, by which every rep updates stages, close dates and next steps. The coordinator then shares the hygiene exception report from lesson 2 and a one-page dashboard: target, closed to date, coverage, Commit and Best case, and new pipeline created this week. Nobody updates the CRM during the meeting.

::: diagram steps | A 60-minute weekly pipeline review
Numbers vs target (10 min): closed, forecast range and coverage
Pipeline movement (10 min): the waterfall since last week
Stuck and slipped deals (15 min): stale deals and repeated slippage
Top deals (15 min): the five largest deals closing this period
Actions (10 min): owners and dates recorded in the action log
:::

The pipeline waterfall shows how the pipeline changed during the week:

- Closing pipeline = opening pipeline + new deals added − deals won − deals lost ± value changes

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Opening pipeline | Monday 1: open qualified pipeline 180,000,000 Kz | Take a snapshot each week
SLIDE Step 2: Additions | New qualified deals this week: 25,000,000 Kz | Shows prospecting health
SLIDE Step 3: Exits | Won 15,000,000 Kz and lost 20,000,000 Kz | Check the lost reasons
SLIDE Step 4: Changes | Value reduced on two deals: minus 10,000,000 Kz | Ask why each value changed
SLIDE Step 5: Closing pipeline | 180 + 25 − 15 − 20 − 10 = 160,000,000 Kz | Pipeline shrank because exits were larger than new deals
:::

::: quiz
? Opening pipeline is 200,000,000 Kz. This week the team added 30,000,000 Kz, won 25,000,000 Kz, lost 15,000,000 Kz and there were no value changes. What is the closing pipeline?
- 230,000,000 Kz || This adds new deals but ignores the won and lost deals.
* 190,000,000 Kz || Correct. 200 + 30 − 25 − 15 = 190,000,000 Kz.
- 160,000,000 Kz || This subtracts the new deals instead of adding them.
- 175,000,000 Kz || This subtracts only the won deals. Lost deals also leave the pipeline.
:::

## 3. Asking evidence-based questions

The manager's questions decide the quality of the meeting. Questions such as "How is it going?" or "Do you feel good about this one?" invite stories. Evidence-based questions ask for facts:

- What did the customer do since last week?
- Have we met the person who signs, and what did they say?
- What has to happen for this deal to close by the date in the system?
- What is the next step, and on what date?
- What could make us lose this deal?

Keep the tone of coaching, not punishment. If reps are attacked for honest bad news, they will hide it, and the forecast gets worse. Record every agreed action in an action log with an owner and a date, and start the next review by checking last week's actions.

::: quiz
? During the review, a rep says a supermarket deal "is going very well, the buyer likes us". Which follow-up question is most useful?
- Are you confident about this one? || This asks for a feeling, not evidence.
- Why has it not closed yet? || This sounds like blame and may produce excuses.
* What has the buyer done since last week, and what is the next step and date? || Correct. It asks for buyer actions and a dated next step.
- Can you increase the value of the deal? || Value is not the issue. The question is whether the deal is progressing.
:::

Paula and her co-manager run the B2B and key accounts channel for a food distributor in Luanda, with seven reps covering wholesalers, supermarkets and HoReCa. Their Monday meeting took two hours: each rep talked through every deal, and actions were rarely written down.

They redesign it:

- Data deadline Friday at 15:00. The coordinator sends the exception report and dashboard by 17:00.
- The meeting takes 60 minutes with the agenda above.
- A shared action log records owner, action and date.

Two months later, the pipeline is back above 300,000,000 Kz.

::: case | The review that became storytelling
Carlos's weekly review lasts 90 minutes. Reps describe each customer visit in detail, and the meeting ends without decisions. The forecast missed by 25% last month, and two large deals slipped without warning.

- Make the meeting longer so every deal can be discussed || More time for stories will not produce better decisions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- set sales targets top-down and bottom-up and reconcile the two;
- write SMART targets and split an annual target by month;
- design sales territories that balance potential and workload;
- calculate forecast accuracy and bias and use them to improve.

## Key terms

::: terms
Top-down target: a target that starts from the company budget and is split across regions, teams and reps
Bottom-up target: a target built from the potential of each account and each rep's pipeline
Territory: the group of accounts or the area that one rep is responsible for
Account potential: the total amount a customer could buy in your categories, from any supplier
Forecast accuracy: how close the forecast was to the actual result
Forecast bias: a consistent tendency to forecast too high or too low
:::

## 1. Setting targets

Targets can be set in two ways. Top-down starts from the company budget, for example 4.8 billion Kz for the B2B channel next year, and splits it by region and rep. Bottom-up starts from each account: what it buys today, its potential, and the new business the pipeline can realistically produce.

Do both and compare. If bottom-up is 4.2 billion Kz, the 600,000,000 Kz gap needs a specific plan, such as new accounts in Huambo, not just a bigger number for each rep.

Write each target as SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, a framework first described by George T. Doran in 1981. "Grow sales" is not a target. "Win 300,000,000 Kz of new HoReCa business in Luanda between January and June" is.

Split annual targets by month using your own seasonality: each month's share of annual sales over the last two or three years, not one twelfth each.

::: quiz
? A rep's annual target is 600,000,000 Kz. Over the last three years, March averaged 7% of annual sales. What is a fair March target?
- 50,000,000 Kz || This is one twelfth of the year and ignores seasonality.
* 42,000,000 Kz || Correct. 600,000,000 × 7% = 42,000,000 Kz.
- 70,000,000 Kz || This is 7% of 1,000,000,000 Kz, not of 600,000,000 Kz.
- 35,000,000 Kz || Check the calculation: 7% of 600,000,000 is 42,000,000.
:::

## 2. Designing balanced territories

A territory should give each rep fair potential and a workload one person can cover. Otherwise one rep cannot visit all accounts while another lacks the potential to reach target.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: List and classify | List every account with current sales and potential, then classify by ABC analysis | A accounts are the few that give most of the value
SLIDE Step 2: Set visit frequency | Example: A weekly, B every two weeks, C monthly | Frequency follows value and needs
SLIDE Step 3: Calculate workload | Visits per month = accounts × visits per month for each class | Compare with each rep's capacity
SLIDE Step 4: Group and balance | Group accounts by geography, such as Viana, Cazenga or Talatona, then compare potential and workload per rep | Adjust boundaries until both are close
:::

Example of workload: a territory has 10 A accounts (4 visits a month), 30 B accounts (2 visits) and 60 C accounts (1 visit). Workload = 40 + 60 + 60 = 160 visits a month. A rep who can make 8 visits a day for 20 working days has a capacity of 160 visits, so the territory is fully loaded and leaves no time for prospecting. Move some C accounts to telesales or another rep.

::: diagram matrix | Territory balance check | Potential (low → high) | Workload (low → high)
Overloaded, low potential: move C accounts to telesales
Overloaded, high potential: split or add support
Under-loaded, low potential: merge or add accounts
Under-loaded, high potential: room to add prospecting
:::

## 3. Measuring and improving forecast accuracy

You cannot improve a forecast you do not measure. Track two numbers every month, per rep and for the team:

- Forecast accuracy = 1 − (absolute difference between actual and forecast ÷ actual)
- Forecast bias = (forecast − actual) ÷ actual

Example: forecast 110,000,000 Kz, actual 100,000,000 Kz. Accuracy = 1 − 10 ÷ 100 = 90%, bias = +10% (too high). One month tells you little. A rep at +15% for six months is optimistic. A rep always at −15% is probably sandbagging: holding deals back to beat the forecast.

Use the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle to improve:

::: diagram cycle | Improving forecast accuracy with PDCA
Plan: agree stage definitions, Commit rules and stage probabilities
Do: forecast every month with the same method
Check: measure accuracy and bias per rep, and compare stage probabilities with real win rates
Act: recalibrate probabilities, coach biased reps and fix unclear rules
:::

Each quarter, recalculate stage probabilities from the latest closed deals, as in lesson 1. Review lost deals: if many are lost at Terms agreed for the same reason, fix that reason.

::: quiz
? A rep forecast 80,000,000 Kz and the actual result was 100,000,000 Kz. What are her accuracy and bias?
- Accuracy 80%, bias +20% || The sign is wrong: she forecast too low, so bias is negative.
- Accuracy 75%, bias −25% || These divide by the forecast instead of the actual.
- Accuracy 120%, bias −20% || Accuracy cannot be above 100% with this formula.
* Accuracy 80%, bias −20% || Correct. 1 − 20 ÷ 100 = 80%, and (80 − 100) ÷ 100 = −20%.
:::

## Worked example: rebalancing territories in Luanda

A food distributor has four B2B reps in Luanda. Last year the Talatona rep reached 140% of target and the Cacuaco rep 70%. The commercial director suspects the territories.

She lists all 420 accounts, classifies them by ABC analysis and calculates workload and potential for each territory. Talatona has 30% of the team's potential but only 20% of the workload. Cacuaco has 35% of the workload but only 15% of the potential.

She moves 25 B accounts from Cacuaco to telesales and gives the Cacuaco rep 8 A accounts in Viana. Both territories now hold 22% to 28% of potential with similar workload.

She also starts tracking forecast accuracy. After three months, the team's accuracy is 84%, with one rep consistently at +20% bias. Coaching on the Commit rules brings that rep's bias down to +6%.

::: case | The rep who always beats the forecast
Fátima, a sales rep for a distributor in Benguela, has beaten her forecast by 18% to 25% every month for six months. Her manager likes the results, but the warehouse keeps running short of stock at the end of each month because purchasing plans from the forecast.
? What should the manager do?

* Show her the bias data, review her Best case deals together and agree to forecast deals she expects to close || Correct. A consistent negative bias suggests sandbagging, and better forecasts help purchasing and stock.
- Raise her target by 25% as a penalty || This punishes good results and encourages more hiding.
- Ignore her forecast and let purchasing guess || This removes the information purchasing needs.
:::
