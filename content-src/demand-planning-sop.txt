## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain the difference between demand, sales and orders;
- clean a sales history for stock-outs, outliers and promotions;
- apply ABC-XYZ segmentation to decide where planning effort goes;
- choose the right level of detail and horizon for a forecast.

## Key terms

::: terms
Demand plan: the agreed forecast of what customers will buy, by product and period, used to drive supply decisions
Censored demand: recorded sales that are lower than real demand because stock was not available
Baseline demand: the normal level of demand without promotions, tenders or one-off events
Coefficient of variation (CV): standard deviation of demand divided by average demand
Planning horizon: how far into the future the forecast looks
:::

## 1. What exactly are we forecasting?

An importer of rice, sugar and flour into Luanda commits money months before the goods sell, because an order placed today may arrive three or four months later. The demand plan drives that decision, so it must describe what customers will want, not what the warehouse happened to ship. Four numbers are often confused:

- Customer orders: close to true demand, but inflated when customers over-order for fear of shortages.
- Invoiced sales: what you delivered, lower than demand whenever you were out of stock.
- Purchase orders on suppliers: your own supply decisions, never demand history.
- True demand: what customers would have bought with full availability. This is what you forecast.

Most distributors start from invoiced sales, the most reliable data in the ERP, and correct them, adding unfulfilled order lines or lost-sale notes where they exist.

::: quiz
? Your system shows 600 t of sugar invoiced in March, but the warehouse had no stock for the last 10 days of the month. Which statement is correct?
- March demand was 600 t || Invoices only show what you could deliver, not what customers wanted.
* March demand was probably higher than 600 t || Stock was missing for a third of the month, so recorded sales are censored.
- March should be removed from the history || Removing a month loses information. Adjust it instead.
:::

## 2. Cleaning the history step by step

A clean history matters more than a clever method. Work through four steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Correct stock-outs | Find periods without stock; Replace with average daily sales x days in the period | Note every adjustment
SLIDE Step 2: Investigate outliers | Flag unusual values; Ask sales and finance why | Never delete without a reason
SLIDE Step 3: Separate events | Split promotions and tenders from baseline | Forecast them separately
SLIDE Step 4: Align the calendar | Adjust for selling days and holidays | Compare like with like
:::

Stock-out correction: a 25 kg flour bag sold 1,800 bags in 20 days with stock, then had 10 days without stock in a 30-day month. Average daily sales = 1,800 / 20 = 90 bags, so estimated demand = 90 x 30 = 2,700 bags. Using 1,800 would make the next forecast too low and cause the next stock-out.

Outliers: flag any month more than about two standard deviations from the average. A flag is a question, not an answer. A spike may be a typing error, a one-off tender, or a real increase because a competitor left the market. Only the first two should be corrected.

Promotions: if a promotion sold an extra 300 cases, record 300 as uplift and keep the baseline. Plan the uplift again only if the promotion is repeated.

## 3. ABC-XYZ segmentation: where to spend effort

A distributor with 400 SKUs cannot review every forecast every month. ABC-XYZ segmentation combines two views:

- ABC by value: A items are the few that make about 80% of sales value, B the next 15%, C the long tail. These cut-offs are conventions.
- XYZ by variability, using CV: X stable, Y variable, Z erratic. Cut-offs such as CV below 0.5 for X and above 1.0 for Z are common.

Example: rice 5 kg sells an average of 4,000 bags a month with a standard deviation of 600, so CV = 0.15, an X item. A specialty pulse averaging 50 cases with a standard deviation of 70 has CV = 1.4, a Z item.

::: diagram matrix | ABC-XYZ: planning approach | Variability (X stable → Z erratic) | Value (C low → A high)
AX: high value, stable – statistical forecast, monthly review
AZ: high value, erratic – planner and sales review, more safety stock
CX: low value, stable – automate fully
CZ: low value, erratic – order on demand or review the range
:::

## 4. Level of detail and horizon

Forecasts are more accurate when aggregated, because SKU errors partly cancel out. Use product family and month for the S&OP plan, cash budget and container bookings, SKU and month for purchase orders, and SKU and province only where stock sits in regional depots.

The horizon must be longer than the total lead time. If rice takes four months from order to available stock, a three-month forecast cannot support today's order. A practical choice is 12 to 18 months for families and 4 to 6 months for SKUs.

::: quiz
? Total lead time for imported sugar is four months. What is the minimum forecast horizon for SKU purchase orders?
- One month || A one-month view cannot cover goods that arrive in four months.
- Three months || This still ends before the order arrives.
* More than four months || The forecast must cover the lead time plus the review period.
:::

## Worked example: cleaning rice history in Luanda

A food importer in Luanda sells 25 kg rice bags in five provinces. Its new planner, Teresa, cleans the history:

- In May the warehouse was out of stock for 8 of 31 days. Invoiced sales were 9,200 bags in 23 days. Teresa estimates 9,200 / 23 x 31 = 12,400 bags of demand.
- August shows 31,000 bags against a usual 12,000 to 13,000. Sales confirm a one-off food-basket tender of 18,000 bags. Teresa records 18,000 as an event and keeps 13,000 as baseline.
- October shows 1,250 bags. Finance confirms a keying error: the real figure is 12,500. She corrects it.

Rice 25 kg is an AX item, so it is forecast statistically and reviewed monthly.

::: case | A sugar spike before the festive season
In November, 1 kg sugar sales at a Luanda distributor jumped from about 40,000 to 65,000 packs because one wholesaler bought heavily after hearing that a competitor's ship was delayed. Planner Joaquim is preparing the history.
? What should Joaquim do with the November figure?
- Use 65,000 as normal demand || This inflates the baseline with a one-off buying wave.
* Keep about 40,000 as baseline and record 25,000 as an event || Forward buying is usually followed by a dip, so separating it protects both months.
- Delete November from the history || Deleting loses the real baseline and the evidence of the event.
- Raise the December forecast by 25,000 || The wholesaler has already bought, so December is more likely to fall.
:::

- Deleting outliers without finding the cause.
- Leaving promotions and tenders inside the baseline.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- recognise the four common demand patterns: level, trend, seasonal and intermittent;
- calculate naive, weighted moving average and exponential smoothing forecasts;
- build and apply a seasonal index;
- choose a suitable method for each type of item.

## Key terms

::: terms
Time series: demand recorded at regular intervals, such as monthly sales
Naive forecast: a forecast equal to the last actual value, used as a benchmark
Smoothing constant (alpha): the weight between 0 and 1 that exponential smoothing gives to the latest actual
Seasonal index: the ratio of a period's typical demand to the demand of an average period
Intermittent demand: demand with many periods of zero sales
:::

## 1. Know the pattern, then choose the method

Plot 24 to 36 months of cleaned history before choosing a method. Most series show one or more of four patterns:

::: diagram steps | Choosing a forecasting method
Level: stable around an average – moving average or simple exponential smoothing
Trend: steady rise or fall – Holt's method (exponential smoothing with trend)
Seasonal: repeating peaks such as December – seasonal indices or Holt-Winters
Intermittent: many zero months – Croston's method or order on demand
:::

Always start with a benchmark. The naive forecast says next month equals last month, and the seasonal naive says next December equals last December. Any method must beat them on accuracy (lesson 3). If it cannot, check the data first.

## 2. Weighted moving average and exponential smoothing

A simple moving average treats each month the same. A weighted moving average gives more weight to recent months, and the weights must add up to 1.

Example: rice sales were 900 t in July, 1,000 t in August and 1,200 t in September. With weights 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5 (most recent highest), the October forecast = 0.2 x 900 + 0.3 x 1,000 + 0.5 x 1,200 = 180 + 300 + 600 = 1,080 t. A simple 3-month average gives 1,033 t, slower to follow the rise.

Exponential smoothing, developed in the 1950s and built into most planning software, gives every past month a weight that shrinks with age. You only need the last forecast and the latest actual:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Start the model | Use an average of the first months as the first forecast | One starting value is enough
SLIDE Step 2: Choose alpha | 0.1 to 0.3 for stable items; Higher values react faster but follow noise | Test alpha on past data
SLIDE Step 3: Update each month | New forecast = alpha x actual + (1 - alpha) x old forecast | Only two numbers needed
SLIDE Step 4: Check the errors | Errors always on one side mean a trend | Then add a trend term with Holt's method
:::

Calculation: the 50 kg sugar forecast for September was 1,000 bags, actual demand was 1,200 and alpha is 0.3. October forecast = 0.3 x 1,200 + 0.7 x 1,000 = 360 + 700 = 1,060 bags. If October actual is 1,100, November = 0.3 x 1,100 + 0.7 x 1,060 = 330 + 742 = 1,072 bags.

Simple smoothing lags behind a trend. Holt's method adds a second smoothed value for the trend: the forecast h months ahead = level + h x trend. With a level of 1,072 bags and a trend of +15 bags a month, the forecast three months ahead is 1,072 + 3 x 15 = 1,117 bags. The Holt-Winters method adds a third component for seasonality. Excel's FORECAST.ETS function uses this family of methods.

::: quiz
? Last month's forecast was 500 cartons, actual demand was 600 and alpha is 0.2. What is the new forecast?
- 600 cartons || That is the naive forecast.
* 520 cartons || 0.2 x 600 + 0.8 x 500 = 120 + 400 = 520.
- 580 cartons || This would need an alpha of 0.8.
:::

## 3. Seasonality: indices for festive peaks

Many food categories peak before the December festive season. The classical ratio-to-average method handles this in five steps:

- Step 1: average each calendar month's sales over at least two years.
- Step 2: divide by the overall monthly average to get the index. The 12 indices add up to about 12.
- Step 3: deseasonalise the history by dividing each month's sales by its index.
- Step 4: forecast the deseasonalised series with a moving average or smoothing.
- Step 5: reseasonalise by multiplying the forecast by the index of the target month.

Example: over two years, sugar sold an average of 2,000 t a month and December averaged 2,600 t. December index = 2,600 / 2,000 = 1.30. February averaged 1,700 t, an index of 0.85. If the deseasonalised forecast is 2,100 t a month, the December forecast = 2,100 x 1.30 = 2,730 t and February = 2,100 x 0.85 = 1,785 t.

::: quiz
? A product's November index is 1.15 and the deseasonalised forecast is 4,000 cases. What is the November forecast?
- 3,478 cases || This divides by the index instead of multiplying.
- 4,000 cases || This ignores the seasonal index.
* 4,600 cases || 4,000 x 1.15 = 4,600.
:::

## 4. Intermittent items and judgement

Some items sell in only a few months, such as an ingredient bought by two bakeries. Averages and smoothing give a small, misleading number every month. Croston's method (Croston, 1972) forecasts two things separately: the size of orders when they occur and the interval between them. For low-value intermittent items, consider ordering on demand or a simple min-max rule.

Statistical methods only see the past. Add judgement for known future events, such as a lost customer or a new listing, but record each adjustment and its reason. Lesson 3 shows how to test whether adjustments help.

## Worked example: flour forecast for a Luanda importer

A distributor sells 50 kg flour bags to bakeries. Planner Domingos tests three methods on 12 months of cleaned history. Average errors are 14% for naive, 11% for a 3-month moving average and 8% for exponential smoothing (alpha 0.2) with seasonal indices. He selects the last.

The December index is 1.25 and the deseasonalised forecast is 16,000 bags, so December = 20,000 bags. A new bakery chain opening in December will buy about 1,500 bags, recorded as a judgement adjustment: final forecast 21,500 bags.

::: case | Choosing a method for dried beans
Planner Ana in Benguela plans dried beans. Sales have risen by about 3% a month for a year as a new wholesaler grows, with no clear seasonal pattern. Her 6-month moving average forecast has been below actual sales for nine months in a row.
? What should Ana change?
- Use a 12-month moving average to make the forecast smoother || A longer average lags even further behind a trend.
* Switch to Holt's method, which adds a trend component || The pattern is a trend, and Holt's method projects it forward.
- Use seasonal indices || There is no seasonal pattern to model.
- Keep the method and add a fixed 10% each month || An untested manual uplift can simply replace one bias with another.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- calculate MAD, MAPE and WAPE for an item or a product family;
- measure bias and use a tracking signal to detect a biased forecast;
- apply forecast value added (FVA) analysis to test each step of the forecasting process;
- choose the correct lag at which to measure accuracy for imported products.

## Key terms

::: terms
Forecast error: forecast minus actual for one period, so a positive error means an over-forecast
MAD (mean absolute deviation): the average size of the errors in units, ignoring the sign
WAPE (weighted absolute percentage error): total absolute error divided by total actual demand
Bias: the tendency of a forecast to be consistently too high or too low
Tracking signal: cumulative error divided by MAD, used to detect bias
Forecast lag: the number of periods between when a forecast was made and the period it forecasts
:::

## 1. Measuring the size of the error

Measure error in units and in percentages. Take one flour item over four months, with forecasts of 500, 520, 480 and 510 t and actuals of 450, 560, 400 and 470 t:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Errors | Forecast - actual = +50, -40, +80, +40 | Keep the sign for bias
SLIDE Step 2: MAD | Absolute errors 50, 40, 80, 40 = 210 in total; MAD = 210 / 4 = 52.5 t | Average error in units
SLIDE Step 3: MAPE | Percentage errors 11.1%, 7.1%, 20.0%, 8.5%; MAPE = 46.7 / 4 = 11.7% | Simple average of percentages
SLIDE Step 4: WAPE | Total actual = 1,880 t; WAPE = 210 / 1,880 = 11.2% | Big months count more
:::

MAPE treats every period and every item equally. Across a portfolio this misleads: a small spice item that sold 10 cases against a forecast of 20 has a 100% error and can dominate the average, while a 1% error on 5,000 t of rice hardly counts. WAPE weights errors by volume, or by value if you calculate in kwanzas, so it reflects business impact. Use WAPE for family and company reports. If management prefers a positive number, report accuracy = 100% - WAPE.

::: quiz
? Rice was forecast at 1,000 t and sold 900 t. A spice was forecast at 20 cases and sold 10. Which measure gives a fair picture of the business impact?
- A simple MAPE of the two items || The spice item's 100% error dominates the average.
* WAPE calculated in kwanzas || Errors are weighted by value, so rice counts much more.
- The naive forecast || That is a benchmark method, not an error measure.
:::

## 2. Bias and the tracking signal

Accuracy measures size. Bias measures direction. In the example, the sum of errors is +50 - 40 + 80 + 40 = +130 t, and bias % = 130 / 1,880 = 6.9%. Over four months the forecast was too high, which builds excess stock and ties up cash and foreign currency.

The tracking signal turns this into an alarm: tracking signal = cumulative error / MAD = 130 / 52.5 = 2.5. A common rule of thumb is to review the forecast when the tracking signal moves outside about plus or minus 4. A value near zero means errors are balancing out. A steady movement towards the limit means the method or the inputs are biased, for example a sales team that forecasts high to make sure stock is available.

Bias is often more damaging than random error. Random errors partly cancel out and are covered by safety stock. Bias accumulates: a 7% over-forecast on a container-based import programme becomes several containers of excess stock every quarter.

::: quiz
? With error = forecast - actual, a tracking signal has moved from -1 to -5 over six months. What does it show?
- The forecast is accurate || A value beyond -4 signals a problem.
- The forecast is consistently too high || A negative signal means actuals are above forecasts.
* The forecast is consistently too low || Negative cumulative errors mean under-forecasting, so stock-outs are likely.
:::

## 3. Forecast value added (FVA)

Forecast value added analysis, popularised by Michael Gilliland, asks a simple question: does each step in the forecasting process make the forecast better or worse? Measure the error of each step against the same actuals.

::: diagram steps | FVA: measure every step
Naive forecast: the free benchmark
Statistical forecast: should beat naive
Sales and marketing adjustment: should beat statistical
Final consensus forecast: approved in S&OP, should be the best
:::

Example for a flour family over six months: naive WAPE 18%, statistical WAPE 12%, after sales adjustments 14%. The statistical model added 6 points of value. The sales adjustments destroyed 2 points. The answer is not to stop listening to sales, but to accept only adjustments based on specific information, such as a confirmed customer order or a lost listing, and to track FVA by person or team.

## 4. Measuring at the right lag

For imported goods, the forecast that matters is the one used when the order was placed. If rice has a three-month lead time, the June forecast that drove the purchase order was made in March: the lag-3 forecast. Measuring the forecast made at the start of June (lag 0) makes accuracy look better than it was, because most of the month is already known. Save a snapshot of the forecast every month so you can measure at the lead-time lag.

## Worked example: an accuracy report at a Luanda distributor

A distributor of rice, sugar and flour prepares its first accuracy report for the S&OP meeting, at lag 3 and family level:

- Rice: WAPE 9%, bias +2%, tracking signal +1.1. Healthy.
- Sugar: WAPE 15%, bias -11%, tracking signal -4.6. Repeated under-forecasting before the December peak caused stock-outs.
- Flour: final WAPE 13%, statistical WAPE 10%. Manual adjustments made it worse.

Actions: the planner reviews the sugar seasonal indices for November and December with sales, and every flour adjustment now needs a written reason.

::: case | Sales overrides on pulses
Planner Hélder in Luanda sees that the sales director has raised the dried beans forecast by 20% every month for six months to be safe. The statistical WAPE is 11%, the final WAPE is 19% and bias is +17%. The warehouse holds four months of beans.
? What should Hélder bring to the next demand review?
- A request to remove sales from forecasting || Sales input is valuable when it is based on specific information.
* An FVA report showing the override adds error and bias, plus a rule that adjustments need a reason || Data shows the cost of the habit and keeps sales involved under clear rules.
- A higher safety stock to cover the errors || The problem is over-forecasting, so more stock makes it worse.
- A proposal for new forecasting software || The statistical forecast is already better. The process is the issue.
:::

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- break down the end-to-end lead time for an imported product;

## Key terms

::: terms
End-to-end lead time: the time from deciding to order until stock is available to sell
Review period: the time between two ordering decisions for the same item
Pipeline stock: stock already ordered but not yet received, at origin, on the water or at the port
Net requirement: the quantity still needed after allowing for stock on hand and stock on order
Time fence: a point in the planning horizon that sets which changes are allowed
:::

## 1. End-to-end lead time

The lead time an importer must plan with is much longer than the sailing time. For a container of rice from Asia to Luanda it includes five stages:

::: diagram steps | End-to-end lead time for imported rice
Order and payment set-up: purchase approval, foreign currency and letter of credit or transfer
Supplier production and loading: milling, bagging, booking and loading at origin
Sea freight: transit time, including any transhipment
Port and clearance: discharge, document checks, customs and inspection
Delivery and receipt: transport to the warehouse and quality check
:::

Record the actual days of each stage for your last 10 to 20 shipments, then calculate the average and standard deviation. Foreign currency access and port clearance can vary as much as sea freight, or more, and that variability drives safety stock.

## 2. Safety stock with demand and lead-time variability

The basic safety stock formula covers demand variability only. When both demand and lead time vary, and are independent, a widely used formula is:

- SS = Z x square root of (LT x σd² + d² x σLT²)

Here Z is the service factor (1.65 for about a 95% cycle service level), LT is average lead time, d is average demand per period, σd is the standard deviation of demand per period and σLT is the standard deviation of lead time. Use the same time unit for all of them.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Collect inputs | Average demand d = 800 t a month; Demand standard deviation = 150 t; Lead time 3 months, standard deviation 0.5 month | Same time unit for all
SLIDE Step 2: Demand part | LT x σd² = 3 x 22,500 = 67,500 | Risk from demand
SLIDE Step 3: Lead-time part | d² x σLT² = 640,000 x 0.25 = 160,000 | Risk from delays
SLIDE Step 4: Combine | Square root of 227,500 = 477 t; SS = 1.65 x 477 = 787 t | About one month of sales
:::

The lead-time part is more than twice the demand part. Reducing the variability of payment set-up and port clearance would cut safety stock more than a better forecast would. This is why planners, procurement and logistics must work together.

::: quiz
? Demand is very stable, but port clearance time varies widely. Which action reduces safety stock most?
- Improve the forecasting method || Demand variability is already small.
* Reduce variability in clearance time || Lead-time variability is the larger part of the formula here.
- Lengthen the review period || A longer review period increases the stock needed.
:::

## 3. Net requirement and container rounding

With a monthly review, each order must cover demand until the following order can arrive: lead time plus review period.

- Net requirement = forecast over (lead time + review period) + safety stock - stock on hand - stock on order.

Continuing the example: forecast 800 t a month, lead time 3 months and review 1 month, so cover = 4 x 800 = 3,200 t. Stock on hand is 1,100 t and stock on order is 1,600 t.

Net requirement = 3,200 + 787 - 1,100 - 1,600 = 1,287 t.

Round to feasible quantities. If a 20-foot container carries 25 t of bagged rice in this trade, 1,287 / 25 = 51.5, so order 52 containers = 1,300 t. Then check constraints: supplier minimums, warehouse space when the goods arrive, and the cash and foreign currency available. If a constraint applies, the S&OP process decides the trade-off (lesson 5).

For seasonal products, add up the forecast for each month of the cover period instead of using one average. An order placed in September for arrival in December must include the December index.

::: quiz
? Cover forecast 2,000 t, safety stock 400 t, stock on hand 600 t, stock on order 900 t. What is the net requirement?
- 500 t || This leaves out the safety stock.
* 900 t || 2,000 + 400 - 600 - 900 = 900 t.
- 1,800 t || This forgets the stock already on order.
:::

## 4. Time fences: what can still change

The forecast changes every month, but an importer cannot change goods already loaded. Time fences set the rules:

- Frozen zone (for example 0 to 3 months): goods ordered, paid or on the water. No changes except emergencies approved by management.
- Slushy zone (for example 3 to 5 months): orders placed or being negotiated. Changes need supply and finance approval.
- Liquid zone (beyond 5 months): forecasts and planned orders can change freely.

Set the fences from your real lead times, not from habit.

## Worked example: sugar order for the festive season

A Luanda importer plans 50 kg sugar with a three-month lead time and monthly review. In August, planner Isabel prepares the order that will arrive in November. Her cover period is August to November, with seasonal forecasts of 2,000, 2,000, 2,200 and 2,400 t: a total of 8,600 t. Safety stock is 900 t, stock on hand 1,900 t, and 4,000 t are on order, arriving in September and October.

Net requirement = 8,600 + 900 - 1,900 - 4,000 = 3,600 t, or 144 containers of 25 t.

The bank confirms foreign currency for only 3,000 t. Isabel prepares options for S&OP: 3,000 t now and 600 t next month from a faster supplier, or protecting key accounts if the gap remains.

::: case | Port delay on a flour shipment
A distributor in Lobito expects 1,000 t of wheat flour next week, but the shipping line reports a three-week delay at transhipment. Stock on hand covers two weeks of sales. The following shipment is already in the frozen zone.
? What should planner Mateus do first?
- Cancel the following shipment to save cash || It is frozen, and stock is about to run short, not too high.
* Calculate the shortage week by week and agree allocation and local purchase options with sales || Quantifying the gap lets the business decide on allocation or bridging supply.
- Raise all future forecasts by three weeks of sales || The delay is a supply problem, not a change in demand.
- Wait for the ship and tell customers when stock runs out || Customers and sales need a warning before the stock-out.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain the purpose of sales and operations planning (S&OP) and its outputs;
- run the five steps of a monthly S&OP cycle with clear inputs and outputs;
- prepare decision options for the executive meeting;
- use scenarios to plan for port delays, foreign currency limits and supply risks.

## Key terms

::: terms
S&OP: a monthly decision process that balances demand and supply and agrees one plan across sales, supply chain and finance
Unconstrained forecast: what customers would buy if supply were unlimited
Constrained plan: the plan after supply, capacity and cash limits are applied
Product family: a group of items planned together, such as rice or sugar
Scenario: an alternative plan prepared for a possible event, such as a port delay
Integrated business planning (IBP): a broader form of S&OP that links fully to financial plans and strategy
:::

## 1. What S&OP is and is not

S&OP is a monthly process in which sales, marketing, supply chain and finance agree one plan for the next 12 to 24 months, at product family level, in units and in money. It is a decision process: its output is a set of approved decisions, not a pile of reports. For an importer, those decisions include how many containers or vessel lots to buy, which customers get priority in a shortage, how much foreign currency to request and whether to run a promotion.

S&OP is not the weekly purchasing meeting. Detailed SKU and weekly decisions belong to the planners. S&OP sets the frame they work within.

## 2. The five-step monthly cycle

Most companies follow a five-step cycle across the month:

::: diagram cycle | The monthly S&OP cycle
Data gathering (week 1): close the month, update actuals, stock, open orders and accuracy
Demand review (week 2): agree the unconstrained consensus forecast and its assumptions
Supply review (weeks 2 to 3): test the forecast against suppliers, port, warehouse, transport and cash
Pre-S&OP (week 3): resolve gaps and prepare options with their financial impact
Executive S&OP (week 4): decide, approve one plan and record decisions
:::

Demand review. Led by the demand planner with sales and marketing. Inputs are the statistical forecast, sales intelligence, promotions, tenders and the accuracy report. The output is a consensus forecast by family with clear assumptions, for example a December uplift of 30% or a new wholesaler in Huambo from March, plus upside and downside risks.

Supply review. Led by supply planning with procurement, logistics and the warehouse. Inputs are the consensus forecast, open orders, supplier capacity, shipping schedules, the port situation, warehouse space and available foreign currency. The output is a constrained supply plan and a list of gaps, for example sugar short by 1,200 t in December.

Pre-S&OP. A working meeting of the planners with the sales, supply and finance managers. They resolve what they can and prepare two or three options for each remaining gap.

Executive S&OP. The general manager chairs a one-hour meeting: performance review, decisions on gaps, approval of the plan and key risks. Every decision is recorded with an owner and a date.

::: quiz
? In which step is the unconstrained forecast tested against port and warehouse capacity?
- Demand review || Demand review agrees what customers want, without supply limits.
* Supply review || Supply review checks the forecast against capacity and flags gaps.
- Executive S&OP || Executives decide on gaps already analysed. They do not do the analysis.
:::

## 3. Preparing options and scenarios

Executives should receive choices, not raw data. For each gap, the pre-S&OP team uses the same four steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Describe the gap | Family, month, units and value; Cause, such as a delayed vessel or a currency limit | One sentence per gap
SLIDE Step 2: List options | Local purchase, allocation to key accounts, delaying a promotion, another pack size | Two or three real options
SLIDE Step 3: Show the impact | Service, margin, cash and foreign currency for each option | The same measures for every option
SLIDE Step 4: Recommend | The team's preferred option and why | The team recommends, executives decide
:::

Scenarios prepare the business for known risks. A practical approach is to keep the base plan plus two scenarios for the top risks, for example port congestion adding four weeks, or foreign currency available for only 70% of planned imports. Each scenario shows the effect on stock, service and cash, and the trigger that activates it, so the response is fast when the event happens.

::: quiz
? What should the pre-S&OP send to the executive meeting about a sugar shortage?
- The full SKU-level forecast file || Too much detail hides the decision.
- A request for executives to analyse the shortage || Analysis belongs to the team before the meeting.
* Two or three options with their service, cost and cash impact, plus a recommendation || Executives need clear choices and consequences to decide quickly.
:::

## Worked example: December planning at a Luanda importer

In September, the demand review of a rice, sugar and flour importer agrees a December consensus forecast of 3,000 t of sugar, including a 30% festive uplift. The supply review shows that only 2,200 t can arrive by early December, because one supplier has a loading delay. The gap is 800 t.

The pre-S&OP prepares three options:

- A: buy 500 t from a local repacker at a higher cost, reducing margin but protecting supply.
- B: allocate the available sugar, giving key accounts 100% and wholesalers 75% of their forecast.
- C: buy 300 t locally and apply a lighter allocation.

The team recommends C. The executive meeting approves it, records the decision with owners and dates, and asks finance to update the cash forecast.

::: case | Disagreement at the demand review
At a Luanda distributor, the sales director wants a flour forecast of 12,000 bags for March because of a planned campaign. The statistical forecast is 9,000 bags, and last year's similar campaign added about 1,500 bags. The supply planner says the warehouse can hold only 11,000 bags in March.
? What is the best outcome of the demand review?
- Agree 11,000 bags, the warehouse limit || Capacity limits belong in the supply review, not in the demand forecast.
* Agree 10,500 bags with the campaign assumption recorded, and pass the space issue to the supply review || The forecast follows the evidence, and the constraint is handled in the right step.
- Accept 12,000 bags because sales knows customers best || The record shows a smaller uplift, and unsupported overrides add bias.
- Postpone the decision to the executive meeting || Executives decide on gaps. The demand review must deliver a forecast.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- choose a balanced set of KPIs for demand, supply, customer service and finance;
- calculate fill rate, OTIF, days of cover and plan adherence;

## Key terms

::: terms
Fill rate: the share of ordered units or cases that are shipped from stock
OTIF (on time in full): the share of orders delivered on the agreed date with the full quantity
Days of cover: how many days current stock will last at the expected rate of demand
Plan adherence: how closely actual receipts match the approved supply plan
RACI: a chart that shows who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed for each task
Decision log: a record of every S&OP decision with its owner, date and follow-up
:::

## 1. A balanced set of KPIs

S&OP manages trade-offs, so its KPIs must show both sides. Holding more stock improves fill rate but hurts cash. A balanced set has four groups, with one or two measures each:

- Demand: forecast accuracy (WAPE) and bias at the lead-time lag, by family.
- Supply: plan adherence (received against planned quantity) and supplier on-time loading.
- Customer: fill rate and OTIF.
- Finance: days of cover, value of ageing or near-expiry stock, and gross margin against plan.

Each KPI needs a written definition, a data source, an owner and a target. Without a definition, sales and supply calculate the same KPI differently and argue about the number instead of the problem.

## 2. Calculating key KPIs

Four calculations cover most S&OP dashboards:

::: slides
SLIDE Fill rate | Customers ordered 10,000 cases and 9,400 were shipped from stock; Fill rate = 9,400 / 10,000 = 94% | Measures availability
SLIDE OTIF | 500 orders delivered, 430 on time and in full; OTIF = 430 / 500 = 86% | Late or short counts as a fail
SLIDE Days of cover | Stock 2,400 t, forecast 800 t a month = 26.7 t a day; Cover = 2,400 / 26.7 = 90 days | Use forward demand, not past sales
SLIDE Plan adherence | Planned receipts 3,000 t, actual receipts 2,550 t; Adherence = 2,550 / 3,000 = 85% | Shows supply reliability
:::

OTIF is usually lower than fill rate because one late or short line fails the whole order. Read the KPIs together: 90 days of cover with a 94% fill rate suggests the wrong mix of stock, too much of some items and too little of others. The response is a mix review by SKU, not more total stock.

::: quiz
? A distributor shipped 18,000 of the 20,000 cases ordered. What is the fill rate?
- 11% || This divides the shortfall by the quantity shipped.
- 10% || This is the shortfall rate, not the fill rate.
* 90% || 18,000 / 20,000 = 90%.
:::

## 3. Governance: making S&OP a habit

S&OP can fade after a few months when executives skip meetings, decisions are not followed up and the meetings drown in detail. Clear governance prevents this:

- Executive sponsor: the general manager or commercial director, who chairs the executive meeting and insists on one set of numbers.
- Process owner: often the head of planning, who runs the calendar, templates and decision log.
- RACI: one person accountable for each step. For the forecast, the demand planner is responsible, the sales director accountable, finance consulted and the warehouse informed.
- Fixed calendar: dates set for the whole year, and meetings shortened rather than cancelled.
- Decision log: reviewed at the start of every executive meeting.

::: diagram pyramid | S&OP maturity stages
Advanced: scenarios, profit optimisation and links to strategy
Integrated: plan linked to budget, cash and foreign currency
Standard: monthly cycle, one consensus forecast, basic KPIs
Reactive: firefighting and different numbers in each department
:::

Assess your stage honestly and move up one stage at a time. A reactive company should first set up the monthly calendar and one forecast, not buy advanced planning software.

::: quiz
? A company holds monthly S&OP meetings with one forecast, but the plan is not linked to the budget or cash plan. What is the best next step?
- Buy advanced scenario-planning software || Software does not fix the missing link with finance.
* Bring finance into the cycle and show the plan in kwanzas and foreign currency || This moves the company from the standard to the integrated stage.
- Stop the meetings until the data is perfect || Stopping destroys the habit, and data improves through use.
:::

## Worked example: a KPI dashboard at a Luanda importer

An importer of rice, sugar and flour has run S&OP for six months. Its one-page dashboard for October shows:

- Rice: WAPE 9%, bias +2%, fill rate 97%, 55 days of cover.
- Sugar: WAPE 16%, bias -10%, fill rate 85%, 25 days of cover.
- Flour: WAPE 12%, bias +8%, fill rate 98%, 120 days of cover and 40 t close to expiry.

The executive sponsor focuses on two decisions: sugar's negative bias before the festive peak, and excess flour. The meeting raises sugar's December plan and confirms extra shipping space, stops flour purchases for one cycle and approves a promotion on near-expiry stock. Both decisions go into the log with owners and dates.
