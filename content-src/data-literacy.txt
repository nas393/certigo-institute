## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain what data literacy is and why it matters for everyday decisions;
- classify data by type: qualitative or quantitative, categorical or numerical, time series or cross-sectional;
- use the DIKW pyramid to describe how data becomes a decision;
- check a report against six common data quality dimensions.

## Key terms

::: terms
Data literacy: the ability to read, question, interpret and communicate data in order to make decisions
Categorical data: data that places items in groups, such as province, product line or customer type
Numerical data: data measured or counted as numbers, such as sales value, units or days late
Time series: values of the same measure recorded over successive periods, such as monthly sales
Data quality: how fit a dataset is for the decision it supports
:::

## 1. What data literacy is

Data literacy is not about writing formulas or code. It is the ability to read a report or dashboard, ask the right questions about it, interpret what it really says and explain it to others. A sales manager in Luanda who receives a weekly sales pack, a finance officer reading an ageing report and an HR officer reviewing absence figures all need the same four habits: read, question, interpret, communicate.

The DIKW pyramid is a widely used way to describe this journey. Raw data sits at the base. When you organise it and add context, it becomes information. When you understand patterns and causes, it becomes knowledge. When you use that knowledge to choose the right action, you reach wisdom. Many reports stop at the second level: they show information but never answer "why?" or "what should we do?". Your job as a data-literate professional is to push the conversation up the pyramid.

::: diagram pyramid | The DIKW pyramid
Wisdom: choosing the right action – "cut credit to province X and visit the top 10 customers"
Knowledge: understanding causes – "sales fell because two large customers stopped buying"
Information: data with context – "Benguela sales fell 18% versus last month"
Data: raw facts – invoice lines, dates, amounts, customer codes
:::

## 2. Types of data

The type of data decides what you can calculate and which chart makes sense. Use these questions in order:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Words or numbers? | Qualitative: comments, reasons, opinions; Quantitative: counts and measurements; Both are useful for decisions | Start by naming the type
SLIDE Step 2: If categorical, is there an order? | Nominal: no order, e.g. province or product line; Ordinal: ordered, e.g. satisfaction from 1 to 5 or A-B-C customers; Count or compare shares, do not average codes | Customer code 4 is not twice code 2
SLIDE Step 3: If numerical, counted or measured? | Discrete: counted, e.g. number of orders; Continuous: measured, e.g. weight, kwanza value, delivery time | Both can be averaged
SLIDE Step 4: One moment or over time? | Cross-sectional: many branches this month; Time series: one branch over 24 months; Time series show trend and seasonality | Compare like periods with like
:::

A common trap is a numeric code. If a system stores provinces as 1 to 18, the "average province" of 7.4 means nothing. Another trap is seasonality: sales of some products in Angola move with the school year, festive seasons or the rainy season, so comparing December with January can mislead. For a time series, compare the same month last year or use a rolling average.

::: quiz
? A customer survey asks people to rate delivery service from 1 (very poor) to 5 (excellent). What type of data is this?
- Continuous numerical data || The scale has only five fixed values and the gaps between them are not measured quantities.
* Ordinal categorical data || Correct. The categories have an order, but the distance between 2 and 3 is not guaranteed to equal the distance between 4 and 5. Report the share of each score.
- Nominal categorical data || The scores do have a natural order, so they are ordinal, not nominal.
:::

## 3. Six data quality dimensions

Before you trust a number, check the data behind it. Data management professionals commonly use six dimensions:

- Accuracy: does the data match reality? (Does the stock report match a physical count?)
- Completeness: is anything missing? (Are all branches included this month?)
- Consistency: do systems and reports agree? (Does the sales report match the finance ledger?)
- Timeliness: is the data recent enough for this decision? (When was it last updated?)
- Validity: does each value follow the rules? (No negative quantities, valid dates, correct units.)
- Uniqueness: is each record counted once? (No duplicate customers or invoices.)

A practical method is to pick three numbers in any report and trace them back to their source. If you cannot, treat the whole report with caution.

::: quiz
? A dashboard shows 1,240 active customers, but the same customer appears three times with slightly different spellings. Which dimension fails?
- Timeliness || Timeliness is about how recent the data is, not about repeated records.
- Validity || Each record may follow the format rules. The problem is that one customer is counted more than once.
* Uniqueness || Correct. Duplicate records inflate the customer count. The number of active customers is overstated.
- Completeness || Completeness is about missing records. Here there are extra records, not missing ones.
:::

## Worked example: A monthly report at a food distributor in Viana

::: case | The missing branch in Viana
Marta is commercial analyst at a food distributor in Viana. The monthly report shows total sales of 412 million Kz, down 9% on last month, and her director wants to cut the marketing budget. Marta notices that the Lubango branch shows zero sales, although trucks left the warehouse all month.
? What should Marta do first?
- Send the report as it is because the director needs it today || The director would make a decision based on an incomplete total. A short delay is better than a wrong decision.
* Check with the Lubango branch and IT whether its invoices were uploaded, then correct and resend the report || Correct. This is a completeness problem. She confirms the cause and fixes the data before anyone acts on it.
- Remove Lubango from last month too so the comparison is fair || This hides the problem and understates the business. It also changes historical data without a reason.
- Estimate Lubango's sales using last month's figure || An estimate may help later, but first she must find out whether the real data exists.
:::

Marta discovers that a system update stopped Lubango's invoices from uploading. Once loaded, total sales are 451 million Kz, roughly level with last month. The planned marketing cut is cancelled. One quality check prevented a costly decision.

## Tool: Data quality checklist for any report

- Source: which system and who prepared it?
- Period and refresh date: is the data recent enough?
- Coverage: are all branches, products and channels included?
- Reconciliation: does the total agree with finance or another trusted source?
- Units and currency: Kz or USD, units or cases, with or without VAT?
- Duplicates and blanks: any repeated or missing records?
- Data type: are categories being averaged as if they were numbers?

## Common mistakes

- Treating numeric codes (province 1–18, customer class 1–4) as real numbers.
- Comparing months without allowing for seasonality.
- Accepting a total without checking that all units are included.
- Forgetting to check whether figures include or exclude VAT.

## Self-check

1. What are the four levels of the DIKW pyramid, from the base up? (Answer: data, information, knowledge, wisdom.)
2. Is "number of deliveries per day" discrete or continuous? (Answer: discrete, because it is counted.)
3. A report was last refreshed three weeks ago. Which quality dimension is at risk? (Answer: timeliness.)

## Practical assignment

Take one report you receive regularly. List every measure in it and classify each by data type. Then apply the data quality checklist and trace three numbers back to their source. Write down any problem you find and who can fix it.

## Summary

- Data literacy means reading, questioning, interpreting and communicating data, with no coding required.
- The data type decides which calculations and charts are valid.
- Check accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity and uniqueness before you trust a number.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- choose the right average (mean, median or mode) for a set of data;
- use the range to describe how spread out results are;
- explain the difference between a percentage change and a change in percentage points;

## Key terms

::: terms
Mean: the sum of all values divided by the number of values
Median: the middle value when all values are sorted from smallest to largest
Mode: the value or category that appears most often
Range: the difference between the largest and the smallest value
Percentage point: the arithmetic difference between two percentages
Base effect: a large percentage change caused by a small starting value
:::

## 1. Choosing the right average

The word "average" usually means the mean, but the mean can mislead when the data is skewed, that is, when a few very large or very small values pull it away from the typical case.

Take nine orders received by a distributor in Kz thousand: 120, 135, 140, 150, 155, 160, 170, 180 and 1,890. The total is 3,100, so the mean is 3,100 ÷ 9 = about 344. The median is the fifth value when sorted: 155. Eight of nine orders are below 200, so the median describes the typical order much better. The one large order, perhaps from a wholesaler, pulls the mean up. The range is 1,890 − 120 = 1,770, which warns you that order sizes vary a lot.

::: diagram steps | Choosing the right average
Is the data categorical? Use the mode, for example the most common reason for returns
Is the numerical data skewed by a few extreme values? Use the median, for example order values, salaries, days to pay
Is the data roughly symmetric? Use the mean, for example daily output of a stable production line
Not sure? Report both mean and median and explain the gap
:::

When the mean and median are far apart, ask what is causing the gap. Often the answer is a small group of very large customers or a data error, and both are worth knowing.

::: quiz
? An HR report says the average salary in a department is 950,000 Kz, but most staff earn between 300,000 and 450,000 Kz. What is the most likely explanation?
- Most staff were paid incorrectly this month || The figure can be explained without any error, so this is not the most likely cause.
* A few very high salaries are pulling the mean up, so the median would describe a typical salary better || Correct. Salaries are usually skewed. The median shows what a typical employee earns.
- The mode should have been used because salary is categorical || Salary is numerical, not categorical. The mode is rarely useful for continuous amounts.
:::

## 2. Percentages and percentage points

A percentage change is relative to the starting value. A percentage point change is the simple difference between two percentages. Suppose your market share in a province rises from 20% to 25%. That is an increase of 5 percentage points, but a relative increase of 25% (5 ÷ 20). Both statements are true, but they sound very different. A presentation that says "share up 25%" without explanation may lead people to think share is now 45%.

The same care applies to margins, interest rates and conversion rates. If gross margin falls from 12% to 9%, say "down 3 percentage points", which is a 25% relative fall in margin. Also check the base: a new product that grows from 2 to 6 cases has grown 200%, but this tells you very little. This is the base effect.

::: quiz
? A bank's non-performing loan ratio rose from 4% to 6%. Which statement is correct?
- It rose by 2% || This is ambiguous. Readers may think it rose from 4% to 4.08%.
- It rose by 50 percentage points || The ratio changed by 2 points, not 50. The 50% figure is the relative change.
* It rose by 2 percentage points, a 50% relative increase || Correct. 6 − 4 = 2 points, and 2 ÷ 4 = 50% relative growth.
- It rose by 6% || 6% is the new level, not the change.
:::

## 3. Calculating growth correctly

Three growth calculations cover most business needs.

::: slides
SLIDE Simple growth | Growth = (new value - old value) / old value; Sales from 40 to 46 million Kz: (46 - 40) / 40 = 15%; Always state the period | Compare with the right base period
SLIDE CAGR | Use for growth over several years; CAGR = (end / start)^(1/years) - 1; From 100 to 133.1 million Kz in 3 years = 10% per year | A smooth yearly rate for uneven years
SLIDE Real growth | Removes the effect of inflation; Real = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1; 25% nominal with 20% inflation = about 4.2% real | Volume tells the truth when prices rise
SLIDE Volume check | Look at units, tonnes or cases as well as value; If value is up but volume is down, growth is price only; Ask how much is price and how much is volume | Kwanza growth can hide fewer sales
:::

In an economy where prices change quickly, nominal growth in kwanzas can look strong while real activity is flat or falling. Always ask for volume figures beside value figures, and use the official inflation rate for the same period when you calculate real growth.

## Worked example: Annual review at a rice importer in Luanda

::: case | Is the rice business really growing?
Hélder, sales manager at a rice importer in Luanda, presents to the board: sales value rose from 2.0 billion Kz to 2.5 billion Kz, so "growth is 25%". Over the same year tonnes sold fell from 5,000 to 4,600, and inflation for the period was 20%.
? Which conclusion is most accurate?
- The business grew strongly by 25% and the team should be rewarded || 25% is nominal value growth. It ignores both inflation and the fall in tonnes.
- The business grew by 25 percentage points || Growth from 2.0 to 2.5 billion is a relative change of 25%, not a change in percentage points.
* Real value growth was only about 4% and volume fell 8%, so growth came from higher prices, not more sales || Correct. 1.25 ÷ 1.20 − 1 ≈ 4.2%, and (4,600 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 = −8%. The team is selling less rice at higher prices.
- Nothing can be concluded without the median order size || The median is useful for other questions, but the data given already answers this one.
:::

Hélder rewrites the slide: "Value +25%, real value +4%, volume −8%: we are losing tonnes. Next step: analyse volume by customer and region." The board now discusses the right problem.

## Tool: Numbers check before you present

- Have I used the median where data is skewed?
- Have I shown the range or spread, not only the average?
- Are percentage changes and percentage-point changes labelled clearly?
- Have I stated the base value and the period for every growth rate?
- Have I shown volume as well as value?
- Have I calculated real growth if inflation is significant?

## Common mistakes

- Reporting only the mean when a few large values distort it.
- Writing "up 5%" when the change is 5 percentage points.
- Presenting kwanza growth without adjusting for inflation or showing volume.
- Celebrating very high growth rates that come from a tiny base.

## Self-check

1. Values are 3, 4, 4, 5 and 34. What are the mean and the median? (Answer: mean 10, median 4.)
2. Market share falls from 30% to 27%. What is the change in percentage points? (Answer: −3 percentage points, a 10% relative fall.)
3. Sales grow from 50 to 72 million Kz in two years. What is the CAGR? (Answer: (72 ÷ 50)^(1/2) − 1 = 1.2 − 1 = 20% per year.)

## Practical assignment

Choose a growth figure from a recent report at your organisation. Recalculate it, state whether it is nominal or real, and find the volume figure for the same period. Write one sentence that explains how much growth came from price and how much from volume.

## Summary

- Use the median for skewed data such as order values and salaries, and always look at spread.
- Percentage points and percentages are different: label them clearly.
- Check growth for base effects, inflation and volume before you celebrate it.

Dashboard: a single screen of key measures that shows performance at a glance

## 1. Matching the chart to the question

Every good chart answers one question. Before you judge a chart, ask what question it is trying to answer, and whether the chart type suits it:

- Comparing categories (sales by province): bar chart, sorted from largest to smallest.
- Trend over time (monthly sales for 24 months): line chart.
- Part of a whole (share of sales by channel): stacked bar, or a pie chart only with two to four slices.
- Relationship between two measures (price versus volume by customer): scatter plot.
- Distribution (how many invoices fall into each payment-delay band): histogram.

A pie chart with twelve slices, a 3D bar chart or a line chart for unrelated categories makes the reader work harder and often hides the message.

::: quiz
? A manager wants to show how the number of days customers take to pay is distributed across 600 invoices. Which chart is best?
- Pie chart with one slice per customer || 600 slices are unreadable, and a pie shows shares, not distribution.
* Histogram of invoices by payment-delay band (0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 90+ days) || Correct. A histogram shows how values are spread across ranges.
- Line chart with invoices in random order || A line chart implies an ordered sequence such as time. Random order gives a meaningless line.
:::

## 2. Misleading axes and the lie factor

The most common distortion is a truncated axis on a bar chart. The length of a bar is supposed to represent the value. If the axis starts at 96 instead of zero, a value of 98 gets a bar 2 units tall and a value of 102 gets a bar 6 units tall. The second bar looks three times bigger, although the real difference is about 4%.

Edward Tufte, in The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983), proposed the lie factor to measure this:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Effect in the data | (102 - 98) / 98 = about 4.1%; This is the real change; Use the actual values | Start from the numbers
SLIDE Step 2: Effect shown in the chart | Bar heights above the axis start: 2 and 6; (6 - 2) / 2 = 200%; This is what the eye sees | Measure what is drawn
SLIDE Step 3: Lie factor | 200% / 4.1% = about 49; A value near 1 is honest; Tufte treated values far from 1 as serious distortion | This chart exaggerates the change about 49 times
SLIDE Step 4: Fix it | Start bar charts at zero; For small changes use a line chart and label the values; Or show the change itself as a number | Honest charts build trust
:::

Line charts may start above zero when the aim is to show variation over time, but the axis must be clearly labelled. Be careful also with dual-axis charts: by choosing the scales of the two axes, almost any two lines can be made to look as if they move together.

## 3. Cherry-picking and missing context

A chart can be technically accurate and still mislead through what it leaves out. Watch for:

- Chosen time windows: starting a trend at an unusually bad month makes recovery look strong.
- Selected units: showing only the three best branches.
- No comparison: a number without a target, last year or trend cannot be judged.
- Cumulative charts: a running total always rises, even when monthly sales are falling.

::: quiz
? A presentation shows a cumulative sales line that rises every month this year, and the presenter says "we are growing every month". What should you ask?
- Whether the line is in a nice colour || Colour does not change the meaning of the data.
- Whether a pie chart would be better || A pie chart cannot show change over time either.
* To see monthly sales, because a cumulative total rises even if each month is lower than the last || Correct. Running totals hide monthly decline. Ask for the monthly figures.
- Whether the y-axis starts at zero || A useful habit, but it does not address the main problem with cumulative totals.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
Correlation: a relationship in which two measures tend to move together
Causation: a relationship in which a change in one factor produces a change in another
Confounding variable: a third factor that influences both measures and creates a false link
Sample: the part of a population that is actually measured
Bias: a systematic error that pushes results in one direction
:::

## 1. Correlation is not causation

When two measures move together, it is tempting to assume one causes the other. Sales of cold drinks and sales of sun hats rise together, but neither causes the other: hot weather drives both. Hot weather is a confounding variable. Other explanations for a correlation are reverse causation (branches with more staff sell more, but perhaps high sales are why they were given more staff) and coincidence.

To test a causal claim, use these questions, simplified from the criteria proposed by the epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill (1965):

::: diagram steps | Testing a causal claim
Time order: did the cause happen before the effect?
Alternatives: could a confounder, seasonality or a price change explain it?
Dose-response: does more of the cause give more of the effect?
Mechanism: is there a sensible explanation of how it works?
Test: did a control group without the cause show a different result?
:::

The strongest evidence is a controlled test. If you want to know whether a new display stand raises sales, place it in 10 shops, keep 10 similar shops without it as a control group, and compare the change in both groups over the same weeks.

::: quiz
? Sales in a supermarket rose 20% in the month a new loyalty card was launched. The month was also December. What is the best conclusion?
- The loyalty card caused a 20% rise || December is a festive month. Seasonality may explain much of the rise.
* The card may have helped, but the effect must be compared with last December and with stores without the card || Correct. Seasonality is a likely confounder, so a proper comparison is needed.
- The card had no effect || Nothing in the data proves the card had no effect. We simply cannot tell yet.
:::

## 2. Sampling and bias

Most business data is a sample. The question is whether the sample is typical of the whole population.

::: slides
SLIDE Selection bias | The sample is chosen in a way that is not typical; Example: a customer survey only at Luanda stores; Results cannot be applied to the provinces | Check how the sample was chosen
SLIDE Survivorship bias | You only see the units that survived; In World War II, Abraham Wald advised armouring the parts of returning planes that had no bullet holes; Planes hit there did not return | Ask what you cannot see
SLIDE Business survivorship | Studying only current customers ignores lost ones; Copying only successful shops ignores failed shops with the same strategy; Exit data is often the most useful | Talk to the customers who left
SLIDE Non-response bias | Only some people reply to surveys; Very happy or very angry customers reply most; Report the response rate | Silence is also data
:::

Survivorship bias is common in sales. If you analyse why current customers buy from you, you learn nothing about why lost customers left. A distributor that wants to reduce churn must collect data on the customers who stopped ordering.

## 3. Simpson's paradox and small samples

Simpson's paradox happens when a trend that appears in every group reverses when the groups are combined. Consider two collectors of overdue invoices:

- Ana: Luanda accounts 80 of 100 collected (80%), provincial accounts 10 of 20 (50%). Total 90 of 120 = 75%.
- Bruno: Luanda 18 of 20 (90%), provinces 60 of 100 (60%). Total 78 of 120 = 65%.

Bruno is better in both segments, yet Ana looks better overall because most of her accounts are in Luanda, where collection is easier. Always break totals down by the main segments before comparing people, branches or products.

Small samples swing widely. For a survey result given as a percentage, a common rule of thumb for the margin of error at about 95% confidence is 1 ÷ √n. With 100 replies the margin is about ±10 percentage points, with 400 replies about ±5 points, and with 25 replies about ±20 points. A satisfaction score of 70% from 25 customers could easily be anywhere from 50% to 90%.

::: quiz
? A survey of 100 customers shows 62% prefer the new packaging. Using the 1 ÷ √n rule, what is the approximate range?
- 61% to 63% || This range is far too narrow for a sample of 100.
* 52% to 72% || Correct. 1 ÷ √100 = 0.10, so the margin is about ±10 percentage points.
- 32% to 92% || This is far too wide. That margin would apply to a very small sample.
- Exactly 62%, because the survey measured it || Any sample has uncertainty. Another 100 customers would give a different result.
:::

## Worked example: A supplier decision at a construction company in Benguela

::: case | The cheaper cement in Benguela
Rui, procurement officer at a construction company in Benguela, reads a site report: on the six sites using a cheaper cement supplier, defect rates were 30% lower than on sites using the usual supplier. He is asked to switch all 20 sites. He learns that the six sites are all small housing jobs, while the others include large structural works.
? What should Rui recommend?
- Switch all 20 sites now because defects are 30% lower || The sites differ in type. The lower defect rate may come from the simpler work, not the cement.
- Reject the cheaper supplier because the data is useless || The data is not useless. It is just not yet enough to prove the cause.
* Compare defects by site type and run a controlled trial on similar sites before deciding || Correct. Site type is a likely confounder. A fair comparison on similar sites tests the real effect of the cement.
- Survey site managers who like the new supplier || This adds selection bias and does not measure defects.
:::

Rui arranges a three-month trial on four pairs of similar sites. Only then does he present a recommendation.

## Tool: Bias and causation checklist

- How was the sample selected, and who is missing?
- How many observations are there? What is 1 ÷ √n?
- What was the response rate?
- Did the cause come before the effect?
- What confounders could explain the link (season, price, region, customer size)?
- Do the results hold inside each main segment?
- Is there a control group or a fair comparison?

## Common mistakes

- Crediting a campaign for growth that came from seasonality.
- Learning only from successful branches or current customers.
- Making big decisions from a handful of results.
- Comparing totals across groups with different mixes.

## Self-check

1. What is a confounding variable? (Answer: a third factor that affects both measures and creates an apparent link.)
2. Why did Abraham Wald recommend armouring areas without bullet holes? (Answer: because planes hit in those areas did not return, so the damage was invisible.)
3. What is the approximate margin of error for 400 survey replies? (Answer: about ±5 percentage points.)

## Key terms

::: terms
Metric: any quantity that is measured, such as calls made or invoices issued
KPI (key performance indicator): a metric directly linked to a key objective, with a target and an owner
Lagging indicator: a measure of a result that has already happened, such as monthly sales
Leading indicator: an early measure that predicts a future result and can be influenced now
Vanity metric: a number that looks good but does not support any decision
:::

## 1. Leading and lagging indicators

Most reports are full of lagging indicators: sales, margin, profit, customer losses. They tell you whether you succeeded, but only after it is too late to change the result. Leading indicators give early warning. For a distributor, sales are lagging, while customer visits completed, active outlets, quotes sent and stock cover in days are leading.

Good scorecards pair them. If the lagging KPI is "monthly sales volume", useful leading KPIs might be "planned visits completed" and "outlets with no stock-outs". If visits fall in week 1, you can act before sales fall in week 4.

A simple grid helps decide which measures deserve KPI status:

::: diagram matrix | Which measures deserve attention? | Your ability to influence (low → high) | Importance to the goal (low → high)
Monitor: important but outside your control, such as exchange rate – track it as context
Key KPI: important and within your control – set a target and manage it weekly
Drop: low importance and low influence – remove it from the dashboard
Operational metric: low importance but easy to influence – let the team manage it
:::

::: quiz
? A sales team's goal is to increase monthly revenue. Which is a leading indicator?
- Monthly revenue || This is the lagging result itself.
- Last quarter's gross profit || This is also a past result, not an early signal.
* Number of planned customer visits completed each week || Correct. Visits happen before orders, can be influenced now and predict future sales.
- Annual market share || Market share is a lagging outcome measured after the period.
:::

## 2. Defining a KPI properly

Many KPI arguments come from vague definitions. "Delivery performance" can mean on time, in full, or both, measured against the promised date or the requested date. Use a KPI card for every KPI:

::: slides
SLIDE Name and purpose | A clear name, e.g. OTIF (on time in full); Which objective it supports; Why it matters to the customer | One KPI, one purpose
SLIDE Formula and source | OTIF = orders delivered on time and complete / total orders; Data from the delivery system and signed delivery notes; Define on time: promised date | Write the formula in words and symbols
SLIDE Frequency and owner | Weekly, by depot; Owner: logistics manager; Reviewed in the Monday meeting | Every KPI needs one owner
SLIDE Target and threshold | Target: 92%; Alert: below 85% triggers a review; Paired KPI: delivery cost per order | Targets need a counterweight
:::

Example: in one week a depot dispatched 400 orders. 340 arrived on the promised date, and 316 of those were complete. OTIF = 316 ÷ 400 = 79%. On-time alone would show 85%, which is why the definition matters.

Pairing is important because of Goodhart's law, often summarised as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". If drivers are judged only on speed, they may skip deliveries that are hard to reach. Pair speed with completeness, or volume with margin.

::: quiz
? Credit sales in a quarter (90 days) were 270 million Kz and receivables at the end were 90 million Kz. Using DSO = receivables ÷ credit sales × days, what is DSO?
- 3 days || This divides credit sales by receivables and forgets to multiply by the number of days.
* 30 days || Correct. 90 ÷ 270 × 90 = 30 days. On average customers take about 30 days to pay.
- 90 days || 90 is the length of the period, not the time customers take to pay.
- 270 days || This confuses the sales amount with a number of days.
:::

## 3. Asking good questions of data

Analytics questions are often grouped into four types, each more valuable than the last: descriptive (what happened?), diagnostic (why did it happen?), predictive (what is likely to happen?) and prescriptive (what should we do?). A good meeting moves through all four instead of stopping at the first.

Then apply the "so what?" test to every number on a dashboard. "Stock-outs rose to 7%" – so what? "We lost an estimated 15 million Kz of sales in Cabinda" – now what? "Increase safety stock on the top 20 items and review supplier lead times." If you cannot answer "so what?", the number is probably a vanity metric. To find root causes, the Five Whys technique from the Toyota Production System is useful: ask "why?" repeatedly until you reach a cause you can act on.

## Worked example: Choosing KPIs at a beverage distributor in Lubango

::: case | Too many numbers in Lubango
Eduardo is the new regional manager of a beverage distributor in Lubango. His weekly report has 42 measures, including website visits, number of emails sent and total trucks owned. Sales fell 6% last quarter and nobody knows why.
? What should Eduardo do first?
- Add more measures so nothing is missed || More measures make it harder to see what matters.
* Agree the main objective, then choose a few lagging and leading KPIs with full KPI cards, and drop measures that fail the so-what test || Correct. He links the measures to the goal, defines them clearly and removes noise.
- Keep the report but colour all falling measures red || Colour helps, but 42 undefined measures still hide the cause.
- Focus only on sales because it is the most important number || Sales is lagging. Without leading KPIs he cannot act early.
:::

Eduardo keeps six KPIs: sales volume, gross margin, active outlets, visits completed, OTIF and DSO. Within a month, the leading KPIs show that active outlets fell in two districts after a salesperson left.

## Key terms

::: terms
Data-informed decision: a decision that uses data as key evidence, combined with context and judgement
Data storytelling: presenting data as a clear message with context, insight and a recommended action
Pyramid principle: a structure that puts the main answer first, then the supporting arguments and evidence
Expected value: the average outcome of a decision, weighting each possible result by its probability
Personal data: any information relating to an identified or identifiable person
:::

## 1. A five-stage decision cycle

"Data-driven" can suggest that the numbers decide. In practice, good managers are data-informed: they combine data with knowledge of customers, markets and people. The process below keeps analysis focused and decisions accountable.

::: diagram cycle | The data-informed decision cycle
Frame: state the decision, the options, the deadline and what evidence would change your mind
Analyse: gather the relevant data, check quality and test the main assumptions
Decide: choose an option, record the reasons and the expected result
Communicate: share the decision as a clear story with the uncertainty stated
Review: compare actual results with the expected result and learn
:::

The framing step is the one most often skipped. "Analyse our sales data" is not a question. "Should we open a depot in Huambo next year, yes or no, decision by 30 June?" is. Writing down in advance what result would change your mind protects you from looking only for evidence that supports what you already believe (confirmation bias).

::: quiz
? A director asks the analyst to "look at all our customer data and find something interesting". What is the best first response?
- Start building charts of every variable available || Without a decision in mind, the analyst may spend weeks on charts that change nothing.
* Ask which decision the director is facing and by when, then focus the analysis on that || Correct. Framing the decision first keeps the analysis relevant and fast.
- Say that the data is not good enough for analysis || That may or may not be true. The first issue is the lack of a clear question.
:::

## 2. Telling the story with data

Busy decision-makers want the answer first. Barbara Minto's pyramid principle recommends starting with the main conclusion, then giving the few key arguments that support it, then the evidence. Many analysts do the opposite: method, then data, then the conclusion on the last slide.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Lead with the answer | One sentence: what you recommend and why; Example: open a Huambo depot in Q3 to cut delivery cost and reach 140 new outlets; Put it in the title or first line | The title is the message
SLIDE Step 2: Give context | Why the question matters now; What the current situation is; Keep it to two or three facts | Context makes numbers meaningful
SLIDE Step 3: Show the insight | One chart per message; Highlight the key bar or line in a strong colour; Grey out the rest | Remove anything that does not support the message
SLIDE Step 4: State the action | What, who, by when; What it costs; How success will be measured | End with the decision you need
:::

A useful frame for each chart is "What? So what? Now what?": what the data shows, why it matters and what should happen next.

## 3. Communicating uncertainty

Every forecast is uncertain. Writing "sales will be 1,247,832,000 Kz" suggests false precision. Better: "about 1.2 billion Kz, likely between 1.1 and 1.4 billion". For bigger decisions, use three scenarios (best, base and worst case) and state the key assumption behind each, such as exchange rate or volume.

Expected value helps compare options. Suppose opening a new depot has a 60% chance of earning 30 million Kz profit in year one and a 40% chance of losing 10 million Kz. Expected value = (0.6 × 30) + (0.4 × −10) = 18 − 4 = 14 million Kz. Compare this with the alternatives, and also ask whether the organisation can afford the worst case.

::: quiz
? Option A has a 50% chance of +40 million Kz and a 50% chance of −20 million Kz. Option B gives a certain +12 million Kz. What is the expected value of A, and what else should be considered?
- 20 million Kz, so A is always better || Expected value is not the only factor. A has a 50% chance of a 20 million loss.
* 10 million Kz, so B is higher and also has no risk of loss || Correct. (0.5 × 40) + (0.5 × −20) = 10 million Kz, below B's certain 12 million.
- 60 million Kz, the sum of both outcomes || Expected value weights each outcome by its probability. It is not a simple sum.
:::

## 4. Privacy and ethics basics

In Angola, personal data is protected by Lei n.º 22/11 of 17 June (the Personal Data Protection Law), supervised by the Agência de Protecção de Dados (APD). Ask your legal or compliance team how it applies to your work. Practical habits for everyone: collect only the data you need for a stated purpose; anonymise or aggregate data before sharing it; restrict access to files with names, phone numbers, salaries or health information; never send such files to personal email or messaging groups. Ethical use also means presenting data honestly: no cherry-picking, no misleading axes, and stating the limits of your analysis.

## Worked example: A depot decision at an FMCG distributor in Luanda

::: case | The Huambo depot presentation
Sofia, a commercial analyst at an FMCG distributor in Luanda, must present her depot analysis to the board in 15 minutes. She has 25 slides starting with data sources and methods. Her forecast is "year one profit 18,436,000 Kz". She also plans to attach the full customer list with phone numbers.
? What is the best improvement?
- Add more slides on method so the board trusts the numbers || More method slides push the answer further back and use the board's limited time.
* Start with the recommendation, show the profit as a range with scenarios, and attach only aggregated customer data || Correct. Answer first, honest uncertainty and data minimisation make the presentation clear and responsible.
- Remove the forecast because it is uncertain || Uncertain forecasts are still useful when the uncertainty is shown clearly.
- Email the customer list in advance so the board can review it || Sharing personal data widely is unnecessary and creates privacy risk.
:::

Sofia cuts the deck to six slides. Her title reads: "Open the Huambo depot in Q3: base case profit 15–20 million Kz in year one."
