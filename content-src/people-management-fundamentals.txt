## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain how a manager's role differs from that of an individual contributor;
- use the Situational Leadership model to decide how much direction and support to give a team member;
- delegate a task using a five-part delegation brief;
- choose an appropriate level of delegation for a given person and task.

## Key terms

::: terms
Individual contributor: someone whose performance is judged mainly on their own work output
Manager: someone whose performance is judged mainly on the results of the people they are responsible for
Competence: a person's knowledge and skill for a specific task
Commitment: a person's motivation and confidence to do that task well
Delegation: giving someone responsibility for a result, together with the authority and resources needed to deliver it
:::

## 1. From doer to manager

When you are promoted, the basis on which you are judged changes. As an individual contributor you were measured on your own output. As a manager you are measured on what your team delivers.

Linda Hill of Harvard Business School followed a group of new managers through their first year. A recurring difficulty was letting go of the hands-on work that had earned them the promotion. Stepping in to do the work yourself whenever results are at risk feels productive, but it overloads you and stops the team from learning.

A useful test: if you were away for two weeks, what would stop? Every item on that list is something you have not yet built the team to handle.

## 2. Situational Leadership: one style does not fit all

The Situational Leadership model, developed by Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard, starts from a simple idea: there is no single best management style. The right style depends on how ready the person is for the specific task in front of them. Readiness has two parts, competence and commitment, and the model matches four development levels (D1 to D4) to four styles (S1 to S4):

::: diagram matrix | Situational Leadership: four styles | Directive behaviour (low → high) | Supportive behaviour (low → high)
Supporting (S3): for D3 – good competence, variable commitment – listen, encourage, share decisions
Coaching (S2): for D2 – some competence, low commitment – direct and encourage
Delegating (S4): for D4 – high competence and commitment – give trust and space
Directing (S1): for D1 – low competence, high commitment – give clear instructions
:::

Two points are often misunderstood. First, development level applies to a task, not to a person. Second, the goal is to move people up the levels, so your style should change as they grow.

::: quiz
? An accountant is D4 on month-end closing but has never used the new reporting system. Which style fits the new system?
- Delegating, because she is experienced || Development level depends on the task, not on general experience.
* Directing, because she is D1 on this task || Correct. On a new task she needs clear instructions, even if she is expert elsewhere.
- Supporting, because she only lacks confidence || She lacks competence on this system, not just confidence.
:::

## 3. The five-part delegation brief

Most delegation failures come from something that was never said. A short, explicit brief prevents them. Before handing over a task, agree five things:

::: slides
SLIDE 1 Outcome and quality standard | Describe what "done" looks like; Show an example if possible | No example, no shared picture
SLIDE 2 Deadline and milestones | The final date; Intermediate points for longer tasks | Milestones catch problems early
SLIDE 3 Decision authority | What they decide alone; What needs your input (see section 4) | Authority must match responsibility
SLIDE 4 Resources and people | Budget, systems and data; Colleagues they may involve | Remove obstacles before they start
SLIDE 5 Check-in points | Agree review dates in advance; Then check-ins feel like support, not suspicion | Put the dates in both calendars
:::

## 4. Choosing the level of delegation

Delegation is not all-or-nothing. Jurgen Appelo's "seven levels of delegation" (from the Management 3.0 approach) give a precise vocabulary:

- 1 Tell: you decide and announce the decision.
- 2 Sell: you decide and explain why.
- 3 Consult: you ask for input, then decide.
- 4 Agree: you decide together.
- 5 Advise: they decide, after hearing your advice.
- 6 Inquire: they decide, and tell you afterwards.
- 7 Delegate: they decide; you do not need to be informed.

Match the level to the development level from section 2: a D1 person might work at level 3 or 4 on a task, a D4 person at level 6 or 7. Say the level out loud, for example: "On supplier choice, this is a level 5: you decide, but talk to me first."

::: quiz
? You tell a supervisor: "Choose the new cleaning supplier, but talk to me before you sign." Which delegation level is this?
- Level 3, Consult || At level 3 you would decide yourself after hearing their input.
- Level 7, Delegate || At level 7 they would not need to talk to you at all.
* Level 5, Advise || Correct. They decide, after hearing your advice.
:::

## Worked example: a sales supervisor in Luanda

Marta has just been promoted to supervise five sales representatives at a food distributor in Luanda. In her first month she still visits her old key accounts, prepares the weekly sales report and approves every discount. She works until 9 p.m. and the team waits for her decisions. Her two-week test shows three items that would stop: the report, discount approvals and two key accounts.

- Weekly report → Pedro, good with Excel but new to the report (D1). Marta directs: she shows him past reports and briefs him: one page by Thursday 17:00, data errors fixed alone (level 6), differences above 5% discussed first (level 5).
- Discount approvals → Joana, a senior rep who knows margins well (D4). Level 6 for discounts up to 3%: Joana decides and records it in the CRM. Above 3% stays with Marta.
- Key accounts → Ana, who handled them before but lost confidence after a difficult negotiation (D3). Marta supports: she asks how Ana wants to approach the next visit, listens, and joins only as an observer.

::: case | A discount request in Cazenga
A large wholesaler in Cazenga asks Joana for 5% off a big rice order and wants an answer today. Joana's limit is 3%. She calls Marta, who is in a meeting with finance.
? What is the best way for Marta to handle this?
- Tell Joana to give the 5% so the order is not lost || This overrides the agreed limit and teaches the team that limits are not real.
* Call Joana back, ask for her recommendation, decide together, and later review whether the limit should change || Correct. Above 3% the decision stays with Marta, but asking for Joana's view builds her judgement.
- Take over the account and negotiate with the client herself || This undermines Joana's ownership.
:::

Within six weeks Marta has recovered about eight hours a week, and three people have clear areas of ownership.

## Common mistakes

- Using one style for everyone. Over-directing a D4 person feels like micromanagement; leaving a D1 person alone feels like abandonment.
- Taking the task back at the first problem. Help solve the problem, but leave ownership with the person.
- Giving responsibility without authority. Asking for a result without the decision rights or information to achieve it.

## Key terms

::: terms
Priority: an area of results the organisation has decided matters most in a period
Goal: a specific result to be achieved by a specific date
Baseline: the value of a measure before you start working to improve it
Objective: in OKRs, a short qualitative statement of what you want to achieve
Key result: in OKRs, a measurable outcome that shows whether the objective has been achieved

## 1. SMART goals

Senior management often states priorities broadly, such as "improve customer service". A manager turns them into goals the team can act on and measure. The most widely used test is SMART, introduced by George T. Doran in a 1981 article in Management Review. Today the letters are usually read as:

- Specific: clearly states what will change and in which area.
- Measurable: names the metric and target, so anyone can tell whether it was achieved.
- Achievable: stretching but realistic with the people, budget and time available.
- Relevant: supports a priority of the team or the company.
- Time-bound: has a deadline and, if needed, intermediate dates.

Write a SMART goal in four steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Choose the result area | Start from the company priority; Example: delivery reliability | One area per goal
SLIDE Step 2: Pick a metric and find the baseline | One metric, such as OTIF (on-time, in-full) deliveries; Find today's value in your data | No baseline, no real target
SLIDE Step 3: Set target, date and owner | Check the target with the people who will do the work; Name one owner | In Doran's article, A meant "assignable"
SLIDE Step 4: Write it and test it | "Increase OTIF to supermarkets in Luanda from 82% to 95% by 31 March"; Tick S, M, A, R and T | One sentence, five ticks
:::

::: quiz
? Which goal meets all five SMART criteria?
- "Improve customer service this year" || There is no metric or target, so it is neither specific nor measurable.
- "Call more clients every week" || There is no baseline, target or deadline.
* "Cut complaint response time from 3 days to 1 day by 30 June" || Correct. It names the area, metric, baseline, target and date.
:::

## 2. OKRs: objectives and key results

OKRs were developed at Intel by Andy Grove and made widely known by John Doerr, who introduced them to Google and described them in his book Measure What Matters (2018). An OKR has two parts. The objective is what you want to achieve: qualitative, short and motivating The key results, typically three to five, show in numbers whether you got there. Doerr summarises the format as: "I will [objective] as measured by [key results]."

A key result describes an outcome, not an activity. "Hold weekly route-planning meetings" is an activity. "Reduce failed deliveries from 6% to 2% of drops" is an outcome. Doerr also distinguishes committed OKRs, which must be fully achieved, from aspirational OKRs, which are deliberately ambitious and where reaching most of the target is a good result. Tell your team which type each OKR is.

Track each key result as a share of the distance from baseline to target:

Progress = (current value − baseline) ÷ (target − baseline) × 100

If OTIF started at 82%, the target is 95% and it is now 90%, progress is (90 − 82) ÷ (95 − 82) = 8 ÷ 13 = 62%. Review it weekly, while there is time to act.

::: quiz
? A key result is "Increase active outlets from 200 to 300". There are now 260. What is the progress?
- 87% || This divides 260 by 300. Progress is measured from the baseline, not from zero.
- 23% || This divides the gain by 260 instead of by the planned gain of 100.
* 60% || Correct. (260 − 200) ÷ (300 − 200) = 60 ÷ 100 = 60%.
:::

## 3. The Eisenhower matrix: urgent versus important

Goals usually fail not because they are badly written but because daily pressure pushes them aside. The Eisenhower matrix, named after US President Dwight D. Eisenhower and popularised by Stephen Covey in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, sorts every task with two questions. Is it urgent: does it demand attention now? Is it important: does it contribute to our goals?

::: diagram matrix | The Eisenhower matrix | Urgency (low → high) | Importance (low → high)
Schedule: important, not urgent – training, fixing the causes of repeated errors
Do now: important and urgent – a key client's delivery truck has broken down
Eliminate: neither – a report nobody reads
Delegate: urgent, not important – routine requests and interruptions
:::

Goal work sits mostly in the "schedule" box, the work most often postponed. Block fixed time for it and sort your tasks with the matrix each week.

## 4. Cascading goals

Cascading links every level: company priority → department goals → team goals → individual goals. At each level, ask: "What must we deliver for the level above to succeed?" Every goal should show which higher goal it supports. Do not only push goals down. Doerr recommends that some OKRs are proposed by the people who will do the work, then agreed with the manager. People commit more strongly to goals they helped to shape.

## Worked example: a distribution manager in Luanda

Ricardo manages distribution for a food importer that supplies supermarkets in Luanda. The company priority is "Win and keep modern-trade customers". His data shows OTIF at 82% and failed deliveries at 6% of drops. He writes one OKR for the quarter:

Objective: Become the most reliable supplier for our supermarket clients in Luanda.

- KR1: Increase OTIF from 82% to 95% by 31 March (committed).
- KR2: Reduce failed deliveries from 6% to 2% of drops by 31 March (committed).

He cascades: the warehouse supervisor proposes "Reduce picking errors from 3% to 1% of order lines by 28 February", which supports KR1. By mid-February OTIF is 90%, or 62% progress on KR1.

::: case | Ricardo's Monday morning
On Monday Ricardo faces a store manager calling about a delivery missing today, a weekly report nobody reads, a routine finance request, and a driver training session due in two weeks that supports KR2.
? Using the Eisenhower matrix, what should he do with the training session?
- Do it first, before everything else || It matters, but the missing delivery is urgent and important, so it comes first.
- Delegate it to his assistant || It drives KR2 directly and needs Ricardo's own input.
* Block fixed time for it in his calendar this week || Correct. Important but not urgent work must be scheduled, or urgent tasks will keep pushing it aside.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
One-to-one (1:1): a regular, private meeting between a manager and one team member
Active listening: listening with full attention to understand both the facts and the feelings behind what someone says, and showing that understanding
Paraphrasing: restating what the other person said in your own words to check understanding
Open question: a question that cannot be answered with "yes" or "no" and invites explanation
Closed question: a question that can be answered with "yes", "no" or a single fact
Action log: a written list of agreed actions, each with an owner and a due date
:::

## 1. Why one-to-ones matter

People rarely raise concerns, doubts or ideas in front of colleagues. The one-to-one gives each person regular, protected time with their manager. Andy Grove, in High Output Management, described it as the team member's meeting, with the agenda and tone set mainly by them, while the manager listens, coaches and learns. Regular one-to-ones reveal problems before they become missed deadlines or resignations, and build the trust that feedback and coaching need.

How often? A common rhythm is 30 minutes every week or two. Grove linked frequency to experience with the task: more often with someone new to a job, less often with someone experienced. Do the arithmetic first: six direct reports at 30 minutes weekly is three hours a week; fortnightly it is ninety minutes. Choose a rhythm you can keep, because a cancelled one-to-one tells people they are not a priority.

## 2. A four-part agenda

A repeated structure makes one-to-ones efficient. Share a short agenda document and ask the team member to add their topics before the meeting.

::: diagram steps | The one-to-one agenda
Their topics (about half the time): problems, ideas and requests – this shows the meeting belongs to them
Your topics: information they need, decisions, observations
Progress: key results from lesson 2 and actions agreed last time
Development and agreements: one growth question, then a summary of actions
:::

Prepare for five minutes: read your last notes, check the action log and choose your one or two topics. Meet somewhere private and put your phone away.

## 3. Active listening

The term active listening was introduced by the psychologists Carl Rogers and Richard Farson in a 1957 paper. They argued that listeners should try to understand the total meaning of a message, both its content and the feelings behind it, and show the speaker that they have understood. Five techniques put this into practice:

::: slides
SLIDE Attend | Full attention and eye contact; No phone, no interrupting | Attention is the first message you send
SLIDE Paraphrase | "So the main problem is that the warehouse releases orders late?"; Use your own words, then check | Confirms the facts
SLIDE Reflect feelings | "It sounds like you are frustrated that nobody told you."; Name the emotion you notice | Confirms the feelings
SLIDE Clarify and summarise | "When you say the client is unhappy, what exactly did they say?"; Summarise key points at the end of each topic | Close the topic with shared understanding
SLIDE Allow silence | Ask, then wait; Do not answer your own question | Silence gives people time to think
:::

::: quiz
? A team member says: "Nobody told me the delivery schedule changed, and I looked stupid in front of the client." Which reply reflects feelings?
- "Why didn't you check the system?" || This sounds like blame and does not acknowledge the feeling.
* "It sounds like you felt embarrassed and let down by the team." || Correct. It names the emotion behind the words.
- "The schedule is always in the shared folder." || This defends the process instead of listening.
- "Let's move on to the sales figures." || This ignores what the person has just said.
:::

## 4. Open questions and the question funnel

Open questions invite people to think and explain. They start with what, how, tell me or describe: "What is getting in your way this week?" "Tell me more about the delay." Closed questions such as "Is the report finished?" confirm facts, but a meeting made only of closed questions becomes an interrogation.

Use the question funnel: start wide with open questions, narrow down with probing questions, then confirm with closed questions. For example: "How are the new routes working?" → "What happens on the Cacuaco route in the afternoon?" → "So if we move that drop to the morning, will it solve the problem?" Be careful with "why": "Why did you do that?" can sound like blame, while "What led you to that decision?" asks the same thing safely.

::: quiz
? Which question is the best way to open a topic in a one-to-one?
- "Did the client meeting go well?" || A closed question invites only "yes" or "no".
- "Why was the client unhappy?" || "Why" can sound like blame, and it assumes the answer.
* "How did the client meeting go?" || Correct. An open question invites the person to explain in their own words.
:::

## 5. Documenting agreements

Spoken agreements are often remembered differently by each person. Keep one shared document per team member with the date, the main points and the actions. Every action needs what, who and by when. Include your own actions as manager, such as "Request the van budget from finance by Friday", because people notice when managers do not follow through. Start each one-to-one by reviewing the previous actions, a habit you will develop in lesson 6.

## Worked example: an operations supervisor in Viana

Helena supervises six warehouse team leaders at a distribution centre in Viana. She now holds a 30-minute weekly one-to-one with each leader. Paulo tells her the night shift leaves about twenty pallets in the loading area every night, and says, with some anger, that he is always the one complaining. Helena reflects: "It sounds like you feel alone with this." Paulo relaxes and explains that the night shift has one forklift fewer than planned.

::: case | Closing Helena's one-to-one with Paulo
The meeting has five minutes left. Helena and Paulo agree that repairing the second forklift should solve the pallet problem, and that Paulo will count the pallets left each morning to check.
? What should Helena do before the meeting ends?
- Trust that both of them will remember || Spoken agreements are easily forgotten or remembered differently.
- Send Paulo a long email next week || Too late, and it is not a shared record reviewed in the next meeting.
* Write both actions in the shared notes with an owner and date, and review them next week || Correct. Helena will ask maintenance to repair the forklift by Wednesday, and Paulo will count pallets for two weeks.
- Promise to fix the problem herself || This removes Paulo's ownership, and no measurable action is agreed.
:::

At the next one-to-one Helena opens with the actions. The forklift is back, and the count has fallen to three pallets.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- give positive and corrective feedback using the Situation-Behaviour-Impact (SBI) model;
- distinguish observable behaviour from judgements about personality;
- use feedforward to focus a conversation on future improvement;

## Key terms

::: terms
Feedback: information about a person's past actions and their effect, given so the person can keep or change them
Reinforcing feedback: feedback that recognises effective behaviour so it is repeated
Corrective feedback: feedback about behaviour that needs to change
Observable behaviour: what a person actually said or did, which others could also see or hear
Feedforward: suggestions focused on what someone can do better in the future, rather than on past mistakes
:::

## 1. Why feedback must be actionable

"Good job" and "You need to be more professional" are well meant, but neither tells the person what exactly to repeat or change. Feedback is only useful if the receiver can act on it: it must describe specific behaviour, explain why it matters, and come soon enough to be relevant.

Give reinforcing feedback as well as corrective feedback. When people only hear from their manager about problems, they start to hide them. Give feedback close to the event, ideally within a day or two, not saved for the annual review. Reinforcing feedback can be public if the person is comfortable with that; corrective feedback should be private, often in the one-to-one from lesson 3.

## 2. The SBI model

The Situation-Behaviour-Impact (SBI) model was developed by the Center for Creative Leadership. It gives feedback a simple three-part structure:

::: slides
SLIDE Situation | When and where it happened; "At yesterday's 9 o'clock meeting with the supermarket buyer..." | Anchor the feedback in a real moment
SLIDE Behaviour | What the person did or said, without interpretation; "...you presented the promotion plan with stock figures for each store..." | What a video camera would record
SLIDE Impact | The effect on you, the team, the client or results; "...and the buyer approved the order on the spot, which saved a second visit." | Link it to goals the person cares about
SLIDE Then ask | "How do you see it?"; Listen actively before agreeing a next step | SBI opens the conversation
:::

The hardest part is the behaviour. A judgement is a conclusion about the person: careless, lazy, unprofessional, excellent. Behaviour is what a camera would record. "You were careless with the invoices" invites an argument. "Three invoices this week had the wrong client code" invites a solution.

::: quiz
? Which statement describes behaviour rather than a judgement?
- "You have a bad attitude in meetings." || "Bad attitude" is a judgement about the person.
- "You are not a team player." || This labels the person and gives nothing specific to change.
* "You looked at your phone during most of the client's presentation." || Correct. A camera could record this.
- "You seemed uninterested in the client." || This interprets what the person felt, which you cannot observe.
:::

## 3. Steps for a feedback conversation

SBI opens the conversation; it does not finish it. Follow these steps, especially for corrective feedback:

::: diagram steps | The feedback conversation
Prepare: write your SBI statement and remove judgement words
Deliver: say it calmly and briefly
Ask: "How do you see it?" and listen – there may be a cause you do not know
Agree: what they will keep doing or change, and the support you will give
Follow up: notice and recognise the change when it happens
:::

The Center for Creative Leadership has extended the model to include exploring the person's intent at the "ask" stage. Avoid the "feedback sandwich", where criticism is hidden between two compliments: people learn to wait for the criticism, or miss it completely.

## 4. Feedforward and receiving feedback

Marshall Goldsmith, an executive coach, proposed feedforward as a complement to feedback. Feedback looks back at a past that cannot be changed and that people often defend. Feedforward asks for suggestions for the future: choose one behaviour to improve, ask a colleague for two ideas, listen without arguing, and thank them. After an SBI statement you can ask: "Next month's launch is similar. What would you do differently?" It turns a correction into a plan.

Managers must also receive feedback well, or their team will stop offering it. In Thanks for the Feedback (2014), Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen describe three triggers that block us: truth triggers (we think the feedback is wrong), relationship triggers (we react to who is giving it) and identity triggers (it threatens how we see ourselves). Respond in four steps: thank, clarify (ask for a specific example), consider (decide later what is true and useful) and respond (say what you will do).

::: quiz
? Your supervisor tells you your reports are hard to read. You think: "He writes worse reports than I do!" Which trigger is this?
- Truth trigger || A truth trigger is about whether the feedback itself is right.
* Relationship trigger || Correct. You are reacting to who is giving the feedback, not to its content.
- Identity trigger || An identity trigger threatens how you see yourself, for example "I am a bad manager".
- Feedforward trigger || This is not one of the three triggers described by Stone and Heen.
:::

## Worked example: a key account coordinator in Luanda

Sílvia manages four key account coordinators at an FMCG distributor in Luanda. Mário often sends order confirmations to a large supermarket chain late in the afternoon, and the buyer has complained twice. Instead of calling him "disorganised", Sílvia prepares SBI: "On Monday and Wednesday (situation), the confirmations for the supermarket chain were sent after 16:00 (behaviour). Their warehouse plans receiving by 14:00, so our trucks waited about two hours each time (impact)." Mário explains that he waits for a warehouse stock report that often arrives at 15:00.

::: case | Agreeing the next step with Mário
Sílvia now knows the cause: Mário waits for a late stock report. The client needs confirmations before 14:00, and Sílvia wants Mário to own the solution.
? What should Sílvia do next?
- Tell Mário to work harder to send confirmations on time || The cause is a process problem, not effort.
* Ask "What could we do so confirmations go out before 14:00?" and agree a trial || Correct. This is feedforward. Mário proposes confirming from the morning report, and Sílvia asks the warehouse to send it by 10:00.
- Move the account to another coordinator || This punishes Mário for a problem in the process.
- Repeat the feedback to make sure he understood || He has understood. The conversation should now move to the future.
:::

Two weeks later every confirmation goes out before 13:00, and Sílvia gives reinforcing SBI feedback.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain the difference between telling and coaching;
- run a structured coaching conversation using the GROW model;
- ask effective open questions at each stage of GROW;
- decide, using clear criteria, whether to tell or to coach in a given situation.

## Key terms

::: terms
Coaching: helping a person improve their performance by asking questions that develop their own thinking, rather than giving instructions
Telling (directive approach): giving the person the answer or instruction
GROW model: a four-stage coaching structure: Goal, Reality, Options, Will
Awareness: a clear understanding of the situation and of oneself
Responsibility: the person's sense of ownership of a choice and its results
:::

## 1. Why coach before you solve

A team member asks: "The client wants a 10% discount. What should I do?" The fastest response is to answer. But if you always answer, the person learns to bring you every problem, you become the bottleneck, and the team never develops its own judgement.

Sir John Whitmore, in Coaching for Performance (first published in 1992), argued that the purpose of coaching is to build awareness and responsibility. When people think a problem through themselves, they understand it better and own the solution they choose. Coaching is not abandoning people: you still share knowledge, but you ask first and advise only after the person has done their own thinking.

## 2. The GROW model

GROW is the most widely used structure for coaching conversations. Whitmore popularised it and credits its development to work with colleagues in the 1980s, including Graham Alexander and Alan Fine.

::: diagram cycle | The GROW model
Goal: agree what the person wants to achieve, now and longer term
Reality: explore the facts, what has been tried and what is in the way
Options: generate several possible actions without judging too early
Will: agree what they will do, by when, and how to overcome obstacles
:::

Follow the stages in order but stay flexible: if Reality shows the goal is wrong, go back and redefine it. Spend real time on Reality. Many problems become easy once the facts are clear, and managers who skip it solve the wrong problem. Some versions call the last stage "Way forward".

## 3. Questions for each stage

Coaching depends on good open questions, as practised in lesson 3:

::: slides
SLIDE Goal questions | "What do you want to achieve?"; "What would a good result look like?"; "What do you want from the next 20 minutes?" | Start with the destination
SLIDE Reality questions | "What is happening now?"; "What have you tried, and what happened?"; "Who else is involved?" | Facts before solutions
SLIDE Options questions | "What could you do?"; "And what else?"; "What would the most experienced person in the team do?" | The first idea is rarely the best
SLIDE Will questions | "Which option will you choose?"; "What is your first step, and by when?"; "What might stop you?" | End with a committed action
:::

Michael Bungay Stanier, in The Coaching Habit, calls "And what else?" the best coaching question, because the first idea a person offers is rarely their best one. Avoid leading questions such as "Have you thought about calling the client?", which are advice disguised as questions. If you have an idea, offer it openly near the end of Options ("Can I add one option?") and let the person decide.

::: quiz
? "What have you already tried, and what happened?" belongs to which GROW stage?
- Goal || Goal questions ask what the person wants to achieve.
* Reality || Correct. It explores the current situation and past attempts.
- Options || Options questions generate new possible actions.
- Will || Will questions secure commitment to a specific action and date.
:::

## 4. Deciding whether to tell or to coach

Coaching is not always right. Use four criteria:

- Urgency: in a crisis, such as a truck accident or a client threatening to cancel today, tell.
- Risk: when safety, legal compliance or large sums of money are involved and there is a clear right answer, tell.
- Competence: a person new to the task (D1 in lesson 1) has nothing yet to draw on. Teach first, then coach as competence grows.
- Recurrence: if the problem will come back and the person has some knowledge, coach.

You can also calculate the payback. A team member asks how to handle a type of customer query three times a week, and answering takes 10 minutes: 30 minutes of your time weekly. A 45-minute coaching conversation plus two 15-minute follow-ups costs 75 minutes. After about two and a half weeks the coaching has paid for itself, and the person now handles the queries alone.

::: quiz
? A forklift is leaking fuel in the warehouse and a new operator asks you what to do. What is the right approach?
- Coach with GROW, so he learns to solve it himself || There is urgency and a safety risk, and he is new to the task.
- Ask "And what else?" until he finds the answer || This delays action in a dangerous situation.
- Tell him to decide and report back later || He lacks the knowledge, and the risk is too high.
* Tell him clearly what to do now, then review it later || Correct. Urgency, safety risk and low competence all call for telling.
:::

## Worked example: a sales manager in Benguela

António manages six sales representatives at a food distributor's office in Benguela. Every week Rosa asks him how to respond when small shop owners request longer credit, and António always decides for her. The situation is not urgent, the credit policy is clear, Rosa has two years' experience and the issue recurs, so he coaches. Goal: Rosa wants to decide alone. Reality: most requests come from good customers with seasonal cash problems, and the policy allows up to 15 extra days for customers with no overdue invoices. Options: Rosa suggests 15 days for clean accounts, split deliveries and a small cash discount.

::: case | Moving Rosa to the Will stage
Rosa has listed three options. António has his own idea: checking each customer's payment history in the system before every visit. Ten minutes of the conversation remain.
? What should António do now?
- Choose the best option for Rosa to save time || This takes back ownership, and Rosa will keep asking him.
* Ask "Can I add one option?", share his idea, then ask Rosa which she will do and by when || Correct. He offers advice openly, last, and lets Rosa commit to her own action.
- End the meeting and let Rosa think about it || Without a Will stage there is no committed action or date.
- Ask "Have you thought about checking payment history?" || This is a leading question that disguises advice.
:::

Rosa decides to check payment history before visits, offer up to 15 days to clean accounts and escalate only the other cases. Over the next month she escalates two.

## Key terms

::: terms
Accountability: being answerable for a result, including explaining what happened if it was not achieved
RACI matrix: a table that shows, for each task, who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed
Action log: a shared list of agreed actions, with owners, due dates and status
Commitment: a specific promise to deliver something by a date
Performance gap: the difference between the expected performance and the actual performance

## 1. Clarifying roles with a RACI matrix

Many missed commitments come not from poor effort but from unclear roles: two people each think the other is doing a task, or nobody knows who decides. The RACI matrix is a widely used tool to prevent this. For each task it shows who is Responsible (does the work), Accountable (the one person who owns the result and signs off), Consulted (gives input before the work or decision) and Informed (is told the outcome). Build it in five steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: List the tasks | One row per task or decision; Example: monthly price list update | Be specific
SLIDE Step 2: List the roles | One column per role or person; Include other departments involved | Names, not "the team"
SLIDE Step 3: Fill the cells | R, A, C or I, or leave empty; The A and R can be the same person on a simple task | Every cell is a decision
SLIDE Step 4: Check the rules | Exactly one A per row; At least one R per row; Few Cs, because each consultation slows the work | One A, always
SLIDE Step 5: Agree and share | Review it with everyone involved; Correct it so it reflects reality | A RACI nobody agreed is just a drawing
:::

::: quiz
? In a RACI for "approve a new customer's credit limit", both the sales manager and the finance controller are marked A. What is the problem?
- There are too many Cs || The problem is in the A column, not the C column.
* Two people are accountable, so nobody clearly owns the decision || Correct. Each task needs exactly one A.
- Nobody is informed || Informed roles may be missing, but the main error is the double A.
- The finance controller should be R || That may be true, but it does not solve the double accountability.
:::

## 2. Tracking commitments with an action log

Accountability depends on follow-up. Keep one shared action log for the team with these columns: number, action, owner, date raised, due date, status (open, done, late) and notes. Write each action the moment it is agreed, using "what, who, by when" from lesson 3. Review the log at every team meeting and one-to-one, and close actions only when they are actually done. Measure reliability with a simple ratio:

On-time completion rate = actions completed by their due date ÷ actions that fell due in the period × 100

If 20 actions fell due in March and 17 were completed on time, the rate is 17 ÷ 20 × 100 = 85%. Track it monthly. A falling rate warns of overload, or of a team that has learned that deadlines are not followed up.

## 3. Handling a missed commitment

Respond early, calmly and consistently. Ignoring the first missed deadline teaches the whole team that deadlines are optional.

::: diagram steps | Handling a missed commitment
Check the facts: what was agreed, when, and what was delivered – use the action log
Talk privately and soon: use SBI from lesson 4
Find the cause: clarity, ability, resources or motivation
Agree a recovery plan: new date, interim measures and your support
Follow up: check on the new date and recognise success
:::

Match your response to the cause. Clarity and ability problems need a better brief or training (lesson 1). Resource problems, such as missing time, tools, information or authority, need you to remove the obstacle. Only motivation problems call for a firmer conversation about expectations.

::: quiz
? A coordinator misses a deadline because she still has no access to the sales system she needs. Which cause is this, and what should you do?
- Motivation, so set clear expectations firmly || She has not chosen to ignore the task. She is blocked.
- Ability, so send her on training || She has the skill. She lacks a tool.
* Resources, so arrange the system access quickly || Correct. Remove the obstacle, then agree a new date.
- Clarity, so rewrite the brief || She knows what is expected. The issue is access.
:::

## 4. The performance conversation

When problems continue despite these steps, hold a structured performance conversation:

- Prepare specific evidence from the action log, reports and SBI notes.
- State the purpose and the gap: what was expected and what happened, with examples.
- Listen and explore causes. Do not decide the cause before the meeting.
- Agree an improvement plan: one to three SMART goals (lesson 2) and the support you will give.
- Set review dates, for example every two weeks for two months.
- Write a short summary, share it with the person, and follow up on each date.

If performance still does not improve, the next steps are formal. Follow your company's disciplinary and performance procedures and involve HR early, so that any action respects Angolan labour law and is fair and consistent.
