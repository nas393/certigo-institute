## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain how a manager's role differs from that of an individual contributor;
- use the Situational Leadership model to decide how much direction and support to give a team member;
- delegate a task using a five-part delegation brief;
- choose an appropriate level of delegation for a given person and task.

## Key terms

- Individual contributor: someone whose performance is judged mainly on their own work output.
- Manager: someone whose performance is judged mainly on the results of the people they are responsible for.
- Competence: a person's knowledge and skill for a specific task.
- Commitment: a person's motivation and confidence to do that task well.
- Delegation: giving someone responsibility for a result, together with the authority and resources needed to deliver it.

## 1. From doer to manager

When you are promoted, the basis on which you are judged changes. As an individual contributor you were measured on your own output: the orders you processed, the clients you visited, the reports you wrote. As a manager you are measured on what your team delivers.

Linda Hill of Harvard Business School followed a group of new managers through their first year (published as Becoming a Manager). Many expected the job to be mainly about authority and control; in practice it was about building the team's capability and managing many relationships at once. A recurring difficulty was letting go of the hands-on work that had earned them the promotion. Stepping in to do the work yourself whenever results are at risk feels productive, but it overloads you and stops the team from learning.

A useful test: if you were away for two weeks, what would stop? Every item on that list is something you have not yet built the team to handle.

## 2. Situational Leadership: one style does not fit all

The Situational Leadership model, developed by Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard, starts from a simple idea: there is no single best management style. The right style depends on how ready the person is for the specific task in front of them. Readiness has two parts, competence and commitment, and the model describes four development levels:

- D1 – low competence, high commitment. Typical of someone new to a task and keen to learn. They need clear instructions: the Directing style (S1).
- D2 – some competence, low commitment. The task has turned out harder than expected and enthusiasm has dropped. They need direction and encouragement: the Coaching style (S2).
- D3 – good competence, variable commitment. They can do the task but may lack confidence or motivation. They need support and listening more than instructions: the Supporting style (S3).
- D4 – high competence, high commitment. They can do the task well and want to. They need trust and space: the Delegating style (S4).

Two points are often misunderstood. First, development level applies to a task, not to a person: an experienced accountant may be D4 on month-end closing and D1 on a new reporting system. Second, the goal is to move people up the levels over time, so the style you use should change as they grow.

## 3. The five-part delegation brief

Most delegation failures come from something that was never said. A short, explicit brief prevents them. Before handing over a task, agree five things:

- Outcome and quality standard: what "done" looks like. Show an example if possible.
- Deadline and milestones: the final date and any intermediate points.
- Decision authority: what the person can decide alone and what needs your input (see section 4).
- Resources and people: budget, systems, data and the colleagues they may involve.
- Check-in points: when you will review progress together. Agreeing these in advance makes check-ins feel like support rather than suspicion.

## 4. Choosing the level of delegation

Delegation is not all-or-nothing. Jurgen Appelo's "seven levels of delegation" (from the Management 3.0 approach) give a precise vocabulary:

- 1 Tell: you decide and announce the decision.
- 2 Sell: you decide and explain why.
- 3 Consult: you ask for input, then decide.
- 4 Agree: you decide together.
- 5 Advise: they decide, after hearing your advice.
- 6 Inquire: they decide, and tell you afterwards.
- 7 Delegate: they decide; you do not need to be informed.

Match the level to the development level from section 2: a D1 person might work at level 3 or 4 on a task, a D4 person at level 6 or 7. Say the level out loud, for example: "On supplier choice, this is a level 5: you decide, but talk to me first."

## Worked example: a sales supervisor in Luanda

Marta has just been promoted to supervise five sales representatives at a food distributor in Luanda. In her first month she is still visiting her old key accounts herself, preparing the weekly sales report and approving every discount request. She works until 9 p.m. and the team waits for her decisions.

She lists what would stop if she were away. Three items stand out: the weekly report, discount approvals and visits to two key accounts.

- Weekly report → Pedro. He is good with Excel but has never prepared the report (D1). Marta uses a Directing style: she shows him two past reports, explains who reads them, and writes a brief: one page by Thursday 17:00, stock-outs highlighted; he may fix obvious data errors himself (level 6) but must flag differences above 5% (level 5); they review the first two reports together.
- Discount approvals → Joana, a senior rep who knows margins well (D4). Marta agrees a level 6 for discounts up to 3%: Joana decides and records it in the CRM; above 3% stays with Marta.
- Key accounts → Ana, who handled them before but lost confidence after a difficult negotiation (D3). Marta uses a Supporting style: she asks Ana how she wants to approach the next visit, listens, and offers to join the first meeting only as an observer.

Within six weeks Marta has recovered about eight hours a week, and each of the three team members has a clear area of ownership.

## Common mistakes

- Using one style for everyone. Over-directing a D4 person feels like micromanagement; leaving a D1 person alone feels like abandonment.
- Taking the task back at the first problem. Help solve the problem, but leave ownership with the person.
- Giving responsibility without authority. Asking for a result without the decision rights or information to achieve it.
- Skipping the check-ins, then discovering a problem at the deadline.

## Self-check

- A new team member is excited but has never used the ordering system. Which development level and style apply? (Answer: D1, Directing.)
- What are the five parts of a delegation brief? (Answer: outcome and standard, deadline and milestones, decision authority, resources and people, check-in points.)
- At delegation level 5, who decides? (Answer: the team member, after hearing your advice.)

## Practical assignment

List everything that would stop if you were away for two weeks. Choose one item, assess the development level of the person you would give it to, and complete a delegation brief using the template above. Hold the delegation conversation this week.

## Summary

- As a manager, your output is your team's results; managing by doing is the most common trap.
- Diagnose competence and commitment for each task, then choose Directing, Coaching, Supporting or Delegating.
- Use a five-part brief and name the delegation level, so both sides know exactly who decides what.

A team can work very hard and still achieve little if it is working on the wrong things. One of a manager's most important jobs is to take broad direction from above, such as "grow sales" or "improve customer satisfaction", and turn it into a small number of clear goals that the team can act on every day.

## Why fewer goals work better

Most teams can genuinely focus on three to five goals at a time. Beyond that, goals start competing for the same time and attention, and people quietly decide for themselves which ones matter. The result is effort spread thinly across many things and few of them finished.

A useful exercise is to ask: if we could only achieve three things this quarter, which three would make the biggest difference? The answer is usually clearer than you expect. Everything else is not forgotten, but it becomes second priority, and the team knows that when there is a conflict, the top goals win.

## Writing goals that can be measured

A useful goal says what will change, by how much, and by when. Compare these:

- "Improve customer service." This is a wish. Nobody can tell whether it has been achieved.
- "Answer 90% of customer emails within 24 hours by the end of March." This is a goal. Everyone knows what success looks like and can see progress each week.

When you write a goal, check that it describes an outcome rather than an activity. "Make 50 calls a week" is an activity; "sign five new accounts this quarter" is an outcome. Activities can be useful as part of the plan to reach a goal, but the goal itself should describe the result you want. Otherwise people can complete every activity and still miss the point.

Also check that the goal is realistic. A goal that the team believes is impossible does not motivate; it gets ignored. Stretching is good, but people need to believe they have a real chance of success.

## One owner for every goal

Each goal needs one named owner. The owner is not necessarily the person who does all the work; they are the person who tracks progress, raises problems early and answers for the result. When a goal is "owned by the team", it is often owned by nobody: everyone assumes someone else is watching it.

Write the owner's name next to every goal and review progress with that person regularly.

## Connecting goals to the bigger picture

People commit more to goals when they understand why they matter. Explain how the team's goals support the company's targets: for example, how reducing overdue invoices improves cash flow, which allows the company to buy stock and serve customers. Then help each person see how their own work contributes.

If you cannot explain why a goal matters, that is a warning sign. It may be a goal inherited from the past that nobody has questioned, and it may be worth dropping.

## Saying no and making trade-offs

Setting priorities means deciding what you will not do, at least for now. This is where many managers struggle, because new requests arrive constantly from customers, colleagues and senior managers.

When a new request arrives, compare it honestly with your current goals:

- If it matters more than something already planned, accept it and agree openly what will be delayed or dropped. Tell the people affected.
- If it does not matter more, explain why it will have to wait, and when you could look at it.

Saying "yes" to everything is not helpful; it simply means that everything is done late or badly. A clear, polite "not now, and here's why" is far more professional.

## Keeping goals visible

Goals that are written once and filed away have little effect. Keep them visible: on a shared board, at the start of team meetings, in one-to-ones. A short weekly check, where each owner says whether their goal is on track, at risk or off track, keeps everyone focused and surfaces problems while there is still time to act.

## A worked example

A sales team in a distribution company had eleven different targets, set over the years by different managers: new accounts, repeat orders, visit numbers, overdue invoices, product launches, report deadlines and more. Weekly meetings took ninety minutes and nobody was sure which targets really mattered.

The new manager reviewed them with her director and agreed four goals for the quarter: sign 12 new retail accounts, raise repeat orders from existing accounts by 10%, reduce invoices overdue by more than 60 days to below 5% of sales, and submit visit reports within 48 hours. She wrote each one with a number and a deadline, named an owner for each, and explained how each linked to the company's annual plan.

Weekly meetings shrank to thirty minutes: each owner reported on track, at risk or off track, and the team spent the time on the goals at risk. By the end of the quarter, three of the four goals were met, and the team understood clearly why the fourth had slipped.

## Common mistakes

- Writing activities instead of outcomes, so people stay busy without achieving the result.
- Changing priorities every week, so nothing is ever finished and people stop taking goals seriously.
- Setting goals without checking that the team has the time, skills and resources to reach them.
- Having goals without owners, or with several owners, so nobody feels responsible.

## Try it

Take one vague goal your team has today. Rewrite it so that it states a measurable result, a deadline and a single owner. Then check: does it describe an outcome or an activity? Is it realistic? Can you explain in one sentence why it matters?

A one-to-one, usually written 1:1, is a regular private meeting between a manager and one team member. It is one of the simplest and most powerful management tools available. Done well, it is where problems surface early, where trust is built and where people grow. Done badly, it becomes a weekly status report that could have been an email, and both people quietly dread it.

## Why 1:1s matter

Many issues never come up in team meetings. People are reluctant to raise concerns about workload, conflicts with colleagues, mistakes they are worried about or doubts about their career in front of others. A private, regular conversation with their manager gives them a safe space to do so. Managers who hold good 1:1s usually find out about problems weeks earlier than those who do not, when they are still small and easy to fix.

1:1s also build the relationship. When people know they will have your full attention regularly, they trust you more and are more open with you.

## It is their meeting

The most important principle is that the 1:1 belongs mainly to the team member. Their topics come first: what is on their mind, what is blocking them, how they are feeling about their work. Your role is mostly to listen, ask good questions and help.

This means 1:1s should not be used for routine task updates. Status information, such as which orders have shipped or how far a project has progressed, belongs in team meetings or shared trackers where everyone can see it. If your 1:1s are filled with status updates, there is no room left for the conversations that matter.

## A simple structure

A light structure helps both people prepare and keeps the meeting useful:

- Their topics: "What's on your mind this week?" Let them lead.
- Obstacles: "What's slowing you down, and what can I do about it?" This is where you can often add the most value by removing blockers.
- Development: "What do you want to get better at, and what would help?" Not every week, but regularly.
- Agreements: "What will each of us do before next time?" End with clear actions on both sides.

Many managers keep a shared document for each person, where the team member adds topics before the meeting and both of you record agreements afterwards. This builds a useful history over time and makes follow-up easy.

## Rhythm and reliability

Hold 1:1s at a regular rhythm, typically weekly or every two weeks, for thirty minutes or so. Choose a fixed time so they become routine.

Protect them. Cancelling 1:1s repeatedly sends a clear message: you are not a priority. If you genuinely must move one, reschedule it immediately rather than skipping it and waiting for the next slot. Being reliable about something as simple as a recurring meeting is one of the easiest ways to build trust.

## Listening well

Good 1:1s depend on good listening. A few habits help:

- Ask open questions that cannot be answered with yes or no. "How is the new process working for you?" invites a real answer; "Is everything fine?" invites "yes".
- Let short silences happen. People often say the most important thing after a pause, once they have decided it is safe to say it. Resist the urge to fill every silence.
- Ask follow-up questions: "Tell me more about that", "What makes you say that?"
- Do not rush to solve. Sometimes people want to think aloud or be heard before they want advice. You can ask: "Would it help if I suggested something, or do you want to think it through?"
- Take brief notes of agreements so you can follow up next time.

## When the conversation gets difficult

Sometimes a 1:1 brings up something hard: a personal problem, a complaint about a colleague, frustration with you. Stay calm, thank them for raising it and focus on understanding before reacting. You do not have to solve everything on the spot. It is fine to say: "Thank you for telling me. Let me think about this and come back to you by Thursday." Then make sure you do.

If someone shares something personal, respect their privacy and only share it with others if they agree or if there is a genuine safety or legal reason.

## A worked example

A warehouse team leader started holding 1:1s every two weeks with each of her eight team members. She kept a simple shared document for each person with three headings: your topics, actions agreed and development goals. Before each meeting, team members added the topics they wanted to discuss.

In the first month, two people separately raised concerns about forklifts being driven too fast in a busy loading area, something nobody had ever mentioned in group meetings. She raised it with the operations manager, and a marked pedestrian zone was introduced. Another team member said he wanted to learn stock control; she arranged for him to shadow the inventory clerk. Within three months, the team said in a survey that they felt much more listened to.

## Common mistakes

- Turning the 1:1 into a list of your instructions and updates.
- Only holding 1:1s when something has gone wrong, so they feel like a warning.
- Forgetting what was agreed last time, which tells people their concerns do not matter.
- Doing most of the talking.

## Try it

Prepare three open questions you could ask in your next 1:1 that are not about task status. For example: "What's been the most frustrating part of your week?", "What would make your work easier?", "What do you want to learn this year?" Use at least one of them in your next 1:1.

Feedback is information about the effect of someone's behaviour, given so that they can keep doing what works and change what does not. It is one of the most powerful tools a manager has, and one of the most avoided. Many managers either give no feedback until a problem becomes serious, or give feedback so vague that nobody knows what to do with it. Good feedback is specific, timely and focused on behaviour rather than personality.

## Why behaviour, not personality

Compare two comments. "You're rude in meetings." And: "In this morning's client meeting, you interrupted the client twice while they were explaining the problem." The first is a judgement about the person. It makes people defensive and gives them nothing concrete to change. The second describes something that actually happened, which the person can recognise and do differently next time.

Labels such as "lazy", "careless", "negative" or "difficult" almost always make feedback less effective. Describe what you saw and heard instead.

## Situation, behaviour, impact

A simple and reliable pattern for feedback is situation, behaviour, impact:

- Situation: when and where it happened. "In this morning's client meeting…"
- Behaviour: what the person actually did, as factually as possible. "…you interrupted the client twice while they were explaining the problem."
- Impact: the effect it had. "They stopped giving details, and we left without the information we needed to prepare the quote."

The same pattern works for positive feedback: "In yesterday's stock count, you double-checked every location with a discrepancy before reporting it. Because of that, the final figures were accurate and we avoided an unnecessary recount."

## Making it a two-way conversation

After describing the situation, behaviour and impact, ask for the other person's view: "How did you see it?" or "What was going on for you?" There may be context you do not know about: unclear instructions, a problem at home, a system that did not work. Listening first makes the conversation fairer and often reveals the real cause.

Then agree together what will be different next time. People are much more likely to follow through on a solution they helped create.

## Timing and balance

Feedback is most useful soon after the event, while details are fresh for both of you. Waiting weeks, or saving it all for an annual review, means the person cannot connect it to what they did, and may have repeated the same mistake many times in the meantime.

Give corrective feedback in private. Criticising someone in front of colleagues damages trust and usually makes them focus on their embarrassment rather than the lesson. Positive feedback can often be given in public, though some people prefer it privately too.

Do not underestimate positive feedback. When you tell people specifically what they did well and why it mattered, they are much more likely to repeat it. Generic praise such as "good job" is pleasant but teaches little; specific praise shows exactly what to keep doing.

Make feedback a regular habit rather than an occasional event. When feedback is frequent and normal, each individual conversation feels less threatening.

## Avoiding the "sandwich"

Some managers hide corrective feedback between two compliments, hoping to soften it. In practice, the real message often gets lost, and people learn to distrust any praise because they wait for the "but". It is better to be clear, respectful and direct about the issue, and to give positive feedback separately when it is genuinely deserved.

## Receiving feedback yourself

Ask your team for feedback on your own management, for example in 1:1s: "What's one thing I could do differently that would help you?" When you receive feedback, thank the person, ask questions to understand it, and avoid defending yourself on the spot. Then show what you changed. People give honest feedback to managers who visibly use it, and they learn from your example how to receive feedback well.

## A worked example

A finance supervisor noticed that one colleague's month-end bank reconciliations were accurate but consistently late. The easy reaction would have been "you're too slow". Instead, she arranged a short private conversation.

She said: "The last two reconciliations arrived on the 6th instead of the 3rd. Because of that, the management report was delayed and the director had to present without final cash figures. What's getting in the way?" The colleague explained that he was waiting for bank statements, which only arrived when requested, and he usually requested them on the 1st. They agreed he would request them on the last working day of each month. The next reconciliation arrived on the 2nd, and she made a point of thanking him specifically for it.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling the person instead of describing what they did.
- Waiting for the annual review instead of giving feedback when it happens.
- Hiding the real message between compliments.
- Giving feedback in front of others when it is corrective.
- Not listening to the other person's side.

## Try it

Write one piece of positive feedback and one piece of corrective feedback for someone you work with, using situation, behaviour and impact. For the corrective one, add an open question you will ask to hear their view. Deliver the positive one today.

BODY
When a team member brings you a problem, the quickest response is usually to tell them the answer. You probably know it, and it takes thirty seconds. But if you do this every time, your team learns to bring you every problem, and you become the bottleneck for every decision. Coaching means helping people find their own solutions, so that they grow and so that the team can solve problems without you.

## The cost of always telling

Telling has real advantages: it is fast and it usually produces a decent answer. But it has hidden costs. People do not develop their own judgement, because they never practise it. They feel less ownership of solutions that were not theirs. And as the team grows, you simply do not have enough hours to answer every question. Many managers who feel overwhelmed are, without realising it, the main decision-maker for dozens of small issues that their team could handle.

Coaching takes a little more time at first and saves a great deal of time later.

## When to coach and when to tell

Coaching is not always the right choice. Tell people directly when:

- there is a safety risk, and there is no time for discussion;
- there is an urgent deadline and a wrong decision would be costly;
- the person genuinely does not have the information or experience to work it out.

Coach when the person has the knowledge or experience to find a good answer and there is time to think it through. In practice, most everyday problems fall into this category.

## Questions that help

Coaching is mostly about asking good questions and listening to the answers. A simple sequence works for most situations:

- What is the situation, and what have you tried so far? This helps them describe the problem clearly, which often reveals the answer.
- What outcome do you want? This focuses on the goal rather than the obstacle.
- What options do you see? Encourage several options, not just one.
- What are the risks or downsides of each option? This builds judgement.
- What will you do first, and when? This turns thinking into action.

Ask one question at a time and give the person time to think. Silence is fine: it usually means they are thinking. Resist the urge to hint at your preferred answer with questions such as "Don't you think it would be better to…?" That is telling in disguise, and people notice.

## Ownership stays with them

The aim of a coaching conversation is for the person to leave owning both the decision and the next step. If their plan is reasonable but different from what you would do, let them try it. They will learn more from their own approach, it may work better than you expect, and you may learn something too.

Step in only if the risk is genuinely too high, for example if a mistake would harm a customer relationship, break a rule or cost a lot of money. Even then, explain your concern and ask them to adjust the plan rather than simply replacing it with yours.

At the end of the conversation, avoid the very common phrase "leave it with me". It feels helpful, but it takes ownership straight back. Instead, ask: "So what's your next step?" and agree when you will check in.

## Coaching in everyday moments

Coaching does not need a formal meeting. Many of the best coaching moments are short conversations at a desk or on the phone, when someone asks, "What should I do about this?" Simply replying "What do you think the options are?" before giving your view starts building the habit. Over time, people start coming to you with problems and a proposed solution, which is exactly what you want.

## A worked example

A new customer service agent came to her manager with a problem: a long-standing customer was demanding a refund for a product bought outside the return period, and was threatening to move their business elsewhere.

The manager could have answered immediately. Instead he asked: "What does our policy say?" She explained that refunds were only possible within 30 days. "What does the customer really need?" She thought about it: the product had developed a fault, and the customer needed a working product, not necessarily money. "What options do you have?" She listed: refuse, escalate, offer a repair, offer a replacement. "What are the risks of each?" Refusing risked losing the customer; a replacement cost less than losing a regular account. "What will you do?" She proposed offering a replacement as a goodwill gesture, which the manager approved. The customer accepted.

Two weeks later, a similar case came in. She handled it alone and simply informed her manager afterwards.

## Common mistakes

- Asking questions while clearly steering towards your own answer.
- Coaching during a crisis when the person needs a clear instruction.
- Taking the problem back at the end with "leave it with me".
- Asking too many questions at once, which overwhelms rather than helps.

## Try it

The next time someone brings you a problem, ask at least three of the five questions above before offering any suggestion of your own. Afterwards, note what happened: did they find a workable solution? How long did it take compared with telling them?

BODY
Agreeing goals and actions is only half of a manager's job. The other half is making sure that what was agreed actually happens. Accountability means that people do what they committed to, and that when they do not, the reasons are discussed openly and fairly. It is not about pressure, blame or threats. In healthy teams, accountability is built through clear agreements and consistent, predictable follow-up.

## Why accountability breaks down

When commitments are regularly missed, the cause is rarely laziness. More often it is one of these:

- The agreement was never clear. Nobody wrote down who would do what by when, so everyone remembers it differently.
- Nobody followed up. People learn quickly that deadlines are optional if nobody checks.
- Follow-up was inconsistent. If the manager checks some people's work and not others', follow-up feels personal and unfair.
- The obstacles were real. The person hit a problem they could not solve alone and did not feel able to say so.

Each of these can be fixed with simple habits.

## Making agreements clear

Every action agreed in a meeting or conversation needs three things: an owner, a deliverable and a date. "Let's look into the delivery problem" is not an action. "Paulo will send a list of the five most delayed routes, with causes, by Wednesday" is.

A simple habit makes a big difference: at the end of every meeting, read back each action with its owner and date, and ask: "Is that right? Is that realistic?" This catches misunderstandings while everyone is still in the room. Then record the actions in one shared place, such as a tracker, a shared document or a board, where everyone can see them.

## Following up consistently

Check progress at the points you agreed, not randomly. Many teams use a short routine, such as reviewing the action list for five minutes at the start of each weekly meeting. Each owner simply says whether their action is done, late or blocked. Blocked items get help.

Because everyone goes through the same routine, follow-up stops feeling personal. Nobody is singled out; it is simply how the team works. And because it happens every week, small delays are spotted before they become big problems.

If you forget to follow up, people notice. They conclude that the deadline did not really matter, and next time they will treat it that way.

## When commitments are missed

When something is late, start with curiosity, not blame: "The report was due Tuesday and I haven't seen it. What happened?" There may be a genuine obstacle: missing information, a competing urgent request, a system problem. If so, help remove it and agree a new date, and what will be different this time.

If the same person misses commitments repeatedly, it becomes a performance issue, and it needs a more serious conversation. Describe the pattern factually ("this is the fourth deadline missed in two months"), explain the impact on the team and the business, listen to their view, and agree clearly what must change and by when. Record the agreement. Being calm and specific is far more effective than being angry.

Ignoring repeated misses is unfair to the rest of the team, who see that some people are held to commitments and others are not.

## Leading by example

Teams copy their manager. If you arrive late to meetings, forget your own actions or quietly let your deadlines slip, your team will do the same, whatever you say. If you consistently meet your commitments, and when you miss one you say so openly and explain how you will fix it, people are far more likely to behave the same way.

Accountability also applies upwards. If you promised your team you would resolve something with another department, report back on progress, even when the news is not good.

## A worked example

A logistics manager noticed that many actions agreed in weekly meetings were never completed, and the same problems came up again and again. There was no written list; people relied on memory.

He introduced two changes. First, at the end of each meeting, he read back every action with an owner and a date and put them in a shared spreadsheet. Second, each meeting started with a five-minute review of the previous week's list: each owner said done, late or blocked, and blocked items were discussed immediately.

In the first few weeks, several actions were reported late, and he simply asked what had got in the way and agreed new dates. Within a month, most actions were completed on time. Team members said the meetings felt more useful and fairer, because everyone followed the same routine and problems were no longer forgotten.

## Common mistakes

- Agreeing actions without owners or dates.
- Following up only with the people you are unhappy with.
- Ignoring missed commitments for months, then reacting angrily.
- Not keeping your own commitments as a manager.

## Try it

At your next team meeting, end by reading back every action with its owner and date, and put them all in one shared list. At the following meeting, start by reviewing that list for five minutes.
