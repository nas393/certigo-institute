## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain the three flows that run through every supply chain;
- map an importer-distributor's supply chain using the SCOR process areas;
- recognise the bullwhip effect and name its four main causes;
- describe the trade-off between service level, cost and inventory.

## Key terms

::: terms
Supply chain: the network of organisations, activities and resources that moves a product from raw material to the final customer
Supply chain management: planning and coordinating that network so the right product reaches the right place at the right time and cost
Lead time: the time between placing an order and having the goods available for use or sale
Upstream and downstream: upstream means towards suppliers, downstream means towards customers
Stock point: a place in the chain where inventory is held, such as a port warehouse, central warehouse or regional depot
:::

## 1. Three flows, not one

Many people think supply chain means trucks and warehouses. That is only one of three flows, and problems usually start in the other two.

- Product flow: goods move downstream from the producer, through the port, the importer's warehouse, depots and wholesalers, to the shop shelf.
- Information flow: orders, forecasts, stock levels and shipping documents. It runs in both directions. A planned supermarket promotion should reach the buyer weeks before the order arrives.
- Cash flow: customers pay distributors, distributors pay importers or suppliers. In an import business, cash often leaves months before it returns.

For any problem, ask "Which flow broke first?" A stock-out of cooking oil may look like a transport issue, but the root cause is often late information or blocked cash.

## 2. The SCOR model: a common language

The Supply Chain Operations Reference (SCOR) model was developed by the Supply Chain Council and is now maintained by ASCM (the Association for Supply Chain Management). Its classic version groups supply chain work into six process areas. Recent versions rename and expand some, but the classic names are still widely used.

::: diagram steps | The classic SCOR process areas
Plan: balance demand and supply, set stock targets, decide what to buy and when
Source: select suppliers, place purchase orders, receive and pay
Make: transform the product, which for a distributor means repacking, labelling or building promotional packs
Deliver: manage customer orders, warehousing and transport to the customer
Return: handle damaged, expired or wrong goods coming back, and returns to suppliers
Enable: support processes such as data, contracts, compliance, performance measurement and risk management
:::

How to map your own chain with SCOR, step by step:

SLIDE Step 5: Add up lead times | The total shows how far ahead you must plan; Mark the stage with the widest range | Your planning horizon

## 3. The bullwhip effect

The bullwhip effect describes how small changes in consumer demand become larger and larger swings in orders as you move upstream. The term was popularised by Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (1997), who identified four main causes:

- Demand forecast updating: each link forecasts from the orders it receives, not from real consumer demand, and adds its own safety margin.
- Order batching: customers order once a month or by the full truck, so the supplier sees lumpy demand.
- Price fluctuations: promotions and expected price rises make buyers stock up, then stop ordering.
- Rationing and shortage gaming: when supply is short, customers order more than they need to get a bigger share, then cancel later.

Counter-measures: share sell-out data with suppliers, order smaller quantities more often, keep prices stable where possible, and allocate scarce stock on past sales rather than on the size of the latest order.

::: quiz
? Sugar is scarce and a wholesaler suddenly doubles its usual order. What is the best response?
- Ship the full order to keep the customer happy || This rewards shortage gaming and starves other customers.
* Allocate stock based on the customer's past sales || Allocating on history removes the incentive to over-order.
- Raise the next purchase order by the same amount || The spike may not be real demand, so this feeds the bullwhip.
:::

## 4. The service, cost and inventory triangle

Every supply chain decision trades three things against each other. More stock raises service but ties up cash and space. The cheapest shipping route may lengthen lead time, which needs more stock. Daily delivery raises service but increases transport cost. Make the trade-off visible and agree it with sales and finance.

## Worked example: mapping sugar at a Luanda distributor

A food importer-distributor in Luanda sells imported white sugar in 50 kg sacks and 1 kg packs. Sales complain about frequent stock-outs, finance complains about too much cash in stock, and the warehouse is full.

The analyst's map shows: supplier production and loading, about 2 weeks; ocean transit, about 5 weeks; port discharge and customs clearance at Luanda, 1 to 3 weeks; transfer to the central warehouse, 2 days; repacking into 1 kg packs (Make), 3 days; delivery to depots in Benguela and Huambo, 4 to 6 days. Total lead time from purchase order to depot shelf: roughly 9 to 12 weeks.

Two findings stand out. The buyer orders from last month's depot orders, not actual sales to shops, so one large wholesaler order triggered an oversized purchase (forecast updating). And sales ran a promotion without telling planning, emptying the depots before the next ship.

::: case | Fixing the sugar chain in Luanda
The analyst, Helena, must propose one first action to management, based on these two findings.
? What should Helena propose first?
- Order an extra vessel to rebuild stock || This adds cash and warehouse pressure without fixing the cause of the swings.
* Send weekly sell-out data from the top 20 customers to planning, and enter promotions 12 weeks ahead || This fixes the broken information flow behind both findings.
- Switch to a cheaper supplier || Price is not the problem here, and a new supplier adds risk.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Treating supply chain as only transport and warehousing, and ignoring planning and sourcing.
- Optimising one department, for example buying full vessels to cut freight cost, while the chain pays in excess stock.

## Key terms

::: terms
Demand forecast: an estimate of how much customers will buy in a future period
Forecast error: the difference between actual demand and the forecast for the same period
MAPE (mean absolute percentage error): the average size of forecast errors, as a percentage of actual demand
Bias: a tendency to forecast consistently too high or too low
Planning horizon: how far ahead the plan looks, at least as long as the total lead time
S&OP (sales and operations planning): a monthly process that agrees one plan linking sales, supply and finance
:::

## 1. Start with real demand

A forecast is only as good as the history behind it. Clean the data in four steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Use real demand | Use sales or shipments to customers; Do not use orders you placed on suppliers | Start from the customer
SLIDE Step 2: Add back lost sales | Stock-outs make recorded sales too low; Estimate missing days from the average of days with stock | Do not let stock-outs shrink the forecast
SLIDE Step 3: Separate one-offs | Record promotions and tenders apart from base demand; Stops next year's forecast being inflated | Base demand plus events
SLIDE Step 4: Check seasonality | Many food products peak in December; Patterns change around public holidays | Know your calendar

## 2. Simple forecasting methods

Three simple methods cover most needs in a distribution business.

- Naive forecast: next month equals last month. Useful as a baseline to beat.
- Moving average: the average of the last n periods. A 3-month moving average reacts quickly to change. A 6-month average is smoother but slower to react.
- Seasonal index: if December sales are normally 1.4 times an average month, multiply the base forecast by 1.4 for December. Calculate the index from at least two years of history.

Then apply judgement for known events: a new customer, a lost listing, a planned promotion, or a competitor's stock-out. Record every adjustment and the reason, so you can learn later whether the judgement helped.

Example: a distributor sold 2,800, 3,100 and 3,000 cases of tomato paste in the last three months. The 3-month moving average is (2,800 + 3,100 + 3,000) / 3 = 2,967 cases. Sales confirm a promotion with a supermarket chain next month expected to add 400 cases, so the forecast is 3,367 cases, recorded as 2,967 base plus 400 promotion.

::: quiz
? Sales of a product were 900, 1,200 and 1,500 cartons in the last three months. What is the 3-month moving average?
- 1,500 cartons || That is the naive forecast, using only last month.
* 1,200 cartons || (900 + 1,200 + 1,500) / 3 = 1,200.
- 3,600 cartons || That is the total, not the average.
:::

## 3. Measuring forecast accuracy and bias

If you do not measure the forecast, it will not improve. Measure monthly, per product, using two indicators.

- Absolute percentage error for one month = the difference between actual and forecast, ignoring the sign, / actual x 100.
- MAPE = the average of the absolute percentage errors over several months. Lower is better.
- Bias = the sum of (forecast - actual) over several months. A large positive total means you forecast too high, which builds excess stock. A large negative total means you forecast too low, which causes stock-outs.

Example over three months: forecasts 1,000, 1,200 and 1,100; actuals 900, 1,000 and 1,000. Absolute percentage errors are 11.1%, 20.0% and 10.0%, so MAPE is about 13.7%. Bias is (100 + 200 + 100) = +400 units: the forecast is consistently too high. Accuracy alone would not show this pattern.

::: quiz
? A planner's forecasts are below actual demand every month for six months. What does this show?
- High MAPE only || MAPE shows error size, not direction.
- Good accuracy || Consistent misses in one direction are a problem, not accuracy.
* Negative bias, which will cause stock-outs || Forecasting too low every month is negative bias and leads to shortages.
:::

## 4. From forecast to supply plan

For an importer, the supply plan answers: how much must we order now to cover demand until the next shipment can arrive? A simple net requirement calculation is:

- Net requirement = forecast demand over the lead time and review period + safety stock - stock on hand - stock already on order.

Then round the result to the supplier's order multiple, for example full containers or full pallets. Safety stock is covered in detail in lesson 4.

## 5. The S&OP cycle

Sales and operations planning brings sales, supply chain and finance together every month to agree one plan. A common five-step monthly cycle is:

::: diagram cycle | The monthly S&OP cycle
Data gathering: update sales history, stock and open orders
Demand review: sales and marketing agree the unconstrained forecast, including promotions
Supply review: check suppliers, port, warehouse and transport capacity, and flag gaps
Pre-S&OP: resolve gaps and prepare options, such as limiting a promotion
Executive S&OP: senior management chooses options and approves one plan with its financial impact
:::

## Worked example: cooking oil planning in Luanda

A distributor imports 20-litre and 1-litre cooking oil. Total lead time from order to warehouse is 10 weeks, and the buyer orders every 4 weeks, so each order must cover 14 weeks of demand.

The planner cleans the history. In August the product was out of stock for 9 working days out of 22. Recorded sales of 1-litre oil were 13,000 cartons. Average daily sales on the 13 days with stock were 1,000 cartons, so true August demand was about 22 x 1,000 = 22,000 cartons, not 13,000.

The 3-month moving average after correction is 21,000 cartons per month, about 4,850 per week. Demand for 14 weeks is about 67,900 cartons. Safety stock is set at 8,000 cartons. Stock on hand is 15,000 and 30,000 cartons are already on the water. Net requirement = 67,900 + 8,000 - 15,000 - 30,000 = 30,900 cartons. Rounded up to full containers of 1,800 cartons each, that is 18 containers (32,400 cartons).

::: case | The 18-container decision
At the executive S&OP meeting, the finance manager, Rui, says cash is tight this month and asks whether all 18 containers are needed now. The planner knows demand is uncertain for the last few weeks of the horizon.
? What is the best decision?
- Order all 18 now to protect service || Possible, but it ignores a real cash limit when part of the need can wait.
- Order only 10 to save cash || This falls well below the net requirement and risks another stock-out.
* Order 16 now and confirm the last 2 after the next demand review || This covers most of the need and delays only the least certain part.
- Postpone the order until next month || With a 10-week lead time, delay means certain stock-outs.
:::

## 2. The Kraljic matrix

Peter Kraljic (1983) proposed classifying purchases on two dimensions: the profit impact of the item (how much you spend on it and how much it affects your results) and supply risk (how hard it is to secure supply). This gives four quadrants, each with a different strategy.

::: diagram matrix | Kraljic matrix | Supply risk (low → high) | Profit impact (low → high)
Leverage: high impact, low risk – use competition, tenders and volume
Strategic: high impact, high risk – build partnerships, share forecasts, develop a backup source
Non-critical: low impact, low risk – simplify with catalogues or framework agreements
Bottleneck: low impact, high risk – secure supply with safety stock or longer contracts
:::

A strategic item might be the main staple you import from a few origins. A bottleneck item might be a special packaging film from one supplier. To apply the matrix, score your top 30 items by spend from 1 to 5 on profit impact and on supply risk, place them on the grid, and write one strategy line per quadrant.

::: quiz
? A distributor buys large volumes of standard sugar that many exporters can supply. Which strategy fits?
- Long-term single-source partnership || That suits strategic items with high supply risk.
* Use competition and tenders to get the best price || High spend with low risk is a leverage item.
- Hold extra safety stock to secure supply || That is the bottleneck strategy, for hard-to-replace items.
:::

## 3. Total cost of ownership

The cheapest price is often not the cheapest supplier. Compare landed cost, the full cost of getting goods into your warehouse, then add the costs of poor performance.

- Purchase price per unit.
- Ocean freight and insurance (depending on the delivery terms agreed).
- Import duties and taxes, port charges, clearance and agent fees.
- Inland transport from port to warehouse.
- Quality losses: damaged bags, short weight, rejected lots.
- Extra stock needed because of long or unreliable lead times.
- Admin time spent on claims and follow-up.

Example: Supplier A offers vegetable oil at USD 1,000 per tonne, Supplier B at USD 1,030. A's shipments arrive on average three weeks late and 2% of drums are damaged. The distributor estimates that A's delays force it to hold an extra 100 tonnes of safety stock, costing about USD 2 per tonne bought in financing and storage, and that damage costs USD 20 per tonne. A's real cost is about USD 1,022 per tonne before freight, while B, with reliable delivery and negligible damage, is USD 1,030. The gap is far smaller than the price list suggests, and B may be the better choice for a strategic item.

::: quiz
? Which cost is part of total cost of ownership but not of the unit price?
- The supplier's price per tonne || The price itself is the starting point, not an extra cost.
- The supplier's profit margin || The margin is already inside the price.
* Extra safety stock needed because the supplier is often late || Unreliable delivery forces you to hold and finance more stock.
:::

## 4. Supplier scorecards and sourcing strategy

A scorecard turns opinions into facts. Choose four criteria, weight them, and score every quarter from data.

- Quality (for example 30%): percentage of lots accepted without claim.
- Delivery (30%): percentage of shipments on time and with correct documents.
- Cost (25%): price compared with market or target, and willingness to share cost changes early.
- Service (15%): speed of response, handling of claims, flexibility.

Score each criterion from 1 to 5, multiply by its weight and add up. Share the result with the supplier and agree improvement actions.

Then decide how many sources to use. Single sourcing gives better prices and closer cooperation but high risk if the supplier fails. Dual sourcing, for example 70% with a main supplier and 30% with a second, keeps competition and protects supply. Multiple sourcing suits leverage items where many suppliers are equal.

## Worked example: choosing rice suppliers in Lobito

A distributor in Benguela province imports rice through Lobito port. Rice is its largest spend and a strategic item: it buys from only one exporter, and a delayed vessel last year left depots empty for three weeks.

The buyer sends a full specification to four exporters from two origins and scores each using past shipment data or references.

- Exporter 1: lowest price, quality 3, delivery 2, cost 5, service 3. Weighted score = 0.9 + 0.6 + 1.25 + 0.45 = 3.2.
- Exporter 2: price 2% higher, quality 4, delivery 5, cost 4, service 4. Weighted score = 1.2 + 1.5 + 1.0 + 0.6 = 4.3.

::: case | Rice sourcing decision in Lobito
The buyer, Teresa, must recommend a sourcing strategy for rice to management. Exporter 2 scores 4.3 but costs 2% more. Exporter 1 scores 3.2, is cheapest, and ships from a different origin.
? What should Teresa recommend?
- 100% to Exporter 1 for the lowest price || This repeats last year's risk with a weaker supplier.
- 100% to Exporter 2 for the best score || Better, but a single source of a strategic item still leaves depots exposed.
* 70% to Exporter 2 under a 12-month agreement, 30% to Exporter 1 shipment by shipment || Dual sourcing from two origins protects supply and keeps competition.
- Split equally among all four exporters || Too many sources weakens the partnership a strategic item needs.
:::

## 1. Why we hold inventory

Inventory is cash in physical form. Every unit should have a clear reason to exist. The main types are:

- Cycle stock: if you order 24,000 bags every two months, stock starts high and falls to near zero before the next delivery. On average you hold half the order quantity. Average cycle stock = Q / 2.
- Safety stock: demand is never exactly as forecast, and ships are sometimes late. Safety stock covers this uncertainty.
- Pipeline stock: goods on the water or waiting at Luanda or Lobito port. Long import lead times mean large pipeline stock that ties up cash before you can sell it.
- Seasonal and promotional stock: built up in advance of a known peak, such as the festive season.

Average inventory in the warehouse is approximately Q / 2 + safety stock. This simple formula shows the two main levers: smaller, more frequent orders reduce cycle stock, and better forecasting or more reliable suppliers reduce safety stock.

## 2. Calculating safety stock

When demand varies but lead time is fairly stable, a widely used formula is:

- Safety stock = Z x standard deviation of daily demand x square root of lead time in days.

Z is the service factor from the normal distribution: 1.28 for 90% service, 1.65 for 95%, 2.05 for 98% and 2.33 for 99%.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Collect demand | Daily or weekly demand for 3 to 6 months; Corrected for stock-outs | Clean data first
SLIDE Step 2: Average and variation | Excel AVERAGE for mean demand; Excel STDEV.S for standard deviation | Measure the noise
SLIDE Step 3: Use actual lead time | From recent orders, not the supplier's promise; Include port clearance and inland transport | Real days, not quoted days
SLIDE Step 4: Choose service level | Match it to the item's importance; Look up the Z value | Higher service, more stock
SLIDE Step 5: Apply and round up | SS = Z x standard deviation x square root of lead time; Round up to whole units | Write it in the system

Very high service targets are expensive, so reserve them for the most important items. If lead time varies a lot, safety stock must be larger; more advanced formulas include lead time variation.

::: quiz
? A buyer raises the service target for an item from 95% to 99%. What happens to safety stock?
- It stays the same || Safety stock depends directly on Z, which changes.
* It rises by about 40% || Z rises from 1.65 to 2.33, about 40% higher.
- It doubles || The increase is large but well below double.
:::

## 3. The reorder point

The reorder point tells you when to order. It must cover expected demand during the lead time, plus safety stock:

- ROP = average daily demand x lead time in days + safety stock.

Compare the ROP with your inventory position, not only with what is on the shelf. Inventory position = stock on hand + stock on order - customer orders not yet shipped. If you forget stock already on order, you will order twice.

::: quiz
? Daily demand is 100 units, lead time is 40 days and safety stock is 600 units. What is the ROP?
- 4,000 units || This forgets the safety stock.
* 4,600 units || 100 x 40 + 600 = 4,600.
- 700 units || This adds demand and safety stock without multiplying by lead time.
:::

## 4. ABC analysis and review policies

You cannot control thousands of SKUs with the same effort. ABC analysis, based on the Pareto principle, ranks items by annual consumption value (annual units x unit cost):

::: diagram pyramid | ABC analysis
A items: often about 20% of SKUs and about 80% of value – review weekly, count often
B items: the middle group – review monthly
C items: many SKUs of small value – simple rules, less frequent review
:::

Cut-off percentages vary between companies; what matters is that control effort follows value.

Two common review policies are:

- Continuous review: order a fixed quantity when the inventory position reaches the ROP. Good for A items.
- Periodic review: check at fixed intervals, for example every two weeks, and order up to a target level. Easier to fill a container from one supplier, but safety stock must cover the review period plus the lead time.

## Worked example: 25 kg rice in a Luanda warehouse

A distributor sells 25 kg bags of imported rice. Daily demand over the last six months averaged 400 bags, with a standard deviation of 80 bags. Recent orders took 60 days from purchase order to stock available in the warehouse, including about two weeks at the port. Management wants a 95% service level.

Safety stock = 1.65 x 80 x square root of 60. The square root of 60 is about 7.75, so safety stock = 1.65 x 80 x 7.75 = 1,023 bags.

Reorder point = 400 x 60 + 1,023 = 24,000 + 1,023 = 25,023 bags.

The buyer orders 24,000 bags each time. Average cycle stock = 24,000 / 2 = 12,000 bags. Average warehouse inventory is about 12,000 + 1,023 = 13,023 bags.

::: case | Monday reorder check
On Monday the buyer, Carla, sees 9,000 bags on hand, 17,000 bags on a vessel, and 1,500 bags in customer orders not yet shipped. The reorder point is 25,023 bags and the order quantity is 24,000.
? What should Carla do?
- Order extra, because only 9,000 bags are on the shelf || Ignoring the vessel means ordering far too much.
* Order 24,000 bags today, because the inventory position of 24,500 is below the ROP || 9,000 + 17,000 - 1,500 = 24,500, which is below 25,023.
- Wait, because 26,000 bags are on hand and on order || This forgets the 1,500 bags already promised to customers.
:::

## Common mistakes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- compare the main transport modes on cost, speed, flexibility and reliability;
- choose between direct, hub-and-spoke and cross-docking distribution designs;

## Key terms

::: terms
Distribution network: the set of warehouses, depots and transport links used to move goods to customers
Hub and spoke: a design in which goods move in bulk to regional hubs, then out in smaller loads to customers
Cross-docking: moving goods from incoming vehicles directly onto outgoing vehicles with little or no storage
Vehicle utilisation: the share of a vehicle's weight or volume capacity actually used
Drop density: the number of deliveries a vehicle makes in a given area or route
Proof of delivery (POD): a signed or electronic record that the customer received the goods
:::

## 1. Transport modes

Each transport mode has strengths and weaknesses. Choose by comparing cost, speed, flexibility, reliability and the value of the goods.

- Sea: the lowest cost per tonne for long distances and the backbone of food imports through Luanda and Lobito. Slow, and exposed to port delays.
- Road: the most flexible, door to door, but higher cost per tonne than rail or sea, and road conditions affect transit times.
- Rail: efficient for heavy, bulky loads over long fixed corridors. In Angola, the Benguela railway links Lobito port with the interior, which can suit regular bulk flows inland.
- Air: very fast and very expensive, for urgent, high-value or light items.

Most supply chains combine modes: sea to the port, then road or rail inland. Ask which combination gives the lowest total cost for the service you need.

::: quiz
? A machine is stopped waiting for a small, expensive spare part from Europe. Which mode fits best?
- Sea, because it is cheapest per tonne || The weeks of delay cost far more than the freight saving.
* Air, because the part is urgent, light and valuable || Air suits urgent, high-value, light items.
- Rail, because it is efficient || Rail suits heavy bulk loads on fixed corridors, not urgent imports.
:::

## 2. Distribution network designs

How many stock points you have, and where, is one of the biggest supply chain decisions.

::: diagram steps | Hub-and-spoke flow for an importer
Port: Luanda or Lobito, discharge and clearance
Central warehouse: main stock point
Trunk transport: full trucks or rail to the regions
Regional depot or cross-dock: store briefly or transfer the same day
Local delivery: vans deliver to shops by zone
:::

Three basic designs are:

- Direct delivery from a central warehouse: all stock is in one place, so total inventory is lowest and control is easiest. But long trips to distant provinces raise transport cost and delivery time.
- Hub and spoke: bulk trucks or trains move goods to regional depots, which deliver locally. Trunk transport is cheaper per tonne and local service faster, but each depot needs its own stock and staff.
- Cross-docking: goods leave the depot the same day on local vehicles. This gives hub transport benefits with little extra stock, but needs accurate information and tight timing.

More depots mean cheaper local deliveries but more total stock.

## 3. Vehicle utilisation and cost per tonne

Most transport costs are per trip, not per tonne: the driver, fuel and vehicle cost the same whether the truck is full or not. So utilisation drives cost.

- Cost per tonne = total trip cost / tonnes carried.
- Weight utilisation = tonnes loaded / legal payload x 100.
- A vehicle "weighs out" when it reaches its weight limit first (typical for rice, sugar or flour) and "cubes out" when it fills its volume first (typical for light goods such as snacks). Mixing heavy and light products on one vehicle can use both limits better.

Example: a trip from Luanda to a depot costs 400,000 Kz. A full 20-tonne load gives 400,000 / 20 = 20,000 Kz per tonne. If the truck leaves with only 12 tonnes (60% utilisation), the cost is 400,000 / 12 = 33,333 Kz per tonne, two-thirds higher. Waiting one day to consolidate orders into a full load is often cheaper, as long as customer service allows it. Return loads also cut the cost of a round trip.

::: quiz
? A trip costs 500,000 Kz. The truck can carry 20 tonnes but leaves with 10. What is the cost per tonne?
- 25,000 Kz || That would be the cost at a full 20-tonne load.
* 50,000 Kz || 500,000 / 10 = 50,000 Kz per tonne, double the full-load cost.
- 10,000 Kz || This divides by the wrong number.
:::

## 4. Route planning for local delivery

For multi-drop routes in a city, the key measure is cost per drop or cost per case delivered. To plan routes:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Zone the area | Divide by municipality or main road; Give each zone fixed delivery days | Group orders, raise drop density
SLIDE Step 2: Check delivery windows | Many shops receive only in the morning; Note each customer's window | Respect the customer's clock
SLIDE Step 3: Sequence and load | Start with the earliest windows and avoid traffic peaks; Load last drop first | Shortest sensible route
SLIDE Step 4: Confirm delivery | Collect proof of delivery at every stop; Record shortage or damage immediately | No POD, no claim
:::

## Worked example: depot deliveries in Benguela province

A distributor delivers food products from Luanda to wholesalers and shops in Benguela province. Currently, one truck leaves Luanda every day with whatever has been ordered, often 8 to 12 tonnes on a 20-tonne truck, and delivers directly to 15 to 20 customers spread across the province. Each trip costs about 600,000 Kz, and deliveries are often late.

Current cost per tonne: 600,000 / 10 tonnes on average = 60,000 Kz per tonne.

She proposes a hub-and-spoke design using a small cross-dock at a rented depot in Benguela city. Full 20-tonne trucks leave Luanda three times a week (trunk cost 550,000 Kz per trip, or 27,500 Kz per tonne). At the depot, goods move straight onto two light vans that deliver to customers by zone, with fixed delivery days, at a local cost of about 15,000 Kz per tonne. Total cost is about 42,500 Kz per tonne, plus depot rent and staff.

## Learning objectives

## Key terms

::: terms
KPI (key performance indicator): a measure used to track progress towards an important goal
OTIF (on time in full): the percentage of orders delivered both on the agreed date and with the full quantity
Fill rate: the percentage of ordered units (or lines) that were delivered
Inventory turns: how many times average inventory is sold and replaced in a year
DIO, DSO and DPO: days inventory outstanding, days sales outstanding and days payables outstanding
Cash-to-cash cycle: the number of days between paying suppliers and receiving cash from customers
:::

## 1. Service metrics: OTIF and fill rate

Service should be measured from the customer's point of view. Two KPIs are widely used.

- OTIF = orders delivered on time and in full / total orders x 100.
- Fill rate = units delivered / units ordered x 100. It can also be measured by order lines.

OTIF is stricter. Before measuring, agree exact definitions with your customers: does "on time" mean the agreed day, or a time window? Does "in full" allow any tolerance? Unclear definitions are the main reason why a supplier reports 95% OTIF while the customer feels it is 70%.

::: quiz
? A customer orders 10 lines and receives 9 complete lines on the agreed day. How does this order count?
- As a success for OTIF || One short line makes the order not in full.
* As an OTIF failure, while fill rate by lines is 90% || OTIF counts the whole order, fill rate counts lines.
- As a fill rate of 0% || Nine of ten lines were delivered, so fill rate is 90%.
:::

## 2. Inventory metrics: turns and days of inventory

Inventory turns show how efficiently stock is used.

- Inventory turns = annual cost of goods sold / average inventory value (both at cost).
- Days of inventory (DIO) = 365 / inventory turns, or average inventory / annual cost of goods sold x 365.

Higher turns mean less cash tied up for the same sales, but very high turns may signal frequent stock-outs. Importers with long lead times naturally have lower turns, so compare with your own history and track turns by product family, because an average can hide slow or obsolete stock.

## 3. The cash-to-cash cycle

The cash-to-cash (C2C) cycle measures how long cash is locked in operations, from the day you pay suppliers until the day customers pay you.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Days of inventory | DIO = average inventory / annual cost of goods sold x 365; Falls with better forecasts and smaller orders | Cash sitting on shelves
SLIDE Step 2: Days receivables | DSO = average accounts receivable / annual sales x 365; Falls when deliveries and documents are correct | Cash owed by customers
SLIDE Step 3: Days payables | DPO = average accounts payable / annual cost of goods sold x 365; Set by supplier payment terms | Supplier credit you use
SLIDE Step 4: Combine | C2C = DIO + DSO - DPO; Shorter is better | Days your cash is locked in
:::

Importers that pay suppliers before shipment have very short or zero DPO, which makes managing DIO and DSO even more important.

::: quiz
? DIO is 80 days, DSO is 30 days and DPO is 10 days. What is the cash-to-cash cycle?
- 120 days || This adds DPO instead of subtracting it.
- 60 days || This subtracts DSO by mistake.
* 100 days || 80 + 30 - 10 = 100 days.
:::

## 4. A balanced set of KPIs

No single KPI tells the whole story: huge stocks may lift OTIF but damage turns and cash. Use a small, balanced scorecard with one owner per KPI:

- Service: OTIF and fill rate.
- Cost: transport cost per tonne and total supply chain cost as a percentage of sales.
- Assets and cash: inventory turns, days of inventory and cash-to-cash cycle.
- Quality: forecast accuracy (MAPE) and supplier on-time delivery.

SCOR also groups metrics into performance attributes, including reliability, responsiveness, agility, cost and asset management, a useful check on your scorecard's balance.

## 5. Improving performance: PDCA and the 5 Whys

The Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle, associated with W. Edwards Deming, gives a simple structure:

::: diagram cycle | Plan-Do-Check-Act
Plan: choose one KPI gap, analyse causes and plan a change
Do: test the change on a small scale, such as one depot or product family
Check: compare results with the baseline after a fixed period
Act: adopt the change if it works, adjust it if not, then choose the next gap
:::

To find causes, sort failures by reason and count them (a Pareto analysis), then ask "why?" repeatedly, usually about five times, until you reach a cause you can act on.

## Worked example: KPI review at a Luanda distributor

A food distributor in Luanda reviews last quarter's performance. It delivered 1,000 orders. 920 arrived on the agreed day, and 850 of those were also complete. OTIF = 850 / 1,000 = 85%. Customers ordered 120,000 cartons and received 114,000. Fill rate = 114,000 / 120,000 = 95%.

Annual cost of goods sold is 6,000 million Kz and average inventory is 1,200 million Kz. Turns = 6,000 / 1,200 = 5. Days of inventory = 365 / 5 = 73 days. Days sales outstanding are 40 days and days payables outstanding are 20 days. Cash-to-cash cycle = 73 + 40 - 20 = 93 days.

The team runs a Pareto analysis on the 150 failed orders. 70 failures are "not in full" because of stock-outs on 1-litre oil and 5 kg sugar. Asking why: the products ran out because the order was late; the order was late because the reorder point was based on a 45-day lead time; actual lead time over the last six months was 65 days because of port delays; nobody updated the parameter. Root cause: planning parameters are not reviewed.

::: case | Choosing the PDCA test
The supply chain manager, Mateus, must choose one change to test for two months.
? Which test should he choose?
- Double safety stock on every item || This hits turns and cash and does not fix the root cause.
* Recalculate reorder points for the 18 A items using actual lead times, and review them quarterly || This targets the root cause on the items that matter most.
- Change the definition of OTIF to exclude port delays || This improves the number, not the customer's experience.
- Ask sales to stop promising delivery dates || This hides the problem and damages customer trust.
:::

After two months OTIF rises to 91% and turns stay at about 5, so the quarterly review becomes a standard S&OP step.
