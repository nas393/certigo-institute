## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain what customer success is and how it differs from customer service and sales;
- describe the five stages of the B2B customer lifecycle;
- calculate churn rate, customer retention rate and gross revenue retention;
- estimate customer lifetime value and use it to justify retention work.

## Key terms

::: terms
Customer success: a proactive function that makes sure customers achieve the business results they bought for, so that they renew and grow
Churn: customers or revenue lost when accounts cancel, do not renew or reduce their contract
Customer retention rate (CRR): the percentage of customers at the start of a period who are still customers at the end
Gross revenue retention (GRR): the share of starting recurring revenue kept after churn and downgrades, excluding any growth
Customer lifetime value (CLV): the total gross profit a customer is expected to generate over the whole relationship
Desired outcome: the result the customer needs, delivered in the way they expect to experience it
:::

## 1. What customer success is

In a B2B company the first sale is only the start. A bank selling a cash-management service to companies, a telecom provider connecting a firm's branches, a software company licensing an ERP, or a distributor supplying a supermarket chain: each earns most of its profit over years of renewals and repeat orders.

Customer success (CS) makes sure those customers get the result they paid for. It differs from two neighbouring functions:

- Customer service is reactive: it solves problems when the customer makes contact, and is measured on speed and quality of resolution.
- Sales wins new customers and new contracts, and is measured on bookings.
- Customer success is proactive: it monitors each account, acts before problems grow, and links the customer's use of your product to their business goals. It is measured on retention, expansion and customer outcomes.

A useful way to think about the customer's goal is the desired outcome: what they must achieve (for example, "reduce stock-outs in our 12 stores") plus how they expect to get there ("with weekly deliveries and one contact person"). Deliver the result in a painful way and the customer may still leave.

The B2B lifecycle has five stages, and CS owns or supports each one:

::: diagram cycle | The B2B customer lifecycle
Onboard: set up the account and agree success criteria
Adopt: users actually use the product or service
Realise value: the customer sees measurable business results
Renew: the contract or supply agreement is extended
Expand and advocate: the customer buys more and recommends you
:::

::: quiz
? A company client calls its bank because a transfer failed, and the relationship manager fixes it within an hour. Which function did this work?
* Customer service, because it was a reactive fix to a problem the client raised || Correct. Fast resolution is service. Success would mean spotting the risk earlier or linking the service to the client's goals.
- Customer success, because the client ended up satisfied || Satisfaction after a fix is good service. Customer success is proactive work towards outcomes.
- Sales, because it protected the bank's revenue || Sales wins new business. It does not usually fix operational problems.
:::

## 2. Measuring retention: the core formulas

You cannot manage retention without measuring it. Calculate these four numbers for the same period, usually a quarter or a year:

::: slides
SLIDE 1 Logo churn rate | Customers lost in the period ÷ customers at the start × 100 | Counts accounts, not money
SLIDE 2 Customer retention rate | CRR = (E − N) ÷ S × 100 | E end, N new, S start customers
SLIDE 3 Revenue churn | Recurring revenue lost from cancellations and downgrades ÷ starting recurring revenue × 100 | Shows the money impact
SLIDE 4 Gross revenue retention | GRR = (starting revenue − churn − downgrades) ÷ starting revenue × 100 | Never above 100%
:::

New customers are excluded from CRR. If you include them, strong sales can hide the fact that you are losing existing accounts. Track logo and revenue measures together: losing one large account may be 2% of your logos but 20% of your revenue.

The business case is strong. Frederick Reichheld and W. Earl Sasser (1990) studied service companies and found that, in the industries they examined, reducing customer defections by 5% raised profits by 25% to 85%, because loyal customers buy more over time, cost less to serve and refer others.

::: quiz
? A company had 80 customers on 1 January, won 15 new customers during the year and had 85 on 31 December. What is its customer retention rate?
- 106% || This divides end customers by start customers and ignores the new customers.
- 94% || Check the formula again: (85 − 15) ÷ 80.
* 87.5% || Correct. (85 − 15) ÷ 80 × 100 = 87.5%. It lost 10 of its original 80 customers.
:::

## 3. Customer lifetime value

Customer lifetime value (CLV) tells you what a customer is worth over the whole relationship. A simple B2B formula is:

CLV = average annual revenue per customer × gross margin % × expected lifetime in years

Expected lifetime can be estimated as 1 ÷ annual churn rate. If 20% of customers leave each year, the average customer stays about 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5 years.

CLV supports decisions: how much you can spend on onboarding, whether a dedicated account manager is justified, and what one point of churn is worth. Because lifetime is 1 ÷ churn, cutting churn from 20% to 10% doubles expected lifetime from 5 to 10 years, and roughly doubles CLV.

## Worked example: a business software reseller in Luanda

A company in Luanda resells and supports accounting software for small and medium businesses. Each client pays an average of 1,800,000 Kz per year for licences and support, and gross margin is 40%. Last year it started with 120 clients, won 30 and ended with 126.

- CRR = (126 − 30) ÷ 120 × 100 = 80%. It lost 24 clients, so logo churn = 24 ÷ 120 = 20%.
- Expected lifetime = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5 years.
- CLV = 1,800,000 × 0.40 × 5 = 3,600,000 Kz of gross profit per client.

If churn fell to 15%, lifetime would rise to about 6.7 years and CLV to about 4,800,000 Kz. Across 120 clients that is roughly 144 million Kz of extra lifetime gross profit, far more than the cost of one customer success manager.

::: case | Where to start at the software reseller
The general manager, Helena, asks the new CS lead, Jorge, to reduce churn. Exit notes show that most of the 24 lost clients stopped using the software within six months of buying it.
? What should Jorge do first?
- Offer every client a 10% discount at renewal || A blanket discount cuts margin on every account and does not fix the cause, which is low early usage.
* Review how recent clients were onboarded and add clear success criteria and usage checks in the first 90 days || Correct. The data points to failure early in the lifecycle, so fixing onboarding and adoption attacks the real cause.
- Ask sales to win 30 more clients to replace the lost ones || New sales do not stop the leak, and replacing customers usually costs more than keeping them.
- Wait for the next renewal season and call clients then || By then most at-risk clients have already decided to leave.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Treating customer success as a renamed support desk that only reacts to tickets.
- Measuring only logo churn, so the loss of one large account goes unnoticed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain why time-to-value predicts retention and calculate it;
- run a sales-to-customer-success handover using a checklist;
- plan and lead a kickoff meeting with a new customer;
- write a phased onboarding plan with SMART success criteria.

## Key terms

::: terms
Onboarding: the structured period after signing in which the customer is set up, trained and guided to first results
Time-to-value (TTV): the time between contract start and the moment the customer first achieves a result they care about
First value milestone: the first specific, measurable result agreed with the customer
Success criteria: measurable statements of what the customer must achieve for the project to count as a success
Kickoff meeting: the first formal meeting with the customer team to confirm goals, roles, plan and dates
Executive sponsor: the senior customer manager who owns the business goal and can remove obstacles
:::

## 1. Why the first 90 days matter

When a B2B customer signs, someone has promised their own management that the purchase will solve a problem. Until a result appears, the purchase is only a cost and a risk. If results are slow, users drift back to old habits, such as the old spreadsheet, the old supplier or the manual process, and the sponsor starts to doubt the decision. Many accounts that churn at renewal were really lost during onboarding.

That is why customer success teams track time-to-value:

TTV (days) = date the first value milestone is reached − contract start date

If three new corporate clients reached their first value milestone after 35, 60 and 85 days, the average TTV is (35 + 60 + 85) ÷ 3 = 60 days. Track TTV by segment and product, and set a target. You have two levers: shorten the time to the first result, and make that result visible to the people who decide on renewal.

::: quiz
? A supermarket chain has bought a demand-planning service from a distributor. Which is the best first value milestone?
- The supply contract is signed by both parties || Signing is the start of onboarding, not a result for the customer.
- All buyers have completed the training || Training is an activity. It does not prove a business result.
* Stock-outs on the top 50 products fall below the agreed target for one full month || Correct. It is specific, measurable and tied to the reason the customer bought.
:::

## 2. Handover and kickoff

Sales knows why the customer bought. If that knowledge stays in the salesperson's head, the customer is asked the same questions twice and promises get lost. Run a formal sales-to-CS handover before you contact the customer. Capture:

- the business goals and the pain that triggered the purchase;
- exactly what was promised: scope, dates, prices, service levels and special conditions;
- the stakeholders: executive sponsor, day-to-day lead, end users, and anyone who opposed the purchase;
- the risks: tight deadlines, limited IT capacity, a competitor that was also considered.

Then hold a kickoff meeting with the customer team, following this agenda:

::: slides
SLIDE 1 Introductions and roles | Who is who on both sides; Name the executive sponsor and the day-to-day lead | No sponsor means no escalation path
SLIDE 2 Confirm goals | Restate why they bought; Agree two or three success criteria | Use their words and numbers
SLIDE 3 Review the plan | Phases, milestones and dates; What the customer must provide and when | Onboarding is a joint project
SLIDE 4 Agree ways of working | Meeting rhythm, channels and escalation route; A weekly 30-minute check-in suits most accounts | Decide this now, not during a crisis
SLIDE 5 Next steps | Actions, owners and dates | Send the written summary within 24 hours
:::

## 3. The onboarding plan and success criteria

Onboarding is a joint project, not something you do to the customer. Write a phased plan that shows what each side does and by when:

::: diagram steps | A phased onboarding plan
Handover: sales passes goals, promises and stakeholders to CS
Kickoff: confirm goals, roles, success criteria and dates
Set-up: configure, integrate and load data, with customer inputs on time
Training and adoption: train users by role and check real usage
First value milestone: confirm the agreed result with the sponsor
Transition: move to the ongoing success plan and regular reviews
:::

Success criteria should be SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound, the acronym George Doran published in 1981 for management objectives. Compare "improve our reporting" with "by 31 March, the finance team produces the monthly sales report from the new system within three working days of month end, without manual spreadsheets". Agree two or three criteria, in the customer's words, and get the sponsor to confirm them in writing.

::: quiz
? Which success criterion is SMART?
- The client will be happier with our deliveries || Happiness is not defined or measurable, and there is no date.
* Late deliveries fall below 10% of trips by 30 June, measured from tracking reports || Correct. It is specific, measurable, realistic, relevant and has a deadline.
- Use the new system as much as possible this year || "As much as possible" cannot be measured and is not a business result.
:::

## Worked example: a telecom provider in Luanda

The enterprise unit of a telecom provider in Luanda sold a managed network and vehicle-tracking service to a logistics company with eight depots and 120 trucks, for 48,000,000 Kz a year. In the past, new enterprise clients waited an average of 75 days for any result. The account manager, Carla, changed the approach:

- Handover: the salesperson explained that the client bought tracking to cut late deliveries to supermarkets, currently 18% of trips.
- Kickoff (day 5): the operations director agreed to be executive sponsor. Success criterion: late deliveries below 10% of trips by day 60.
- Set-up (days 5–25): devices were installed depot by depot, starting with the busiest depot in Viana, so results appeared there first.
- Training (days 20–35): dispatchers learned the live map and late-trip alerts, and Carla checked log-ins weekly.
- First value milestone (day 38): the Viana depot recorded 9% late trips for two weeks running.

TTV fell from 75 to 38 days, and the sponsor presented the Viana result to his board.

::: case | The missing data at the Lobito depot
On day 30 the Lobito depot still has no devices, because the client's IT team has not sent the vehicle list. The milestone date is close and the depot manager tells colleagues that "the system does not work".
? What should Carla do?
- Wait quietly, because the delay is the client's fault || Silence lets a false story grow, and the sponsor may blame the provider.
- Install devices on any available trucks without the list || Installing without accurate data creates reporting errors and rework later.
* Escalate the blocked action to the sponsor, showing the impact on the milestone and asking for a date for the list || Correct. The sponsor owns the outcome and can remove internal blockers. Facts and dates keep the plan on track.
- Move the milestone to day 90 without telling anyone || Changing goals silently destroys trust and hides the risk.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain the difference between leading and lagging indicators of churn;
- choose signals for the main health dimensions: usage, outcomes, relationship and support;
- build and calculate a simple weighted health score;
- set thresholds and use the score, with account value, to prioritise your time.

## Key terms

::: terms
Health score: a single number or colour that summarises how likely an account is to renew and grow
Leading indicator: a signal that changes before the outcome, such as falling usage before a cancellation
Lagging indicator: a signal that shows an outcome after it has happened, such as churn or a lost renewal
Adoption rate: the share of licensed or expected users, sites or products actually in use
Weighting: the importance given to each dimension in the total score
Calibration: checking the score against what actually happened to accounts and adjusting it
:::

## 1. Why a health score

An account manager with 40 accounts cannot study every account in depth every week. A health score turns many signals into one view, so you can see which accounts need attention now.

Its value comes from leading indicators. Churn and lost renewals are lagging: when you see them it is too late. Falling usage, a champion who stops replying or rising complaints usually appear months earlier. Group signals into four dimensions, and add a fifth if useful:

- Usage: is the customer using what they bought? Examples: adoption rate (active users ÷ licensed users), log-in frequency, order frequency against the agreed pattern.
- Outcomes: are they reaching their success criteria? Examples: progress against the success plan, measured business results.
- Relationship: do you have access and goodwill? Examples: an active executive sponsor and champion, attendance at reviews, survey scores.
- Support: what is their experience of problems? Examples: number and severity of tickets, open escalations, repeat issues.
- Commercial (optional): payment delays, requests for price cuts, downgrades.

Choose signals you can measure every month from real data. A score built on guesses will not be trusted.

::: quiz
? Which of these is a leading indicator of churn?
* Active users at a client fell from 45 to 28 over two months || Correct. Falling usage appears before a cancellation and gives you time to act.
- The client did not renew its contract last month || This is the outcome itself, a lagging indicator.
- Revenue from the client fell 30% last year || Last year's revenue describes what has already happened.
:::

## 2. Building a weighted score

Follow five steps:

::: slides
SLIDE 1 Choose signals | One to three per dimension; Data you can collect every month | Simple beats complete
SLIDE 2 Define scoring rules | Convert each signal to 0–100; Example: adoption 80% or more = 100, 60–79% = 70, 40–59% = 40, below 40% = 10 | Same rule for every account
SLIDE 3 Set weights | Weights add up to 100%; More weight for signals that best predicted past churn | Start with judgement, then test
SLIDE 4 Calculate | Multiply each dimension score by its weight; Add the results | The total runs from 0 to 100
SLIDE 5 Set thresholds and actions | For example green 75+, amber 50–74, red below 50; Each colour triggers a defined action | A score without action is decoration
:::

The formula is: health score = sum of (dimension score × weight).

Thresholds are your choice; the ones above are only a starting point. Then calibrate: take accounts that churned or downgraded last year and check what their score was six months before. If many looked green, your signals or weights are wrong. Review the model every quarter.

::: quiz
? A team finds that most accounts that churned last year were green six months before they left. What should it do?
- Lower the green threshold so more accounts look healthy || This makes the score even less accurate.
* Review which signals and weights failed to predict churn and adjust the model || Correct. This is calibration: the score must reflect what actually happens to accounts.
- Stop using the score and rely on the account managers' opinions || Judgement matters, but without data, early warnings are easily missed.
:::

## 3. Using the score to prioritise

Health alone does not tell you where to spend time. Combine it with account value:

::: diagram matrix | Health and value: where to spend time | Health score (low → high) | Account value (low → high)
Rescue: high value, low health – senior attention and a save plan now
Grow: high value, high health – success plan, expansion and references
Scale: low value, low health – standard recovery playbook and group training
Nurture: low value, high health – light-touch and automated check-ins
:::

## Worked example: a food distributor in Viana

A food distributor in Viana supplies 60 key accounts: supermarkets, hotels and restaurant chains. The key account team built a monthly score:

- Usage (35%): orders placed against the agreed weekly frequency, and the number of product categories ordered.
- Outcomes (25%): fill rate and sell-through against agreed targets.
- Relationship (20%): access to the buyer and a senior sponsor, and attendance at the last business review.
- Support (10%): complaints and credit notes in the last 90 days.
- Commercial (10%): days overdue against payment terms.

A hotel group in Luanda scored usage 60, outcomes 70, relationship 40, support 80 and commercial 50.

Score = 60 × 0.35 + 70 × 0.25 + 40 × 0.20 + 80 × 0.10 + 50 × 0.10 = 21 + 17.5 + 8 + 8 + 5 = 59.5, which is amber.

The hotel group is 6% of key account revenue, so it needs attention soon. The weak relationship score explained the drop: a new purchasing manager had joined and had never met the team.

::: case | The new purchasing manager
The hotel group's new purchasing manager has asked two competitors for quotes. Orders have fallen from three a week to two. The account manager, Mário, has only ever dealt with the previous buyer.
? What should Mário do first?
- Email a new price list with a 5% discount || Discounting before understanding the new manager's priorities gives away margin and may not address the real issue.
* Request a meeting with the new manager and the operations director to understand their goals and review service results || Correct. Relationship is the weakest dimension. Rebuilding access to decision makers and showing value treats the cause.
- Wait for next month's score to confirm the trend || The leading indicators are already clear, and waiting lets competitors build the relationship first.
- Ask his manager to assign a different account manager || Nothing suggests the problem is Mário. The account needs ownership and action.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
Success plan: a shared document listing the customer's goals, metrics, baselines, targets, actions, owners and dates
Quarterly business review (QBR): a regular strategic meeting with the customer's decision makers to review results and agree next goals
Value realisation: the point at which the customer can see and measure the benefit they expected

## 1. The success plan

Customers renew when they can prove value, and proof needs a baseline and a target. The success plan is a living document agreed with the sponsor, usually built at the end of onboarding. For each goal it records:

- the business goal, in the customer's words;
- the metric and how it is measured;
- the baseline;
- the target and the date;
- the actions on both sides, with owners;
- the current status.

It follows a simple cycle:

::: diagram cycle | The success plan cycle
Agree goals: confirm goals, baselines and targets with the sponsor
Execute: both sides carry out the agreed actions
Measure: track the metrics monthly from real data
Review: present the results at the QBR
Reset: update goals and targets for the next period
:::

If the customer has no baseline, measure one in the first weeks, even roughly. Without it you cannot show improvement.

::: quiz
? A client says your service has "helped a lot" but nobody recorded how things were before you started. What is the main risk at renewal?
- The client will ask for more training || Training is not the issue. The issue is proving value.
* The client cannot prove the value to its management, so the renewal is easier to cut || Correct. Without a baseline, improvement cannot be measured and the budget holder sees only a cost.
- The success plan will have too many goals || The number of goals is not the problem described here.
:::

## 2. Running a QBR

A QBR (some companies call it an executive business review) is neither a sales pitch nor a support meeting. It is a conversation with decision makers about business results. Use this structure:

::: slides
SLIDE 1 Goals recap | Restate the goals from the success plan; Confirm they are still the priorities | Start with their agenda
SLIDE 2 Results versus targets | Baseline, target and actual for each metric; Be honest about misses | Numbers first, opinions second

Preparation matters as much as the meeting. About a week before, hold a short pre-meeting with your champion to check the numbers and avoid surprises. Send the deck two days ahead. Make sure the executive sponsor attends: if only operational staff come, it becomes an operational review. Keep it to about an hour, spend most of it on results and next goals, and send a written summary of actions within 24 hours. Hold QBRs quarterly for the largest accounts and twice a year for mid-sized ones.

## 3. ROI and the value story

Two calculations turn results into business language:

ROI = (benefit − cost) ÷ cost × 100

Payback period (months) = cost ÷ monthly benefit

Count only benefits the customer accepts, use their own figures where possible, and state your assumptions. Then wrap the numbers in a four-sentence value story: the situation and baseline, what was done, the result in numbers, and the next step. A sponsor can repeat a short story to their board; they cannot repeat a spreadsheet.

::: quiz
? A client pays 10,000,000 Kz a year for a service and agrees it produces 14,000,000 Kz of annual benefit. What is the ROI?
- 140% || This divides benefit by cost without subtracting the cost first.
* 40% || Correct. (14,000,000 − 10,000,000) ÷ 10,000,000 × 100 = 40%.
- 4% || The net benefit is 4,000,000 Kz, but ROI divides it by the cost.
:::

## Worked example: a bank's corporate team in Luanda

A bank in Luanda provides an online payments and payroll platform to a distribution company with 350 employees, for 9,600,000 Kz a year. Before, the client's three-person finance team needed six working days a month to process supplier payments and payroll by hand, and the company paid suppliers late-payment penalties.

The success plan set two goals: processing time down to two days by month six, and no supplier penalties. At the QBR, relationship manager Sofia showed:

- Processing time: baseline 6 days, target 2, actual 2. Time saved = 4 days × 3 staff × 12 months = 144 staff-days a year. At an estimated 40,000 Kz per staff-day, that is 5,760,000 Kz.
- Supplier penalties: baseline 6,000,000 Kz a year, now close to zero.
- Total annual benefit: 11,760,000 Kz. ROI = (11,760,000 − 9,600,000) ÷ 9,600,000 × 100 = 22.5%. Payback = 9,600,000 ÷ 980,000 per month, about 9.8 months.

Her value story: "In January your team spent six days a month on payments and paid supplier penalties. You moved payroll and supplier payments onto the platform. Processing now takes two days and penalties have stopped, worth about 11.8 million Kz this year. Next quarter we propose adding collections to save a further day."

::: case | The finance director challenges the numbers
During the QBR, the client's finance director says: "Your 40,000 Kz per staff-day is too high. Our real cost is 30,000."
? What should Sofia do?
- Defend her figure because it came from a general estimate || Arguing about assumptions makes the value story look like a sales trick.
* Accept the client's figure, recalculate on the spot and show the result || Correct. At 30,000 Kz the benefit is 10,320,000 Kz and ROI is about 7.5%, still positive. Using the customer's numbers makes the value credible.
- Skip the ROI slide and move straight to the roadmap || Avoiding the challenge leaves doubt about value in the decision maker's mind.
- Promise corrected figures by email and stop discussing value || The calculation is simple enough to do live, and delay loses the moment.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- recognise early warning signs of churn in B2B accounts;
- separate controllable from uncontrollable churn and find root causes;
- run a structured save play for an at-risk account;

## Key terms

::: terms
At-risk account: an account showing signals that it may not renew or may reduce its contract
Controllable churn: churn caused by things your company can influence, such as poor onboarding, service failures or low adoption
Uncontrollable churn: churn caused by events outside your influence, such as the customer closing or being taken over
Save play: a planned set of actions to recover an at-risk account
Champion: a person inside the customer organisation who supports your solution and helps you internally
Renewal forecast: an estimate of the recurring revenue expected to renew in a period
:::

## 1. Early warning signs and root causes

B2B customers rarely leave without warning. The signs fall into five groups:

- Usage: falling log-ins, orders or volumes, or users returning to old tools.
- Relationship: the champion or sponsor leaves, meetings are cancelled, replies slow down, nobody senior attends reviews.
- Support: repeated or unresolved escalations.
- Commercial: late payments, requests for price cuts, questions about the contract end date or notice period.
- Organisational: a merger, new management, budget cuts, or a tender for the service you provide.

One signal means watch closely. Two or more from different groups means act now.

Before acting, find the cause. Classify it as controllable or uncontrollable, then use the 5 Whys: keep asking "why?" until you reach a cause you can fix. For example: "Your price is too high" → why? They do not see enough value → why? Only 30% of users are active → why? New staff were never trained after the first trainer left. The real problem is a training gap, not price.

::: quiz
? A client's champion has resigned and the client paid its last two invoices 45 days late. Usage is stable. What should the account manager do?
- Nothing yet, because usage is still stable || Two signals from different groups, relationship and commercial, are enough to act.
* Treat the account as at risk, find the new decision makers and investigate the payment delays || Correct. Combined signals call for action before usage also falls.
- Offer a discount to secure the renewal early || A discount does not address the lost champion or the cause of late payment.
:::

## 2. The save play

When an account is at risk, follow a structured save play:

::: slides
SLIDE 1 Detect and confirm | Check the signals with data; Talk to the day-to-day contact | Facts before reactions
SLIDE 2 Diagnose the root cause | Use the 5 Whys; Separate controllable from uncontrollable | Price is often a symptom
SLIDE 3 Build a rescue plan | Actions, owners and dates; What success looks like in 30 to 60 days | Fix the cause, not the symptom
SLIDE 4 Engage the right level | Your executive meets their executive; Admit failures clearly | Senior attention shows commitment
SLIDE 5 Execute and track | Review the plan weekly; Update the health score | Close the loop with the sponsor
:::

Use discounts only as a last resort, and trade them for something of value, such as a longer term or wider scope. A discount that does not fix the cause only delays churn.

## 3. The renewal timeline and forecast

Renewals fail when they start too late. Work back from the renewal date:

::: diagram steps | A 120-day renewal timeline
120 days before: review health, risks and stakeholders and agree the renewal strategy internally
90 days: hold a value review with the sponsor and confirm needs for the next term
60 days: send the renewal proposal with options and map approvers and procurement steps
30 days: resolve final issues and agree the signature route
Renewal date: contract signed and a new success plan started
:::

Two adjustments. First, check the notice period: if the contract renews automatically unless notice is given before a certain date, the real decision date comes earlier. Second, large companies, banks and public bodies often need long internal approvals, so start earlier with them.

Managers need a renewal forecast. Place each renewal in a category with a probability your company agrees, for example commit 90%, probable 60%, at risk 25%. Then:

Weighted forecast = sum of (contract value × probability)

::: quiz
? Three renewals are due this quarter: 20 million Kz at 90%, 10 million Kz at 50% and 8 million Kz at 25%. What is the weighted forecast?
- 38 million Kz || That is the total contract value, not the weighted forecast.
- 21 million Kz || Recheck each line: 18 + 5 + 2.
* 25 million Kz || Correct. 20 × 0.9 + 10 × 0.5 + 8 × 0.25 = 18 + 5 + 2 = 25 million Kz.
:::

## Worked example: an IT services company in Luanda

A company in Luanda provides managed IT support and email to a hotel group with hotels in Luanda and Benguela, for 30,000,000 Kz a year. With 120 days to renewal, the account's health score fell from 78 to 48. The champion, the IT manager, had resigned. Tickets from Benguela had doubled. The new finance director had asked about the notice period.

The account manager, Rui, applied the 5 Whys. Tickets doubled because of email outages in Benguela. Outages happened because the local internet link was unstable and no backup link was set up. No backup existed because the Benguela hotel opened after onboarding and was set up in a hurry. The root cause was controllable: incomplete set-up.

His rescue plan: install the backup link within two weeks, have his director meet the group's general manager to admit the failure and present the plan, send a monthly reliability report, and train the new IT coordinator. At the 90-day value review, tickets were back to normal.

::: case | The discount demand
At 60 days, the finance director says: "We will renew only with a 20% discount." The health score has recovered to 70 and reliability is fixed.
? What should Rui do?
- Accept at once to secure the renewal || This gives away 6,000,000 Kz a year without testing whether price is the real issue.
* Ask what lies behind the request, show the value review results, and offer options such as a smaller discount for a longer term || Correct. Understand the driver, anchor on value, and trade any concession for something in return.
- Refuse to discuss price at all || Rigid refusal can push the client towards a tender. Negotiation at renewal is normal.
- Complain to the hotel group's chief executive about the finance director || Going over a decision maker's head damages the relationship.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- distinguish upsell from cross-sell and recognise expansion signals;
- run an expansion conversation based on customer outcomes;
- calculate net revenue retention (NRR) and compare it with gross revenue retention;

## Key terms

::: terms
Upsell: selling more of the same product or a higher tier, such as more users, more volume or a premium service level
Cross-sell: selling a different, related product or service to an existing customer
Expansion revenue: extra recurring revenue from existing customers through upsell, cross-sell or price increases
Net revenue retention (NRR): recurring revenue kept from existing customers after expansion, downgrades and churn, as a percentage of starting revenue
Advocacy: actions customers take to recommend you, such as references, referrals and case studies
Reference customer: a customer who agrees to speak to prospects about their experience
:::

## 1. Earning the right to expand

Expansion follows value. A customer who has not yet reached their goals will see any new offer as pressure. A good rule: propose expansion only to healthy accounts with documented results.

Look for expansion signals:

- usage near the limit, such as 95% of licences in use or volumes regularly above the contract;
- new sites, branches, departments or projects;
- questions about products or features the customer does not have;
- strong results in the success plan;
- growth in the customer's own business, such as new contracts won.

Then run the conversation in four steps:

::: slides
SLIDE 1 Start from their goals | Review the results so far; Ask about next year's priorities | Their plan, not your quota
SLIDE 2 Connect the gap | Show where the current scope limits their goal; Use usage data and success plan results | Make the need visible
SLIDE 3 Propose and quantify | One option with expected benefit and cost; ROI in their numbers | One clear option beats five
SLIDE 4 Agree next steps | Who decides and by when; Bring in sales if your company splits the roles | Agree the role split internally first
:::

::: quiz
? A telecom client with 200 mobile lines adds 50 more lines and also buys a fleet-tracking service. Which is which?
- Both are cross-sells, because the client bought more || Adding more of the same service is an upsell, not a cross-sell.
* The 50 lines are an upsell and fleet tracking is a cross-sell || Correct. More of the same is upsell, and a different related service is cross-sell.
- The 50 lines are a cross-sell and fleet tracking is an upsell || This reverses the definitions.
:::

## 2. Net revenue retention

NRR shows whether your existing customer base grows or shrinks on its own:

NRR = (starting recurring revenue + expansion − contraction − churn) ÷ starting recurring revenue × 100

GRR, from lesson 1, uses the same formula without expansion, so it can never exceed 100%. NRR above 100% means existing customers are growing even with no new sales. Track both: a high NRR with a low GRR means a few large accounts are hiding a leaky base. Distributors can apply the same logic to the annual purchases of key accounts.

::: quiz
? Starting recurring revenue is 200 million Kz. Expansion is 30 million, contraction 10 million and churn 20 million. What are NRR and GRR?
* NRR 100% and GRR 85% || Correct. NRR = (200 + 30 − 10 − 20) ÷ 200 = 100%. GRR = (200 − 10 − 20) ÷ 200 = 85%.
- NRR 115% and GRR 100% || This counts the expansion but ignores contraction and churn.
- NRR 85% and GRR 100% || The labels are swapped, and GRR cannot ignore losses.
:::

## 3. Advocacy: references and case studies

Satisfied customers help you win new ones. Advocacy works like a ladder, from small actions to large ones:

::: diagram pyramid | The advocacy ladder
Public case study: named results shared publicly or in a joint presentation
Reference calls: speaking to prospects on request
Referrals: introducing you to other companies
Testimonial: a short quote you may use
Positive feedback: high satisfaction or a promoter score
:::

Net Promoter Score, introduced by Fred Reichheld in 2003, labels customers who answer 9 or 10 as promoters, a good place to look for advocates. To build a case study: choose a healthy account with measured results; ask the sponsor after a successful review; interview them about the challenge, the solution and the results; draft it with numbers; and get written approval before using it. Many banks and public bodies have strict rules about naming suppliers. Do not overuse a reference: limit requests and thank them each time.

## Worked example: an ingredients distributor in Luanda

A distributor in Luanda supplies wheat flour to industrial bakeries under annual supply agreements, worth 1,200,000,000 Kz a year at the start of the year. During the year two bakeries closed (churn 60 million Kz), three reduced volumes (contraction 40 million Kz), and ten added yeast and bread improvers or higher contracted volumes (expansion 150 million Kz).

- NRR = (1,200 + 150 − 40 − 60) ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 104.2%.
- GRR = (1,200 − 40 − 60) ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 91.7%.

One bakery chain with six outlets had a green health score, ordered 20% above contract every month and was opening two outlets. The key account manager, Nuno, proposed a higher contracted volume with scheduled deliveries, plus a trial of bread improvers, showing the cost of the emergency orders it was placing.

::: case | The case study request
After a strong business review, the bakery chain's owner says he is very pleased with the service. Nuno would like to use the chain as a case study.
? What is the best approach?
- Write the case study from internal sales data and post it on social media || Publishing without approval breaks trust and may breach the customer's confidentiality.
* Ask the owner whether he will share his story, interview him and get written approval of the final text || Correct. Permission, the customer's own words and approval make the case study credible and protect the relationship.
- Offer a discount in exchange for a five-star online review || Paying for reviews makes them unreliable and can damage both companies' reputations.
- Give the owner's number to every prospect who asks for a reference || Overusing a reference without asking turns an advocate into an annoyed customer.
:::
