## Key terms

::: terms
Table: a set of data about one kind of thing, such as customers or orders, organised in rows and columns
Row: one record in a table, for example one order
Column: one attribute recorded for every row, for example order date
Primary key: a column whose value uniquely identifies each row in a table
Foreign key: a column that holds the primary key of a row in another table, linking the two tables
Grain: what exactly one row in a table represents
:::

## 1. Tables, rows and keys

Most business systems used by distributors, such as ERP, invoicing and stock systems, store their data in a relational database. The relational model was proposed by E. F. Codd in 1970. Its idea is simple: data is kept in tables, each table holds one kind of thing, and tables are connected through shared values called keys. SQL (Structured Query Language) is the standard language used to ask questions of these tables. It is an ISO standard, but each database product, such as PostgreSQL, SQL Server, MySQL or Oracle, adds its own small differences, called dialects.

Each table has a primary key. In a customers table this is usually customer_id. No two customers can share the same customer_id, even if two shops have the same name. A foreign key is a column in one table that holds the primary key of another table. The orders table contains customer_id, which tells you which customer placed each order. This link is what lets you combine tables later in the course.

Storing customers once and pointing to them with a key avoids repeating the same name and address on thousands of orders. If a customer moves from Viana to Cacuaco, you change one row, not thousands.

::: quiz
? In the orders table, customer_id appears many times. What kind of key is it in that table?
- A primary key || A primary key cannot repeat inside its own table. Here customer_id repeats.
* A foreign key || Correct. It points to the primary key of the customers table.
- A duplicate error || Repeating a foreign key is normal: one customer places many orders.
:::

## 2. The sample sales schema and its grain

This course uses one sample schema that looks like a typical food and household goods distributor:

- customers: customer_id, customer_name, segment (wholesale, supermarket, retail), province, city
- products: product_id, product_name, category (rice, sugar, flour, cooking oil), pack_size, list_price
- orders: order_id, customer_id, order_date, status (delivered, cancelled, pending), salesperson
- order_lines: order_line_id, order_id, product_id, quantity, unit_price, discount_pct

The relationships are one-to-many. One customer has many orders, and one order has many order lines. Each order line refers to one product.

The grain is what one row represents. In orders the grain is one order. In order_lines the grain is one product on one order. This matters because revenue lives at line level. An order line for 40 bags of rice at 18,500 Kz per bag has a line amount of 40 × 18,500 = 740,000 Kz. If the same order also has 25 bags of sugar at 21,000 Kz (525,000 Kz), the order total is 740,000 + 525,000 = 1,265,000 Kz. Order totals are built by adding line amounts, never the other way round.

## 3. The analytics workflow

Good SQL starts before you type. Use these five steps for every request.

::: diagram steps | The analytics workflow
Ask: turn the request into a precise question with a measure, a period and filters
Find: identify the tables, columns and grain that hold the answer
Query: write the SQL, starting small and building up
Check: compare with a known total and look for gaps or duplicates
Communicate: give the answer, the reason and a recommended action
:::

A precise question names the measure (revenue, quantity, number of orders), the period (October 2026), and the filters (delivered orders only, Benguela province). "How are sales?" is not a question you can query. "What was delivered revenue in Benguela in October 2026, by product category?" is.

The check step protects your reputation. Compare your total with a figure people trust, such as the invoicing report. Count the rows. Look at the smallest and largest values. A total that is exactly double the expected figure usually means rows were duplicated.

## Worked example: a food distributor in Luanda

A food distributor in Luanda supplies supermarkets and wholesalers in several provinces. The sales manager, Teresa, asks the analyst, Miguel: "How did supermarkets do last month?" Miguel rewrites it as: "Delivered revenue from customers in the supermarket segment, for October 2026, by province." He finds segment and province in customers, the date and status in orders, and quantity and unit price in order_lines. He notes that revenue must be added at line level and that cancelled orders must be excluded. After writing the query, he compares the grand total with the invoicing report for the same month before sending the result.

::: case | A vague request at the Luanda office
Teresa asks Miguel for "supermarket sales" by tomorrow morning. The orders table includes 312 cancelled orders in October, and the invoicing report shows 1.84 billion Kz of delivered sales for all segments.
? What should Miguel do first?
- Start writing SQL on the orders table straight away || Without a precise question he may include cancelled orders or use the wrong period.
* Confirm the measure, period and filters, then map them to tables and columns || Correct. A precise question tells him exactly which data and filters to use.
- Export all four tables to Excel and decide later || This moves the problem elsewhere and risks errors with large data.
- Send the total of all orders, including cancelled ones || Cancelled orders are not sales and would overstate the result.
:::

- Business question in one sentence
- Measure: revenue, quantity, orders, customers or margin
- Period: start date and end date
- Filters: status, segment, province, category
- Breakdown: by month, province, product or salesperson
- Tables and columns needed, with the grain of each
- Check figure: a trusted total to compare against

## Common mistakes

- Writing SQL before the question has a measure, period and filters.
- Ignoring the grain and adding an order-level value across order lines.
- Including cancelled or pending orders in revenue.
- Sending a result without comparing it to a trusted total.

::: terms

Alias: a temporary name for a column or table, given with AS
Condition: a test in WHERE that is true or false for each row
NULL: a marker meaning the value is missing or unknown, not zero and not blank text

## 1. SELECT and FROM

Every query starts with two clauses. FROM says which table to read and SELECT says which columns to return. To list customers and their province:

SELECT customer_name, province
FROM customers

SELECT * returns every column. It is useful for a first look, but in reports you should name the columns you need. This makes the query faster, clearer and safer if the table changes.

You can calculate new columns and give them readable names with an alias:

SELECT order_id, product_id, quantity, unit_price, quantity * unit_price AS line_amount
FROM order_lines

For a line of 60 cartons of cooking oil at 14,200 Kz, line_amount is 60 × 14,200 = 852,000 Kz. If discount_pct is 5, the net amount is quantity * unit_price * (1 - discount_pct / 100.0), which is 852,000 × 0.95 = 809,400 Kz. Writing 100.0 rather than 100 avoids integer division in some databases, where 5 / 100 would give 0.

## 2. Filtering rows with WHERE

WHERE keeps only the rows for which the condition is true. Text and dates go in single quotes, numbers do not.

SELECT order_id, customer_id, order_date
FROM orders
WHERE status = 'delivered' AND order_date >= '2026-10-01' AND order_date < '2026-11-01'

The main operators are:

- Comparison: =, <> (not equal), >, <, >=, <=
- IN for a list: WHERE province IN ('Luanda', 'Benguela', 'Huíla')
- BETWEEN for an inclusive range: WHERE quantity BETWEEN 10 AND 50
- LIKE for patterns, where % means any characters: WHERE product_name LIKE 'Rice%'
- IS NULL and IS NOT NULL for missing values

NULL needs special care. A condition such as salesperson = NULL is never true, because NULL means unknown. Always write salesperson IS NULL.

AND is evaluated before OR. This query looks correct but is not:

WHERE status = 'delivered' AND province = 'Luanda' OR province = 'Bengo'

It returns delivered orders in Luanda plus all orders in Bengo, including cancelled ones. The safe version uses brackets:

WHERE status = 'delivered' AND (province = 'Luanda' OR province = 'Bengo')

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Write the business filter in words | Delivered orders only; October 2026; Luanda or Bengo | Words first, SQL second
SLIDE Step 2: Translate each part | status = 'delivered'; order_date >= '2026-10-01' AND order_date < '2026-11-01'; province IN ('Luanda', 'Bengo') | One condition per business rule
SLIDE Step 3: Join with AND | Combine all rules with AND; Put any OR inside brackets or use IN | Brackets make intent explicit
SLIDE Step 4: Test on a known case | Pick one order you know should appear; Pick one that should not | Prove the filter works
:::

? Which condition correctly finds customers with no salesperson recorded?
- WHERE salesperson = NULL || Comparing with NULL is never true, so no rows are returned.
- WHERE salesperson = '' || This finds empty text, which is different from a missing value.
* WHERE salesperson IS NULL || Correct. IS NULL is the only reliable test for missing values.

## 3. Sorting, limiting and the logical processing order

ORDER BY sorts the result. ASC is ascending (the default) and DESC is descending. You can sort on several columns: ORDER BY province ASC, line_amount DESC sorts by province, then by amount within each province.

To return only the first rows, the syntax depends on the dialect:

- PostgreSQL, MySQL and SQLite: LIMIT 10 at the end
- SQL Server: SELECT TOP 10 at the start
- Standard SQL, also supported by PostgreSQL and Oracle: FETCH FIRST 10 ROWS ONLY

A limit without ORDER BY returns any 10 rows, not the biggest 10. The database does not promise any order unless you ask for one.

The database does not run the clauses in the order you write them. Understanding the logical processing order explains many errors.

::: diagram steps | Logical processing order of a simple query
FROM: read the table
WHERE: keep only rows that meet the conditions
SELECT: compute the columns and aliases
ORDER BY: sort the result
LIMIT: keep the first rows
:::

Because WHERE runs before SELECT, you cannot use a column alias such as line_amount inside WHERE in most databases. Repeat the expression instead: WHERE quantity * unit_price > 1000000. ORDER BY runs after SELECT, so ORDER BY line_amount DESC works.

::: quiz
? An analyst writes a query with LIMIT 10 but no ORDER BY to find the ten largest order lines. What is the problem?
- LIMIT only works with SELECT * || LIMIT works with any column list.
* The database may return any ten rows, not the largest ones || Correct. Without ORDER BY there is no guaranteed order.
- LIMIT must come before WHERE || LIMIT is written last, after ORDER BY.
:::

## Worked example: a top 10 list in Benguela

A distributor's branch in Benguela wants the ten largest order lines delivered in October 2026 for flour, to check pricing. The branch analyst, Rosa, works from a prepared table called sales_lines that already contains order_date, status, province, category, customer_name, quantity and unit_price.

SELECT customer_name, order_date, quantity, unit_price, quantity * unit_price AS line_amount
FROM sales_lines
WHERE status = 'delivered' AND province = 'Benguela' AND category = 'flour' AND order_date >= '2026-10-01' AND order_date < '2026-11-01'
ORDER BY line_amount DESC
LIMIT 10

She checks the first row by hand: 120 bags at 16,000 Kz is 1,920,000 Kz, which matches the invoice.

::: case | The top customers list that included Bengo
Rosa's colleague Paulo sends a list of "delivered sales in Luanda or Bengo". A manager notices a cancelled order of 3,400,000 Kz from Bengo on the list. Paulo's WHERE clause is status = 'delivered' AND province = 'Luanda' OR province = 'Bengo'.
? What is the best fix?
- Delete the cancelled row from the exported file || The query is still wrong and will fail again next month.
* Put brackets around the OR conditions or use province IN ('Luanda', 'Bengo') || Correct. AND is evaluated before OR, so the status filter did not apply to Bengo.
- Change AND to OR everywhere || This would return even more wrong rows.
- Add ORDER BY status || Sorting does not change which rows are returned.
:::

## 1. Aggregate functions

Managers rarely want thousands of rows. They want totals, counts and averages. In this lesson we use a prepared reporting view called sales_lines. It combines the four tables from Lesson 1 into one row per order line, with order_id, order_date, status, customer_id, province, segment, category and line_amount. Lesson 4 shows how such a view is built with joins.

The five standard aggregate functions are:

- COUNT(*): number of rows
- COUNT(column): number of rows where that column is not NULL
- COUNT(DISTINCT column): number of different values
- SUM(column) and AVG(column): total and average, ignoring NULLs
- MIN(column) and MAX(column): smallest and largest values

SELECT COUNT(*) AS lines, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders, COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS customers, SUM(line_amount) AS revenue
FROM sales_lines
WHERE status = 'delivered'

The difference between COUNT(*) and COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) is important. If 150 customers placed 1,200 orders with 4,800 lines, COUNT(*) gives 4,800, not the number of customers.

::: quiz
? A manager asks how many different customers bought in October. Which expression answers this from sales_lines?
- COUNT(*) || This counts order lines, which is much higher than the number of customers.
- SUM(customer_id) || Adding ID numbers gives a meaningless figure.
* COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) || Correct. It counts each customer once, however many lines they have.
:::

## 2. GROUP BY and the sales-by-province report

GROUP BY splits the rows into groups, calculates the aggregates for each group and combines the results into one row per group. This is often called split, apply, combine.

::: diagram steps | How a GROUP BY query works
Filter rows: WHERE keeps delivered orders in the period
Split: rows are grouped by province
Apply: SUM and COUNT are calculated for each group
Filter groups: HAVING removes groups that fail a test
Sort: ORDER BY puts the biggest provinces first
:::

The rule to remember: every column in SELECT that is not inside an aggregate function must appear in GROUP BY. Here is a sales-by-province report for October 2026:

SELECT province, SUM(line_amount) AS revenue, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders, SUM(line_amount) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS avg_order_value
FROM sales_lines
WHERE status = 'delivered' AND order_date >= '2026-10-01' AND order_date < '2026-11-01'
GROUP BY province
ORDER BY revenue DESC

You can group by more than one column, for example GROUP BY province, category, to get one row for each combination.

SLIDE Step 2: Decide the measures | Revenue with SUM; Orders with COUNT(DISTINCT order_id); Average order value as a ratio | Each measure is an aggregate
SLIDE Step 3: Filter the rows | Delivered status; Period with start and end date | WHERE before grouping

## 3. WHERE versus HAVING, and averages at the right grain

WHERE filters individual rows before grouping. HAVING filters whole groups after the aggregates are calculated. You cannot write WHERE SUM(line_amount) > 50000000, because the sum does not exist yet when WHERE runs. Use HAVING:

GROUP BY province
HAVING SUM(line_amount) > 50000000

Use WHERE for row rules, such as status and date, and HAVING for group rules, such as "provinces with more than 20 orders".

Averages need care. AVG(line_amount) gives the average line, not the average order. Suppose Huambo had three orders worth 1,200,000 Kz, 800,000 Kz and 400,000 Kz, made of 2, 3 and 1 lines. Revenue is 2,400,000 Kz. Average order value is 2,400,000 / 3 = 800,000 Kz. AVG(line_amount) would give 2,400,000 / 6 = 400,000 Kz, half the true figure. Always divide the sum by the count of the thing you want to average.

Share of total helps managers compare provinces. If Luanda has 1,100,000,000 Kz out of 1,840,000,000 Kz in total, its share is 1,100,000,000 / 1,840,000,000 × 100 = 59.8%. Check that all shares add up to 100%.

::: quiz
? You want only categories with more than 100 orders in the month. Where does the condition COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) > 100 go?
- In WHERE || WHERE runs before grouping, so the count does not exist yet.
* In HAVING || Correct. HAVING filters groups after the aggregates are calculated.
- In ORDER BY || ORDER BY sorts groups but does not remove any.
:::

## 1. INNER JOIN and LEFT JOIN

In Lesson 1 the data was split across four tables. A join brings it back together. You state the tables and how their keys match:

SELECT o.order_id, o.order_date, c.customer_name, c.province
FROM orders o
INNER JOIN customers c ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id

The letters o and c are table aliases. They keep the query short and tell the database which table each column comes from. Without them, customer_id would be ambiguous because both tables have it.

INNER JOIN keeps only rows with a match on both sides. An order whose customer_id does not exist in customers disappears. In most databases JOIN on its own means INNER JOIN.

LEFT JOIN keeps every row of the left table, the first one named, and fills the right-side columns with NULL when there is no match. This is how you find customers who did not buy:

SELECT c.customer_id, c.customer_name
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id AND o.order_date >= '2026-10-01' AND o.order_date < '2026-11-01'
WHERE o.order_id IS NULL

This is often called an anti-join, and it gives the sales team a ready call list.

There are two other join types. RIGHT JOIN is a LEFT JOIN with the tables swapped, and FULL OUTER JOIN keeps unmatched rows from both sides. MySQL does not support FULL OUTER JOIN directly.

::: diagram matrix | Join types | Keeps unmatched rows of the right table (no → yes) | Keeps unmatched rows of the left table (no → yes)
LEFT JOIN: all left rows, matching right rows only
FULL OUTER JOIN: all rows from both tables
INNER JOIN: matching rows only
RIGHT JOIN: all right rows, matching left rows only
:::

::: quiz
? You need a list of all products, including those that never sold, with their total quantity sold. Which join do you use from products to order_lines?
- INNER JOIN || Products with no sales would disappear from the list.
* LEFT JOIN from products to order_lines || Correct. All products are kept, and those without sales show NULL, which you can turn into 0.
- No join, only the products table || Quantities sold are in order_lines, so a join is needed.
:::

## 2. Building the full sales view

To rebuild the sales_lines view used in Lesson 3, join all four tables, starting from the table with the finest grain:

SELECT ol.order_line_id, o.order_id, o.order_date, o.status, c.customer_name, c.province, c.segment, p.category, ol.quantity * ol.unit_price AS line_amount
FROM order_lines ol
INNER JOIN orders o ON ol.order_id = o.order_id
INNER JOIN customers c ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
INNER JOIN products p ON ol.product_id = p.product_id

The grain stays at one row per order line, because each line matches exactly one order, one customer and one product. Joining from the many side to the one side keeps the grain.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Start from the finest grain | Begin with order_lines; Note the row count | The base table sets the grain
SLIDE Step 2: Join one table at a time | Add orders, then customers, then products; Write each ON with both keys | Small steps, easy checks
SLIDE Step 3: Count again | Row count should stay the same; A higher count means a key is not unique | Same rows, same grain
SLIDE Step 4: Reconcile the total | SUM(line_amount) should match the invoicing report | Numbers prove the join
:::

## 3. Pitfalls: duplicated rows and the LEFT JOIN trap

Row multiplication happens when you join from the one side to the many side and then add a value stored on the one side. Suppose orders has a delivery_fee column. Order 5021 has a fee of 15,000 Kz and four lines. After joining orders to order_lines, the fee appears on four rows. SUM(delivery_fee) gives 4 × 15,000 = 60,000 Kz instead of 15,000 Kz. Fix this by summing the fee from orders alone, or by aggregating lines to order level first and then joining.

The same happens when the one side is not unique. If a price table has an old and a new row for one product, every sale of that product is doubled.

The LEFT JOIN trap: a condition on the right table placed in WHERE removes the NULL rows, so the LEFT JOIN behaves like an INNER JOIN. In the anti-join above, the date filter is in ON for this reason. If it were in WHERE, customers without October orders would be removed before you could find them.

::: quiz
? An analyst joins orders to order_lines and sums delivery_fee. The result is three times the finance figure. What is the most likely cause?
- The database rounds fees || Rounding cannot triple a total.
- The orders table has missing fees || Missing fees would lower the total, not raise it.
* Each fee is repeated on every line of its order || Correct. Order-level values must be summed before joining to lines.
:::

## Worked example: inactive customers in Huíla

A distributor's branch in Lubango wants a list of Huíla wholesale customers who ordered in September 2026 but not in October. The analyst, Ana, first selects customers with a September order, using an INNER JOIN to orders with the September date range and SELECT DISTINCT c.customer_id. Their September purchases totalled 41,000,000 Kz, which shows the sales manager the value at risk.

? What should he check first?
- Whether October had unusually high sales || The gap is too large and the table counts point to a data problem.
- Whether the costs are in USD || A currency mix-up is possible but the row counts give a clearer signal.
* Whether product_id is unique in the price table || Correct. 1,180 rows for 950 products means some products have several rows, which duplicates sales lines.
:::

## 1. Classifying with CASE WHEN

Business reports use categories that are not stored in the database: order size bands, customer tiers, on-time or late. CASE WHEN creates them in the query:

SELECT order_id, order_total,
CASE WHEN order_total >= 5000000 THEN 'Large'
WHEN order_total >= 1000000 THEN 'Medium'
ELSE 'Small' END AS order_band
FROM order_totals

The conditions are tested from top to bottom and the first true one wins. An order of 6,000,000 Kz is true for both the first and second conditions, but it becomes Large because that condition comes first. If you put the 1,000,000 Kz test first, every large order would be called Medium. Always order bands from the most specific or highest down.

CASE also works inside aggregates, which lets you build several measures in one query. SUM(CASE WHEN category = 'rice' THEN line_amount ELSE 0 END) AS rice_revenue gives rice revenue next to total revenue on the same row.

::: quiz
? A CASE expression tests order_total >= 1000000 first and order_total >= 5000000 second. What happens to an order of 7,000,000 Kz?
* It is classified by the first condition, as Medium || Correct. The first true condition wins, so the order of tests matters.
- It is classified as Large because that value is higher || CASE does not look for the best match, only the first true one.
- The query returns an error || The syntax is valid, but the logic is wrong.
:::

## 2. Missing values, zero values and inconsistent text

COALESCE returns the first non-NULL value. COALESCE(discount_pct, 0) treats a missing discount as zero, which is correct if the business rule says no discount was given. COALESCE(salesperson, 'Unassigned') makes missing salespeople visible in a report instead of a blank. Decide on purpose: replacing an unknown cost with 0 would make margins look better than they are.

NULLIF prevents division by zero. Margin percentage is (revenue - cost) / revenue × 100. If a product had no revenue, dividing by zero causes an error in most databases. Write:

SELECT product_id, (revenue - cost) * 100.0 / NULLIF(revenue, 0) AS margin_pct
FROM product_margins

For a product with revenue of 2,500,000 Kz and cost of 2,150,000 Kz, margin is 350,000 / 2,500,000 × 100 = 14%. For a product with zero revenue, the result is NULL instead of an error.

Text often arrives in several spellings: 'Luanda', 'LUANDA', ' luanda'. Use TRIM to remove leading and trailing spaces and UPPER or LOWER to fix case: UPPER(TRIM(province)). Map remaining variants, such as 'LDA', with CASE WHEN or, better, a small lookup table that the team maintains.

::: diagram steps | A cleaning sequence for a sales extract
Profile: count NULLs and distinct values in each key column
Standardise: TRIM and UPPER text, CAST numbers and dates
Fill: COALESCE missing values where a business rule allows it
Classify: build bands and groups with CASE WHEN
Validate: reconcile totals and row counts with the source
:::

## 3. Working with dates

Dates are the most common source of reporting errors. Two methods prevent most of them.

First, use half-open ranges. Many order_date columns are timestamps with a time of day. BETWEEN '2026-10-01' AND '2026-10-31' includes only the first second of 31 October, so orders later that day are lost. Write:

WHERE order_date >= '2026-10-01' AND order_date < '2026-11-01'

This works for dates and timestamps, and the next period always starts where the last one ended.

Second, group by the start of the month. The function differs by dialect:

- PostgreSQL and Snowflake: DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date)
- SQL Server 2022 and later: DATETRUNC(month, order_date), and in older versions DATEFROMPARTS(YEAR(order_date), MONTH(order_date), 1)
- MySQL: DATE_FORMAT(order_date, '%Y-%m-01')
- Standard SQL: EXTRACT(YEAR FROM order_date) and EXTRACT(MONTH FROM order_date), grouped together

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Check the column type | Is order_date a date or a timestamp; Look at a few values | Type decides the method
SLIDE Step 2: Write a half-open range | order_date >= '2026-10-01'; order_date < '2026-11-01' | Start inclusive, end exclusive
SLIDE Step 3: Truncate to month | Use your dialect's function; Give it an alias such as sales_month | One row per month
SLIDE Step 4: Group and sort | GROUP BY the truncated month; ORDER BY sales_month | Ready for trends
:::

::: quiz
? Order_date is a timestamp. Which filter returns all orders placed in October 2026?
- order_date BETWEEN '2026-10-01' AND '2026-10-31' || This misses orders placed after midnight at the start of 31 October.
* order_date >= '2026-10-01' AND order_date < '2026-11-01' || Correct. The half-open range includes every moment of October.
- order_date = '2026-10' || A month is not a single date value.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
CTE: a common table expression, a named temporary result defined with WITH and used in the main query
Window function: a function that calculates across a set of rows related to the current row, without grouping them into one row
PARTITION BY: splits rows into groups for a window function
LAG: returns a value from a previous row in the window, such as last month's revenue
Running total: a cumulative sum that adds each new row to all previous rows

## 1. Common table expressions

Real analysis has several steps: clean, summarise, compare. Nesting queries inside each other quickly becomes unreadable. A CTE gives each step a name:

WITH monthly AS (
SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date) AS sales_month, SUM(line_amount) AS revenue
FROM sales_lines
WHERE status = 'delivered'
GROUP BY DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date)
)
SELECT sales_month, revenue
FROM monthly
ORDER BY sales_month

You can define several CTEs separated by commas, each using the ones before it. Because each step has a name, you can run and check it on its own. CTEs are part of standard SQL and are supported by PostgreSQL, SQL Server, Oracle and MySQL 8.0 and later.

::: quiz
? Why do analysts use CTEs for multi-step analysis?
- They make queries run faster in every database || Speed depends on the database and the query.
* They split a long query into named steps that are easier to read and check || Correct. Each step can be tested on its own.
- They permanently store results in a new table || A CTE exists only while the query runs.
:::

## 2. Window functions: ranking and running totals

GROUP BY collapses rows into one row per group. A window function keeps every row and adds a calculation across related rows. The OVER clause defines the window: PARTITION BY says which rows belong together, and ORDER BY sets the sequence inside each group. Window functions were added to the SQL standard in 2003 and are supported by all major databases, including MySQL from version 8.0.

To find the top three products by revenue in each province:

WITH product_sales AS (
SELECT province, product_name, SUM(line_amount) AS revenue
FROM sales_lines
GROUP BY province, product_name
),
ranked AS (
SELECT province, product_name, revenue, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY province ORDER BY revenue DESC) AS rn
FROM product_sales
)
SELECT province, product_name, revenue
FROM ranked
WHERE rn <= 3

ROW_NUMBER gives 1, 2, 3 with no ties. RANK gives tied rows the same number and then skips: 1, 2, 2, 4. DENSE_RANK does not skip: 1, 2, 2, 3. You filter on the rank in an outer query because window functions are calculated after WHERE.

A running total adds each month to the months before it: SUM(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY sales_month) AS cumulative_revenue. Add PARTITION BY EXTRACT(YEAR FROM sales_month) to restart it every January and get year-to-date revenue.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Summarise in a CTE | Revenue by month or by product; One row per item you will compare | Window functions work best on summaries
SLIDE Step 2: Define the window | PARTITION BY the group, such as province; ORDER BY the measure or the month | Who is compared with whom
SLIDE Step 3: Choose the function | ROW_NUMBER or RANK for top lists; SUM OVER for running totals; LAG for previous values | One function per question
SLIDE Step 4: Filter in an outer query | WHERE rn <= 3; Window results cannot be filtered in the same WHERE | Rank first, filter after
:::

## 3. Month-on-month growth

LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY sales_month) returns the revenue of the previous row, which is last month. Growth is then:

growth % = (this month - last month) / last month × 100

WITH monthly AS (...),
compared AS (
SELECT sales_month, revenue, LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY sales_month) AS prev_revenue
FROM monthly
)
SELECT sales_month, revenue, prev_revenue, (revenue - prev_revenue) * 100.0 / NULLIF(prev_revenue, 0) AS mom_growth_pct
FROM compared

If September revenue was 1,600,000,000 Kz and October 1,840,000,000 Kz, growth is (1,840,000,000 - 1,600,000,000) / 1,600,000,000 × 100 = 15%. The first month has no previous month, so its growth is NULL. Make sure no month is missing from the data, or LAG will compare with the wrong month. Month-on-month changes can reflect seasonality and working days, so also compare with the same month last year where data allows.

::: quiz
? September revenue was 800,000,000 Kz and October revenue was 720,000,000 Kz. What is the month-on-month growth?
- +10% || The direction is wrong. Revenue fell.
* -10% || Correct. (720 - 800) / 800 × 100 = -10%.
- -11.1% || This divides by October instead of September. The base is the previous month.
:::

## 4. Turning results into an insight

Managers need the answer, not the query. The Pyramid Principle, developed by Barbara Minto, recommends starting with the main message and then giving the supporting points.

::: diagram pyramid | An answer-first insight
Main message: the answer to the question in one sentence
Supporting facts: two or three numbers that prove it
Data and method: source, period and filters used
:::

A good insight has three parts: what happened, why, and what to do next. Keep it short and round the numbers.

## Worked example: October performance note

A distributor in Luanda sells rice, sugar, flour and cooking oil in five provinces. The commercial director asks why October was strong. The analyst, Sílvia, runs the monthly CTE with LAG and finds 15% month-on-month growth. A second query with ROW_NUMBER by province shows that 5 kg rice packs are the top product in three provinces. She writes: "October delivered revenue rose 15% to 1.84 billion Kz. Most of the increase came from rice in Luanda and Benguela, where two new wholesale customers placed large orders. We recommend confirming their November volumes and checking rice stock cover for the next six weeks."

::: case | The growth figure for the board
Sílvia's colleague reports that revenue "grew 40% in October". Checking his query, she sees that September is missing from the monthly CTE because of a system migration, so LAG compared October with August.
? What should Sílvia recommend?
- Send the 40% figure because it is technically correct for the data || The data is incomplete, so the comparison is misleading.
* Restore the missing month or label the comparison clearly as October versus August || Correct. LAG takes the previous row, so a missing month gives a wrong base.
