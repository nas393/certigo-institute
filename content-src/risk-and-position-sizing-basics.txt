## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- name the main types of trading risk and give a workplace example of each;
- set a risk budget as a fixed percentage of account equity, in cash and in percent;
- set a total open-risk limit that covers every open position;
- explain why the maximum loss must be decided before entering a trade.

## Key terms

::: terms
Risk budget: the share of account equity you accept losing on one trade or in one period
Equity: the value of your account, including open profit or loss
Gap risk: the risk that price jumps past your stop before you can exit at the planned level
Leverage: borrowed exposure that makes a position larger than your own money
Margin call: a broker request for more funds, or the closing of positions, when equity falls too low
Counterparty risk: the risk that your broker or another party cannot meet its obligations
:::

## 1. The main types of trading risk

Market risk is the chance that prices move against your position. Liquidity risk is the chance that you cannot exit at the price you expected. Gap risk is the chance that price jumps past your exit, often over a weekend or after news. Leverage risk is the chance that borrowed exposure turns a small move into a large loss. Counterparty risk is the chance that your broker or another party fails to meet its obligations, which is why course 1 asked you to check regulation and how client money is held. Operational risk is the chance of your own error, such as a wrong size, a wrong instrument or a platform outage.

Name each risk first, then decide its control.

::: diagram cycle | The risk management cycle
Identify: list every risk the trade carries
Measure: estimate the size of the loss each risk could cause
Limit: set a rule that caps the loss before you enter
Review: check the outcome against the rule and adjust by evidence
:::

## 2. Set a risk budget

A risk budget turns a feeling into a number. Pick a fixed percentage of account equity that you accept losing on any single trade. A common rule of thumb for new traders is one to two percent, but the right figure depends on your experience and on how much you can lose without changing your life. Write it down before you trade, then calculate the cash amount. On a 10,000 USD account, one percent is 100 USD. If your account holds kwanzas, convert it to USD first and count the conversion cost from course 1.

Add a second limit for the total open risk. This is the sum of the cash you would lose if every open position hit its stop at the same time. Set a ceiling for it, and check it before you open a new trade.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Measure your equity | Use the current account value; Include open profit or loss; Recalculate at the start of each week | Equity is the base
SLIDE Step 2: Choose a percentage | Pick one fixed figure; Keep it the same across trades; Do not raise it after wins | One number, every trade
SLIDE Step 3: Convert to cash | Multiply equity by the percentage; Write the cash limit on your plan | Know the cash figure before entry
:::

* 100 USD, one percent of the account value || One percent of 10,000 USD is 100 USD, which is the maximum planned loss on a single trade.
- 10 USD, because the budget is measured in tenths of a percent || The budget is one percent of equity, not one tenth of one percent.

## 3. Decide the risk before you enter

Risk is the amount you lose if your idea is wrong and your exit works as planned. A loss can be larger if a gap or a broker problem stops you from exiting at the planned price. That is why the budget must be decided before you enter, and why it should leave room for error. A trader who decides the risk during a trade is usually reacting to fear or hope. Before you enter, write down the maximum loss, the exit that triggers it and what would prove the idea wrong.

::: quiz
? Why should the maximum loss be decided before entering a trade?
- Because the price always moves in the expected direction after entry || Prices do not always move as expected, and this is not the reason for deciding early.
* Because decisions made during a trade tend to reflect fear or hope rather than the plan || A plan made before entry protects against emotional choices made under pressure.
- Because brokers only accept orders with a risk figure attached || Brokers do not require a risk figure. The reason is to protect your own decisions.
:::

## Worked example: Rui and his open trades

Rui trades from Luanda with a USD account of 8,000. He sets a risk budget of 1.5 percent per trade, which is 120 USD. His total open-risk limit is 5 percent, which is 400 USD. He already has three open positions, each risking 120 USD, so his total open risk is 360 USD, or 4.5 percent of equity. A fourth setup appears that he likes. Adding it would bring his total open risk to 480 USD, which is 6 percent. The rule says no, so he waits. He can take the setup next week when one of the three trades closes.
::: case | The fourth setup
Rui's three open positions each risk 120 USD on an 8,000 USD account. He sees a fourth setup that looks strong, and he feels sure it will work.
? What should Rui do?
- Open the trade and move the stop closer to keep the total risk down || Moving a stop closer to fit a rule can close the trade before its idea has room to work. That is a different decision, made for the wrong reason.
* Wait, because the trade would break his total open-risk limit, and the limit was set before this setup appeared || The limit exists for the moments when a trader feels sure. Keeping to it is the discipline.
- Open the trade and promise himself he will close one of the others later || A promise to act later is not a limit. It leaves the account exposed right now.
:::

- Account equity today, including open profit or loss
- Risk budget per trade, as a percentage and in cash
- Total open-risk limit, as a percentage and in cash
- Maximum loss for the day or the week before I stop trading
- What would prove this trade idea wrong

## Common mistakes

- Setting the risk budget after seeing a setup, which turns the rule into a justification.
- Treating the budget as a target to reach rather than a maximum to stay under.
- Counting only one trade and ignoring the total open risk.
- Changing the percentage after a run of wins or losses.

## Summary

- Risk has several types, including market, gap, leverage, liquidity, counterparty and operational risk, and each needs its own control.
- A risk budget is a fixed percentage of equity, set in advance, with both a per-trade limit and a total open-risk limit.
- The maximum loss is decided before you enter, and the limit holds even when a setup looks certain.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- calculate the position size for a trade from a fixed cash risk and a stop distance;
- distinguish position value from the cash you can lose on a trade;
- round a position size correctly for the instrument you trade;

## Key terms

::: terms
Position size: the number of units you buy or sell in one trade
Risk per unit: the distance between your entry price and your stop price, in money
Position value: the number of units multiplied by the entry price
Lot size: the smallest quantity an instrument or broker allows you to trade
Round down: reduce a calculated size to the nearest allowed quantity, never up
:::

## 1. The fixed-risk sizing formula

The fixed-risk method lets the stop decide how far price can move against you, and the size decides how much money that move costs. You start with the cash you are willing to lose, which comes from your risk budget. Then you divide it by the loss on each unit. For a buy trade, the loss on each unit is the entry price minus the stop price. The result is the number of units you can hold without exceeding the budget.

In plain form, the formula is: position size in units equals maximum cash loss divided by risk per unit. Take the result and round it down to the nearest allowed quantity. Rounding up pushes the loss above the budget, and the rule exists to prevent exactly that.

::: diagram steps | Position sizing steps
Set the cash risk: multiply equity by the risk percentage
Set the stop: choose the exit price that proves the idea wrong
Find risk per unit: subtract the stop from the entry for a buy
Divide and round down: maximum loss divided by risk per unit, rounded down
:::

## 2. Check the position value, not just the risk

Sizing controls the loss, not the amount invested. A trade can risk only 1 percent of equity and still tie up half the account, if the stop is close and the position value is large. So check both numbers. Position value is units multiplied by the entry price. Risk is units multiplied by the risk per unit. If the position value does not fit the funds in your account, the trade is too large, even when the risk looks small.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Set the cash risk | Multiply equity by the risk percentage; Write the figure on the plan | Start from the budget
SLIDE Step 2: Divide by risk per unit | Entry minus stop for a buy; Maximum loss divided by that figure; Round down | Divide, then round down
SLIDE Step 3: Check the position value | Units times entry price; Does it fit the funds available? | Small risk can still mean a large cost

* 80 units, because 120 divided by 1.50 gives 80 || The cash risk is 120 USD and the risk per unit is 1.50, so the size is 80 units.
- 53 units, because 8,000 divided by 150 gives 53 || The budget is 1.5 percent of 8,000, not 150. Recalculate the cash risk first.

## 3. Round down and check the lot size

Instruments trade in whole units or in set lot sizes. A calculated size of 62.5 units may have to become 62 units, or a lot size may force a smaller figure. Always round down. If the broker's minimum size is larger than your calculated size, the trade is too large for your budget. Skip the trade, or choose an instrument where the minimum fits. Do not trade a larger size to make it fit. Keep a short list of the minimum size and the lot step for every instrument you trade.

::: quiz
? A calculation gives 62.5 units, and the broker only accepts whole units. What should you do?
- Round up to 63 units, because the risk is close enough || Rounding up raises the loss above the budget. The rule is to round down.
* Round down to 62 units, so the loss stays within the budget || Rounding down keeps the loss at or below the cash limit.
- Trade 100 units and accept a larger loss this time || A larger size breaks the rule. The purpose of the rule is to prevent that.
:::

## Worked example: Helena's trade in Benguela

Helena is a part-time trader in Benguela with a USD account of 5,000. Her risk budget is 1 percent, which is 50 USD. She wants to buy a share at 25.00, and she has set her stop at 24.20, below the level where her idea fails. The risk per unit is 25.00 minus 24.20, which is 0.80. Dividing 50 by 0.80 gives 62.5 units. She rounds down to 62 units. Her maximum loss is 62 multiplied by 0.80, which is 49.60 USD, inside her budget. The position value is 62 multiplied by 25.00, which is 1,550 USD, or 31 percent of her account. The risk is small, but the exposure is large, so she checks her broker's margin rules before placing the order.

::: case | Two good weeks
Carlos has had two profitable weeks. His account is up 12 percent, and his plan sets 1 percent per trade. He feels confident and thinks he could double his size on the next setup to make the gains faster.
? What should Carlos do?
- Double the position size, because the last two weeks show the method works || Two good weeks are a small sample. Doubling the size doubles the loss if the next trade fails.
* Keep the same percentage, so the cash budget grows with his equity as the plan allows || A fixed percentage lets the cash risk grow as equity grows, and that keeps the rule intact.
- Tighten the stop and keep the same size, so the trade looks safer || A tighter stop can close the trade before its idea has room to work. Set the stop from the chart, then size from the stop.
:::

## Learning objectives

## Key terms

::: terms
Invalidation level: the price at which the reason for the trade is proved wrong
Stop-loss order: an order that exits a position once a set price is reached
Buffer: a small distance added beyond a level, so normal price movement does not trigger the stop
Trailing stop: a stop that moves in the trade's favour by a fixed rule as price moves
Mental stop: an exit kept in your head, with no order placed with the broker
Noise: small random price moves that do not change the direction of the trade

## 1. Start from the reason for the trade

A stop is not a number you pick because it feels comfortable. It is the price at which the reason for entering the trade stops being true. If you buy because price has held above a support area, the invalidation level sits below that area, since a clean break through it means the reason has gone. The stop goes beyond the invalidation level, with a small buffer, so ordinary noise does not close the trade before its idea has a chance to work. Write the reason, the invalidation level and the buffer before you enter.

::: slides

SLIDE Step 3: Add a buffer and place the order | Place the stop beyond the level; Choose the buffer from normal movement; Send the order with the entry | Plan it, then place it

## 2. Distance sets the size

With a fixed cash risk, the distance to the stop decides how many units you can hold. A wider stop gives fewer units, and a tighter stop gives more. Take a 120 USD risk budget. A stop 1.50 below entry gives 80 units. A stop 3.00 below entry gives 40 units. The cash loss is 120 USD in both cases if the stop works. Some traders widen a stop to keep a large position. That changes the risk, so the size must be recalculated, not left the same.

::: quiz
? With a fixed 120 USD risk, a trader moves the stop from 1.50 below entry to 3.00 below entry and keeps the same size of 80 units. What is the new risk?
- Still 120 USD, because the size did not change || The size did not change, but the loss did. 80 units at 3.00 is 240 USD.
* 240 USD, because 80 units multiplied by 3.00 is 240 || The loss doubles when the distance doubles and the size stays the same, which breaks the budget.
- 60 USD, because a wider stop lowers the risk || A wider stop raises the loss on the same size. It does not lower it.
:::

## 3. A stop is not a guarantee

A stop order becomes a market order when it is triggered. In a gap, price may open far beyond the stop, and the fill comes at the first price available. In a thin market, the fill can also be far away. A stop-limit order controls the fill price but may not fill at all, which can leave an open loss. The planned loss is a target for normal conditions, not a promise. Size the position for the worst fill you can survive, not for the stop price on the chart.

## Worked example: Sofia's long trade in Lobito

Sofia wants to buy a commodity-linked share at 12.40. Price has held above a support area near 11.90 for several sessions, and her reason is that the area has held twice before. Her invalidation level is a clean close below 11.90. She places her stop at 11.80, a buffer of 0.10 below the level. Her risk per unit is 12.40 minus 11.80, which is 0.60. With a 100 USD risk budget she can hold 166 units, since 100 divided by 0.60 is 166.67, rounded down.

Her loss if the stop works is 166 multiplied by 0.60, which is 99.60 USD. A week later, price gaps down through her stop after a weekend announcement and fills at 11.45. Her loss is 166 multiplied by 0.95, which is 157.70 USD. She had planned for a stop loss, but the gap cost more. That is why she keeps each position small enough to survive a fill like this one.

Tomas bought a share with a stop at 9.50. Price falls toward the stop. He thinks the market is about to turn, so he moves the stop down to 9.00 to give the trade more room.

- Keep the moved stop, because the price is likely to turn back || Moving a stop further from the price on a losing trade changes the risk after entry. The plan set 9.50.
* Restore the stop to 9.50, and exit if price closes through it, because the plan set that level before entry || A stop moved away from the plan is no longer the stop the plan allowed. The original rule decides.
- Add to the position to lower the average price || Adding to a losing trade raises the risk and breaks the plan without new evidence.
:::

::: quiz
? Which change to a stop is consistent with a rule-based plan?
- Moving the stop further from the price when the trade goes against you || Moving a stop further away on a losing trade increases the planned loss after entry.
* Moving the stop in the trade's favour by a rule set in advance, such as after a fixed gain || A rule set before entry that only moves the stop in the trade's favour keeps the risk under control.
- Removing the stop because the trade looks strong || Removing the stop removes the planned exit and leaves the loss open.

## Key terms

::: terms
Win rate: the share of trades that close with a profit
Average win: the mean profit on winning trades
Average loss: the mean loss on losing trades, shown as a positive figure
Reward-to-risk: the average win divided by the average loss
Expectancy: the average expected result per trade, from wins and losses together
Sample size: the number of trades in a record, which determines how far you can trust its average
:::

## 1. Win rate alone misleads

A trader who wins 70 percent of trades can still lose money if each loss is much larger than each win. A trader who wins 30 percent can make money if the winners are much larger than the losers. The win rate describes how often you are right. Reward-to-risk describes how much you gain when you are right, compared with what you lose when you are wrong. Both matter. The test that combines them is expectancy.

::: diagram matrix | Win rate and reward-to-risk | Win rate (low → high) | Reward-to-risk (low → high)
Trend-style profile: few wins, but large winners, which needs patience
Ideal but rare: many wins with large winners, which needs a strong edge
Weak profile: few wins and small winners, which rarely pays after costs
Scalp-style profile: many small wins, where one large loss can erase them
:::

## 2. Expectancy and the break-even win rate

Expectancy is the win rate multiplied by the average win, minus the loss rate multiplied by the average loss. The loss rate is one minus the win rate. Expectancy is the average result you can expect per trade if the record repeats. A positive expectancy matters more than a profit in a few good weeks.

Break-even is a useful companion check. For a reward-to-risk ratio R, the break-even win rate is 1 divided by (1 plus R). A ratio of 1.5 breaks even at 40 percent wins. A ratio of 2 breaks even at about 33 percent.

::: quiz
? A method wins 40 percent of trades. Its average win is 150 USD and its average loss is 100 USD. What is the expectancy per trade?
- 10 USD, because 150 minus 100 is 50, and 50 divided by 5 is 10 || Expectancy weights wins and losses by how often each occurs, so this division is not the method.
* 0 USD, because 0.4 times 150 minus 0.6 times 100 equals 60 minus 60 || With a 40 percent win rate and a ratio of 1.5, the method breaks even before costs.
- 60 USD, because only the average win matters || Expectancy also subtracts the losses. Ignoring them overstates the result.
:::

## 3. Costs and sample size

Costs come out of every trade. If your expectancy is 5 USD per trade and your costs are 5 USD per trade, your real expectancy is zero. Subtract spreads, commissions and expected slippage before you judge the method, as course 1 showed. Sample size matters as well. A few trades can look good or bad by chance. Keep a record over many trades before you draw a conclusion, and note the period and the market conditions in your record.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Count every trade | Include the losing trades; Note the dates and the market; Do not drop a bad month | Honest counting
SLIDE Step 2: Calculate expectancy | Win rate times average win; Minus loss rate times average loss; Loss rate is one minus win rate | The result per trade
SLIDE Step 3: Subtract the costs | Take off spread, commission and slippage; Judge only what remains | Costs come first
:::

## Worked example: Lucia's demo record

Lucia records her last 20 trades on a demo account. Seven were winners, so her win rate is 35 percent. Her average win is 200 USD and her average loss is 100 USD. Her expectancy is 0.35 times 200, which is 70, minus 0.65 times 100, which is 65. The result is 5 USD per trade. Her reward-to-risk ratio is 2, so the break-even win rate is about 33 percent, and she is just above it. Next she subtracts her average cost of 4 USD per trade. Her expectancy after costs is 1 USD per trade. That is too small to trust from 20 trades, so she keeps the record running and reviews it after more trades, rather than scaling up.

::: case | Scaling up too soon
Lucia's record shows an expectancy of 5 USD per trade before costs. A friend tells her to double her position size, because the method is working.
? What should Lucia do first?
- Double the size now, because a positive result means the method is profitable || A positive average before costs can disappear after costs, and 20 trades is a small sample.
* Subtract all costs from the expectancy, and keep recording until the sample is large enough to judge the method || Costs and sample size are the two checks that decide whether the edge is real.
- Stop recording, because a small sample says nothing useful || A small sample says little on its own, but the record is how she learns more. Stopping removes that learning.
:::

::: quiz

* 25 percent, because 1 divided by (1 plus 3) is 0.25 || With R equal to 3, the method breaks even when one trade in four is a win, before costs.
- 33 percent, because every ratio of 3 breaks even at one third || One third is the break-even for a ratio of 2. Recalculate with R equal to 3.

- Judging a method only by its win rate.
- Ignoring the average loss when the wins look attractive.
- Forgetting costs, and assuming a small positive expectancy is real profit.
- Drawing conclusions from a handful of trades.

## Self-check

1. What is expectancy? (Answer: the average expected result per trade, from wins and losses weighted by how often each occurs.)
2. A method has a reward-to-risk ratio of 1. What is its break-even win rate? (Answer: 50 percent, because 1 divided by (1 plus 1) is 0.5.)
3. Why is a positive expectancy before costs not enough? (Answer: costs are paid on every trade, and they can reduce or remove a small positive expectancy.)

## Practical assignment

Take your last 20 trades from a demo account or from a trading journal you already keep. Calculate the win rate, the average win, the average loss, the reward-to-risk ratio and the expectancy. Subtract your average cost per trade, then write one sentence on whether the method deserves more testing.

## Summary

- Win rate and reward-to-risk must be read together, and expectancy combines them into an average result per trade.
- The break-even win rate for a reward-to-risk ratio R is 1 divided by (1 plus R).
- Subtract costs and keep a large enough record before judging a method.

## 1. Measure drawdown from the peak

Drawdown is measured from a peak, not from your starting balance. If your account rises to 10,000 USD and then falls to 8,000 USD, the drawdown is 2,000 USD, which is 20 percent of the peak. The figure matters because it shows how far you are from your best result, and it is the figure your plan should watch. Record the peak each time it is reached, and calculate the drawdown from that record.

## 2. Recovery arithmetic

Losses are harder to recover than they look, because each percentage loss is taken from a smaller balance. To recover from a loss of L, the gain needed is L divided by (1 minus L). A loss of 10 percent needs a gain of 11.1 percent. A loss of 20 percent needs 25 percent. A loss of 30 percent needs 42.9 percent. A loss of 50 percent needs 100 percent, which means doubling what remains. Use this table in your plan, so the cost of a large loss is clear before you take one.

* 20 percent drawdown, recovered by a 25 percent gain on the 8,000 USD balance || The 20 percent drawdown is from the peak, and a 25 percent gain on 8,000 gives back 10,000.
- 20 percent drawdown, recovered by a 20 percent gain || A 20 percent gain on 8,000 gives 9,600, which is still below the peak.

## 3. Set loss limits in advance

Set three limits before you trade: a daily cash loss limit, a weekly limit, and a maximum drawdown that pauses trading. Write them in cash and as percentages of the equity on the day they are set. When a limit is reached, you stop placing new trades for that period. The limit protects you from the moments when a losing trader wants to take larger risks to get back to even.

## Worked example: Noémia's month

Noémia trades from Huambo with a USD account of 6,000 at the start of the month. Her plan sets a daily loss limit of 2 percent, which is 120 USD, a weekly limit of 3 percent, which is 180 USD, and a maximum drawdown of 10 percent from the monthly peak. On Monday she loses 120 USD on her first two trades, which reaches her daily limit. She stops and writes down what happened. On Tuesday she wins 90 USD. By Wednesday her week-to-date result is minus 90 USD, which is 1.5 percent and inside her weekly limit. Her drawdown from the monthly peak of 6,000 is also 90 USD, or 1.5 percent, so she continues with the same size. Had her equity fallen 600 USD from the peak, her drawdown would have reached 10 percent. The plan would then require a pause and a review, not a larger trade to recover.

::: case | Win it back

? What should Noémia do?
- Double the risk per trade, because a bigger size will recover the loss faster || Doubling the risk doubles the loss if the trades fail, and it pushes the drawdown towards the maximum.
* Keep her risk per trade and her limits as written, and review the record before deciding anything new || The plan was designed for this moment. Recovery needs a steady process, and the review shows whether the method or her execution needs changing.
- Trade the most volatile instrument, so the gain comes quickly || Volatility increases risk, and it does not address the reason for the losses.
:::

## 1. How leverage magnifies the result

Leverage allows a trader to control a position larger than the money in the account. Gains and losses are calculated on the full position, not on the deposit. The leverage ratio is the position value divided by the account equity. If a trader has 1,000 USD and controls a 10,000 USD position, the ratio is 10 times. A 1 percent move in price produces a 100 USD gain or loss, which is 10 percent of the account. Leverage does not change the direction of the trade. It changes how much each move matters.

## 2. Margin and margin calls

Margin is the deposit the broker requires to keep a leveraged position open. Each broker sets its own requirement, and it can change, especially in volatile markets. If equity falls below the required level, the broker may issue a margin call, asking for more funds, or it may close positions without asking. A margin call can force a loss at the worst moment, because the position is closed after the price has already moved against you. Read your broker's margin rules in writing before using any leverage, and keep an equity buffer above the level that triggers a call.

::: quiz
? A trader's account equity is 1,000 USD, and she holds a 10,000 USD position. Price falls 10 percent. What happens to the account?
- She loses 100 USD, which is 10 percent of the account || The 10 percent applies to the position, not to the account. The loss on 10,000 USD is 1,000 USD.
* She loses 1,000 USD, which is the whole account equity, before any margin rule acts || A 10 percent move on a 10,000 USD position is a 1,000 USD loss, which equals the account equity.
- She loses nothing, because leverage protects the deposit || Leverage magnifies losses. It does not protect the deposit.
:::

## 3. Set a leverage cap in your plan

A leverage cap is a maximum ratio you will not exceed, written into your risk plan. It works together with the risk budget from lesson 2. Because the stop distance decides the size, a position can carry a small risk and still use high leverage. A cap of a small multiple of equity keeps the exposure manageable even when the stop is close. Choose the cap before you trade, and check it on every trade with the leverage ratio.

## Worked example: Joana's leveraged trade in Benguela

Joana trades from Benguela with 2,000 USD in her account. She is tempted by a 12,000 USD position, which would be six times leverage. Her plan caps leverage at three times, so she calculates the cap: three times 2,000 is 6,000 USD of maximum position value. She reduces the position to 6,000 USD. Two weeks later, a market she holds falls 8 percent in one session. On the full 12,000 USD position the loss would have been 960 USD, or 48 percent of her starting equity, and a margin call would be likely. On her capped 6,000 USD position the loss is 480 USD, or 24 percent. She keeps most of her equity to trade again. The cap did not prevent the loss, but it kept the loss within what she had planned to accept.
