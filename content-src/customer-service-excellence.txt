## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain how customers judge service, using the five RATER dimensions and the zone of tolerance;
- map the moments of truth in a customer journey in your own workplace;
- explain the service-profit chain and why it matters to a front-line team;
- calculate a simple customer lifetime value and use it to justify good service.

## Key terms

::: terms
Customer expectation: what a customer believes should happen when they deal with you, shaped by past experience, promises and word of mouth
Zone of tolerance: the range between the adequate service a customer will accept and the desired service they hope for
Moment of truth: any contact in which a customer forms an opinion about your organisation
Service-profit chain: the link between how staff are treated, the value customers receive, customer loyalty and profit
Customer lifetime value: the total revenue or profit a customer brings over the whole relationship
:::

## 1. How customers judge service

Customers do not judge service against a fixed standard. They compare what they receive with what they expected. Expectations come from what you promised (adverts, a salesperson, a sign on the wall), from past experience with you and your competitors, and from what friends and family say.

Researchers describe two levels of expectation. Desired service is what the customer hopes for. Adequate service is the minimum they will accept. The space between them is the zone of tolerance. Inside the zone the customer is satisfied. Below it they complain, or more often leave without saying anything. Notice that the zone can narrow: a customer who has been let down before, or who is in a hurry, accepts much less.

To understand what customers look at, use the five dimensions of service quality developed by the researchers Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry for their SERVQUAL questionnaire. They are easy to remember with the word RATER:

- Reliability: doing what you promised, correctly, the first time.
- Assurance: knowledge and courtesy that make the customer trust you.
- Tangibles: the premises, equipment, uniforms and documents the customer can see.
- Empathy: caring, individual attention.
- Responsiveness: willingness to help and speed of response.

Reliability is often found to matter most. A friendly bank teller does not make up for a transfer that was processed wrongly. Warmth makes good service better, but it cannot replace getting it right.

::: quiz
? A telecom shop is clean and modern, but customers often leave without their problem solved because staff do not know the new data plans. Which RATER dimension is weakest?
- Tangibles || The premises are clean and modern, so tangibles are strong.
* Assurance || Correct. Staff lack the product knowledge that makes customers trust them.
- Empathy || Nothing suggests staff are uncaring. The gap is knowledge.
:::

## 2. Moments of truth and the customer journey

Jan Carlzon, who led the airline SAS in the 1980s, made the term "moment of truth" famous. He argued that every brief contact between a customer and an employee decides whether the customer sees the company as good or bad. Today these contacts include phone calls, WhatsApp messages, the website, the security guard at the door, the delivery driver and the invoice.

To map moments of truth, follow these steps:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Choose one journey | For example opening an account or reporting a fault | One journey at a time
SLIDE Step 2: List every contact | Before, during and after the visit or call | Include back-office contacts the customer feels
SLIDE Step 3: Mark what the customer expects | Use the customer's words, not internal jargon | Expectation sets the standard
SLIDE Step 4: Rate each contact | Green inside the zone of tolerance, red below it | Find the red moments
SLIDE Step 5: Fix the worst moment first | Agree one change and an owner | Small fixes, quickly
:::

Some moments matter more than others. The first contact sets the tone, a failure moment (a delay or a mistake) tests the relationship, and the last contact is what the customer remembers when they tell others.

## 3. Why good service pays: the service-profit chain

In 1994 a group of Harvard researchers led by James Heskett published the service-profit chain. It links a chain of causes and effects:

::: diagram steps | The service-profit chain
Internal service quality: good tools, training and support for staff
Employee satisfaction: staff who feel equipped and valued
Retention and productivity: experienced staff who stay and work well
Customer value: service that solves the customer's need
Satisfaction and loyalty: customers who return and recommend
Revenue and profit: growth that comes from loyal customers
:::

The lesson for supervisors is that customer service starts inside. A call centre agent who waits ten minutes for a slow system, or who cannot get an answer from the billing team, will struggle to serve customers well, however good their attitude.

To show the money behind loyalty, calculate customer lifetime value (CLV):

CLV = average spend per visit x visits per year x years as a customer x profit margin

::: quiz
? Which link of the service-profit chain is broken when agents cannot reach the billing team to answer customer questions?
- Customer loyalty || Loyalty will suffer later, but it is not where the chain breaks.
- Revenue growth || Revenue is the final result, not the cause.
* Internal service quality || Correct. Staff lack the internal support they need to serve customers.
:::

## Worked example: a supermarket in Benguela

A supermarket in Benguela notices that some regular families have stopped coming. The manager estimates that a typical family spends 25,000 Kz per visit, visits 50 times a year and stays for about 5 years. The gross margin is 20%.

CLV = 25,000 x 50 x 5 x 20% = 1,250,000 Kz of profit per family.

Losing ten families costs about 12.5 million Kz in future profit. The team maps the shopping journey and finds two red moments: long queues at the tills on Saturday mornings and a rude response when customers ask for items that are out of stock.

::: case | Saturday queues in Benguela
Supervisor Marta has four tills but only two cashiers on Saturday mornings. Queues reach 20 minutes and some customers leave their baskets. The store manager asks her for a solution by Friday.
? What should Marta propose first?
- Put up a sign asking customers to be patient || A sign does not change the wait. Customers stay below their zone of tolerance.
* Move two shelf staff to the tills from 9:00 to 12:00 on Saturdays, after training them as cashiers || Correct. It fixes the worst moment of truth with existing staff and protects high lifetime value customers.
- Offer a 5% discount to customers who complain || This pays for the problem without fixing it, and most customers leave without complaining.
- Ask cashiers to work faster and skip greetings || Speed at the cost of courtesy damages assurance and empathy.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Treating service as smiling only. Reliability comes first.
- Promising more than your process can deliver, which raises expectations you will then miss.
- Ignoring back-office moments such as invoices, deliveries and transfers.
- Judging staff only on speed, which damages assurance and empathy.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- apply the four skills of active listening in a customer conversation;
- write empathy statements that sound sincere and lead to action;
- adapt your tone for face-to-face, phone, WhatsApp and chat service;
- rewrite negative phrases using positive language.

## Key terms

::: terms
Active listening: listening with full attention to understand the customer's need and feelings, and showing that you have understood
Paraphrasing: repeating the customer's message in your own words to check understanding
Empathy statement: a sentence that recognises how the situation affects the customer
Open question: a question that invites a full answer, such as "What happened when you tried to pay?"
Closed question: a question answered with yes, no or a fact, such as "Was it today?"
Positive language: wording that focuses on what can be done rather than on what cannot
:::

## 1. Active listening

Many conversations fail because the agent misunderstands the need: she hears "my card is not working", assumes it is blocked, and solves the wrong problem. Active listening prevents this. It has four skills:

::: diagram steps | The four skills of active listening
Attend: full attention, no interruptions, take notes
Question: open questions to explore, closed questions to confirm facts
Paraphrase: repeat the message in your own words
Summarise: state the need and the agreed next step
:::

Attend means stopping other tasks, keeping eye contact at the counter, and using short signals such as "I see" on the phone. Do not interrupt.

Question in two stages. Start with open questions ("Can you tell me what happened?") to let the customer explain. Then use closed questions ("Was the payment made on Monday?") to confirm details. Asking only closed questions feels like an interrogation.

Paraphrase what you heard: "So the money left your account, but the supplier says they have not received it. Is that right?" This shows you listened and lets the customer correct you early.

Summarise before you act and at the end: "I will check the transfer reference now and send you the result on WhatsApp by 15:00."

::: quiz
? A customer at a bank counter says "I came last week and nothing happened." What is the best first response?
- "That is not possible, we process everything in 48 hours." || This argues before understanding and damages trust.
* "I am sorry to hear that. Can you tell me what you asked for last week?" || Correct. It acknowledges the customer and uses an open question to understand.
- "Do you have your reference number?" || A closed question too early. The customer has not yet explained the need.
:::

## 2. Empathy statements

Empathy is recognising how the situation affects the customer. It is not agreeing that the company was wrong, and it is not apologising for everything. A good empathy statement has three features: it names the feeling or impact, it is specific to this customer, and it is followed by action.

Compare these:

- Weak: "I understand." (Said alone, it sounds automatic.)
- Better: "I can see how stressful it is to have your salary delayed at the end of the month. Let me check the transfer now."
- Weak: "Sorry for the inconvenience."
- Better: "Waiting three days without electricity in the shop must have cost you sales. I will register this as urgent with the technical team."

::: tip | Empathy then action
Always follow an empathy statement with a concrete step. Empathy without action sounds like a script.
:::

## 3. Tone across channels

On the phone, the customer cannot see you, so your voice carries all of your attitude. Smiling while you speak really changes the sound of the voice. Speak a little slower than normal, pause after giving important information, and use the customer's name. Never leave silence unexplained: say "I am checking your account now, it will take about one minute" and, if you put the customer on hold, ask permission and return when you said you would.

On WhatsApp and chat, the written words have no tone of voice to soften them, so they can sound colder than you intend. Follow these rules:

::: slides
SLIDE Open well | Greet the customer and give your name | "Good morning, this is Ana from customer care"
SLIDE Keep it readable | Short messages, one topic each | Avoid one long block of text
SLIDE Watch the tone | No capitals, no row of exclamation marks, no slang | Polite and clear beats casual
SLIDE Set expectations | Say when you will reply if you need to check | "I will reply by 14:00"
SLIDE Close properly | Confirm the solution and ask if anything else is needed | Never leave a chat open without an end
:::

## 4. Positive language

Positive language does not hide bad news. It presents the same facts in terms of what can be done. Practise these rewrites:

- "I cannot do that today" becomes "I can have it ready for you tomorrow at 10:00."
- "That is not my department" becomes "The billing team handles this. I will transfer you and explain your case to them."
- "You did not send the document" becomes "To finish the request, I just need a copy of your ID."
- "It will be done soon" becomes "You will receive an SMS by Friday."

Avoid blame words ("you should have"), vague time words ("soon", "later") and negative words ("problem", "unfortunately") where a clear next step works better.

::: quiz
? Which reply uses positive language best?
- "Unfortunately we cannot deliver on Saturday." || True, but it only says what is not possible.
- "Delivery is not my responsibility." || This pushes the customer away and offers nothing.
* "We can deliver on Monday morning, or you can collect from our Viana warehouse on Saturday." || Correct. It gives the customer clear options they can choose from.
:::

## Worked example: a telecom call centre in Luanda

An agent at a Luanda telecom call centre receives a call from a small business owner whose internet has been down for two days. The agent listens without interrupting, then paraphrases: "So your connection stopped on Monday, you called twice and nobody came. Is that right?" She then uses an empathy statement and a positive next step: "Two days without internet must be affecting your sales. I am booking a technician for tomorrow between 8:00 and 12:00, and I will send you the reference on WhatsApp now."

Customer Sr. Domingos writes on WhatsApp at 18:40: "THIS IS THE THIRD TIME I WRITE. NOBODY ANSWERS." His prepaid data was charged twice, a total of 6,000 Kz. Agent Lúcia sees the message.

- "Send your number and the date." || Too abrupt. It ignores the frustration and sounds like a form.
- "Our system shows no problem." || It argues before checking and closes the conversation badly.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
Complaint: any expression of dissatisfaction from a customer that expects a response
HEARD: a five-step method for handling complaints: Hear, Empathise, Apologise, Resolve, Diagnose
De-escalation: actions that reduce a customer's anger so the conversation can move to a solution
Service recovery: the actions taken to put things right after a service failure
Service recovery paradox: the finding that a customer whose problem is solved very well can become more loyal than one who never had a problem

## 1. Why complaints are valuable

Most unhappy customers do not complain. They simply stop buying and tell other people. A customer who complains is giving you a last chance to keep them, and free information about a weakness in your process. That is why the first words to a complaining customer can be "Thank you for telling us."

Record complaints even when they are solved at once. A pattern of small complaints often shows a problem that no single conversation will fix.

## 2. The HEARD method

HEARD is a simple five-step method used by many service teams. Follow the steps in order:

::: diagram steps | The HEARD method
Hear: let the customer tell the full story without interrupting
Empathise: show you understand the impact on them
Apologise: say sorry sincerely for the experience
Resolve: agree a solution and a time, then act
Diagnose: find out why it happened and stop it happening again
:::

Hear. Let the customer finish and take notes. Interrupting makes an angry customer angrier.

Empathise. Use a specific empathy statement: "Three wrong deliveries in a month must be disrupting your shop."

Apologise. A sincere apology does not need to admit legal fault. "I am sorry this has happened" is honest even while you are still checking the facts. Avoid "I am sorry if you feel that way", which sounds like blaming the customer.

Resolve. Offer a solution, or two options, with a clear time. If you need to check, say when you will call back, and call back on time.

Diagnose. After the customer is helped, find the cause. Was it a wrong address in the system, a picking error or a driver problem? Record it so the team can fix it.

::: quiz
? A customer complains that her new phone was not activated. The agent listens, says sorry, and activates the phone. Which HEARD step is missing?
- Hear || The agent listened to the complaint.
- Resolve || The phone was activated, so the problem was resolved.
* Diagnose || Correct. Nobody checked why activation failed, so it may happen to the next customer.
:::

## 3. De-escalation with angry customers

An angry customer cannot think clearly about solutions. Your first job is to bring the emotion down. Use these techniques:

::: slides
SLIDE Control yourself first | Breathe, lower your voice and slow down | Calm is contagious
SLIDE Let them vent | Do not interrupt or argue while they speak | Anger falls when people feel heard
SLIDE Use their name and agree with facts | "Sr. Paulo, you are right that you waited two hours" | Agreement on facts is not admitting fault
SLIDE Change the setting | Invite them to sit or move to a quiet office | An audience keeps anger high
SLIDE Offer choices | "I can call you at 14:00 or you can wait 20 minutes" | Choice gives back a sense of control
:::

Avoid trigger phrases such as "calm down", "that is our policy" and "there is nothing I can do". Each one tells the customer they are not taken seriously.

Set limits when necessary. You do not have to accept insults: "I want to help you, Sr. Paulo, and I can do that if we speak without insults." If a customer threatens violence, stop the conversation, move away and call security or your supervisor. Your safety comes first.

## 4. Service recovery

Service recovery means putting things right. A good recovery has three parts: fix the problem quickly, add a fair gesture when the customer suffered real loss or effort, and follow up to confirm the customer is satisfied.

The gesture should be proportionate. A 15-minute queue may need only a sincere apology. A missed delivery that left a restaurant without rice for a weekend may justify free delivery on the next order or a credit note. Agree in advance what front-line staff can offer without approval, for example up to 20,000 Kz in credit, so they can act at once.

The service recovery paradox suggests that a customer whose problem is solved fast and fairly may become more loyal than one who never had a problem. Do not rely on it: it rarely works if the same failure happens twice.

Escalate when the solution is beyond your authority, the customer asks for a manager after you have tried to help, or there is a legal, safety or reputational risk. Hand over the full story so the customer does not have to repeat it.

::: quiz
? A restaurant received spoiled cooking oil worth 90,000 Kz. Front-line staff may offer up to 20,000 Kz without approval. What should the agent do?
- Offer 20,000 Kz credit and close the case || This is below the customer's loss and ignores the agent's limit.
* Replace the oil today and escalate to the supervisor for a credit decision, with the full case notes || Correct. It fixes the problem at once and escalates what is beyond the agent's authority.
- Tell the customer to write a formal letter || This adds effort and delays the recovery.
:::

She then checks the order history and finds that both products share similar codes in the system.

::: case | Wrong delivery for the second time
The supervisor, Sr. Kiala, reviews the case. The sugar has been delivered and the customer is calmer, but similar product codes caused two errors this month.

- Close the case because the customer received the sugar || The cause remains, so the error will happen again.

- Give the customer a 50% discount on all orders this year || This is out of proportion and does not fix the cause.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
Service standard: a specific, measurable promise about how a service will be delivered
Service level agreement (SLA): a written agreement that sets the service targets between a provider and its customers
Operational level agreement (OLA): an internal agreement between teams that supports an external SLA
First contact resolution (FCR): solving the customer's issue in the first contact, with no need for them to contact you again
Escalation: passing a case to someone with more skill or more authority

## 1. Service standards and SLAs

Good intentions do not create consistent service. Standards do. A service standard is specific, observable and timed. "We give fast service" is not a standard. "We answer WhatsApp messages within 15 minutes between 8:00 and 20:00" is.

A service level agreement (SLA) puts standards in writing. It may be with external customers (for example, a distributor's key accounts) or between internal teams. Follow these steps to write one:

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Define the service | What is covered and what is not | "Fault repair for business internet customers"
SLIDE Step 2: Set the target | A number and a time | "Technician on site within 24 hours"
SLIDE Step 3: Define the measure | Start and stop points of the clock | Clock starts when the fault is logged
SLIDE Step 4: Set hours and channels | Working hours, weekends, channels | Phone, WhatsApp and email
SLIDE Step 5: Name the owner and reporting | Who reports results, how often | Monthly report to the client
:::

External promises depend on internal teams. If the call centre promises a refund in five days, but finance takes seven days to approve refunds, the promise will fail. An operational level agreement (OLA) between the two teams, such as "finance approves refunds within three working days", makes the external SLA possible.

Two targets are often confused. Response time is how quickly you first reply. Resolution time is how quickly the issue is solved. A customer who gets a fast "we are looking into it" and then waits two weeks has received good response but poor resolution. Set and measure both.

::: quiz
? Which is a usable service standard?
- "We always treat customers with respect" || It is a value, but it is not measurable or timed.
* "Account opening requests are completed within 2 working days of receiving all documents" || Correct. It is specific, measurable and states when the clock starts.
- "We aim to reply to emails quickly" || "Quickly" cannot be measured or checked.
:::

## 2. Measuring the standard: service level and SLA compliance

In call centres, service level is usually expressed as a percentage of contacts answered within a target time. A common target is 80% of calls answered within 20 seconds, often written as 80/20. The formula is:

Service level = contacts answered within target / total contacts offered x 100

SLA compliance uses the same idea for any standard:

SLA compliance = cases meeting the target / total cases x 100

For example, a branch receives 420 WhatsApp messages in a week and answers 357 within 15 minutes. SLA compliance = 357 / 420 x 100 = 85%. If the target is 90%, the branch is 5 points below target and should look at when the late replies happen.

## 3. First contact resolution

First contact resolution (FCR) means the customer's issue is solved in the first contact, without a call-back, transfer or repeat visit. It matters because every repeat contact costs the company time and costs the customer effort and patience.

FCR improves when front-line staff have three things: knowledge (training and an up-to-date knowledge base), authority (permission to solve common issues without approval) and tools (system access to see and change the account). If agents must transfer every billing question, FCR will stay low however hard they work.

## 4. Escalation paths, scripts and flexibility

Not every case can be solved at the front line. A clear escalation path tells staff who handles what. Many organisations use tiers:

::: diagram pyramid | Tiered support
Tier 3: experts, managers or suppliers for rare and complex cases
Tier 2: specialists for technical, billing or credit issues
Tier 1: front-line staff who solve most issues at first contact
:::

There are two directions. Functional escalation moves a case to someone with more skill, for example from a branch teller to the cards team. Hierarchical escalation moves it to someone with more authority, for example a supervisor who can approve a refund. For each type, define the trigger (when to escalate), the route (to whom and through which system) and the handover (the full case notes, so the customer does not repeat the story). A warm transfer, where you introduce the customer and the case to the next person, is better than simply passing the call.

Scripts bring consistency, but too many make staff sound like machines. A practical rule: script what must be said exactly, and guide the rest with principles. Script identity checks, legal and regulatory statements, prices and terms, and safety instructions. Guide greetings, explanations and empathy with principles and examples, and let staff use their own words.

::: quiz
? A teller can see a customer's card is blocked but cannot unblock it, so she sends the case to the cards team. What type of escalation is this?
* Functional escalation || Correct. The case moves to a team with more specialist skill and system access.
- Hierarchical escalation || That would move the case to someone with more authority, such as a manager.
- Tier 1 resolution || The case was not solved at Tier 1, it was passed on.
:::

## Worked example: a bank branch in Talatona

A bank branch in Talatona, Luanda, has many complaints about slow account opening. The supervisor checks the data: last month 120 accounts were opened and 78 were completed within the promised 2 working days. SLA compliance = 78 / 120 x 100 = 65%, against a target of 90%.

She maps the process and finds that documents are scanned at the branch but checked by a back-office team that has no time target. Most delays happen there.

::: case | Slow account opening in Talatona
The supervisor, Sra. Nzinga, must improve SLA compliance from 65% to 90% within three months. Customers are already promised 2 working days.
? What should she do first?
- Tell branch staff to promise 5 days instead || This lowers the promise without fixing the process, and customers will compare with competitors.
* Agree an OLA with the back office to check documents within 1 working day, and track it weekly || Correct. The delay is internal, so an internal agreement supports the external promise.
- Ask customers to call the back office directly || This adds customer effort and makes the delay their problem.
- Add a script asking customers to be patient || A script does not fix where the time is lost.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- calculate customer satisfaction (CSAT) and Net Promoter Score (NPS) from survey data;
- explain what Customer Effort Score (CES) measures and when to use it;
- calculate average handling time (AHT) and first contact resolution (FCR);
- choose a balanced set of service measures that do not push staff into bad behaviour.

## Key terms

::: terms
CSAT: customer satisfaction score, the percentage of customers who say they were satisfied with a contact or transaction
NPS: Net Promoter Score, the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors
CES: Customer Effort Score, a measure of how easy it was for the customer to get what they needed
AHT: average handling time, the average time spent on one contact including talk, hold and after-call work
FCR: first contact resolution, the percentage of issues solved in the first contact
:::

## 1. Customer experience measures: CSAT, NPS and CES

CSAT asks customers how satisfied they were, usually on a scale of 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), straight after a contact. The most common way to calculate it is:

CSAT = number of 4 and 5 responses / total responses x 100

If 300 customers answer and 240 give 4 or 5, CSAT is 80%. CSAT is useful for judging a single contact, a branch visit or a delivery.

NPS was introduced by Fred Reichheld in 2003. It asks one question: "On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague?" Customers are grouped by their answer:

::: diagram steps | Calculating NPS
Ask: "How likely are you to recommend us?" on a 0 to 10 scale
Group: promoters 9-10, passives 7-8, detractors 0-6
Convert: work out promoters and detractors as a percentage of all responses
Subtract: NPS = % promoters minus % detractors
Ask why: read the comments and follow up with detractors
:::

Passives count in the total but are not added or subtracted. NPS is a number, not a percentage, and ranges from -100 (everyone a detractor) to +100 (everyone a promoter). NPS measures the overall relationship, so it is often asked every few months rather than after every contact.

CES asks how easy it was to get the issue solved, for example "The company made it easy for me to handle my issue", often answered on a 1 to 7 agreement scale. It is useful after service contacts, because customers who have to work hard (calling several times, repeating information, visiting a branch) are more likely to leave.

::: quiz
? Out of 100 responses, 50 are promoters, 30 passives and 20 detractors. What is the NPS?
- +50 || This counts only promoters and ignores detractors.
* +30 || Correct. 50% promoters minus 20% detractors equals +30.
- +80 || This adds promoters and passives, which is not how NPS works.
:::

## 2. Efficiency and resolution measures: AHT and FCR

Average handling time measures how long contacts take:

AHT = (total talk time + total hold time + total after-call work) / number of contacts handled

After-call work is the time spent writing notes and completing tasks after the customer has gone. For example, an agent handles 60 calls in a day with 240 minutes of talk, 30 minutes of hold and 90 minutes of after-call work. AHT = (240 + 30 + 90) / 60 = 6 minutes per call.

AHT is essential for planning how many staff you need. As a target on its own it is dangerous. Agents under pressure to cut AHT may rush customers, transfer difficult calls or promise call-backs, which creates repeat contacts.

First contact resolution balances AHT:

FCR = issues solved at first contact / total issues x 100

FCR can be measured by asking customers ("Was your issue solved today?") or by checking whether the same customer contacts you again about the same issue within a set period, such as 7 days.

::: tip | Measures and targets
A principle known as Goodhart's law warns that when a measure becomes a target, people may improve the number rather than the service. Always pair a speed measure with a quality measure.
:::

## 3. Building a balanced service scorecard

No single number tells the full story. A balanced scorecard combines three types of measure:

::: slides
SLIDE Experience measures | CSAT, NPS and CES | How customers feel
SLIDE Efficiency measures | AHT, service level and response time | How fast and how costly
SLIDE Quality measures | FCR, call review scores and complaint numbers | Whether the work was done right
SLIDE Comments | Read what customers write, not only the score | The reason behind the number
:::

Use the scorecard in a monthly review: look for trends rather than single bad days, compare channels (phone, WhatsApp, branch), and choose one or two actions. Small samples change a lot from month to month, so be careful with conclusions from 20 or 30 responses.

::: quiz
? A call centre manager sets one target: cut AHT from 7 to 5 minutes. What is the most likely side effect?
- CSAT will rise because customers like short calls || Short calls help only if the issue is solved.
- Service level will fall || Shorter calls usually improve service level, not reduce it.
* Repeat calls will rise and FCR will fall || Correct. Agents rush or transfer customers, so issues are not solved the first time.
:::

## Worked example: NPS at a telecom provider in Huambo

A telecom provider surveys 400 customers in Huambo after their first three months. The results are: 168 promoters (9-10), 132 passives (7-8) and 100 detractors (0-6).

- Promoters: 168 / 400 = 42%.
- Detractors: 100 / 400 = 25%.
- NPS = 42 - 25 = +17.

The team reads the detractors' comments. Most mention the same issue: top-up problems take several days to solve because agents must email another team. Average CES for these contacts is low, and FCR for top-up issues is only 45%.

::: case | Improving NPS in Huambo
The regional manager, Sr. Chilala, wants to raise NPS from +17. He has budget for one action this quarter.
? Which action is most likely to raise NPS?
- Run a radio advert about the network's quality || Adverts do not fix the effort that is creating detractors.
- Give every surveyed customer free airtime || This may lift one survey but does not fix the cause.
- Ask agents to tell customers to score 9 or 10 || This distorts the measure and breaks trust.
* Give Tier 1 agents system access to fix common top-up problems at first contact || Correct. It reduces effort, raises FCR and removes the main reason for detractors.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- coach a team member using the GROW model;
- run a fair and useful call or chat review with a scorecard;
- give recognition and feedback using the Situation-Behaviour-Impact (SBI) model;

## Key terms

::: terms
Service culture: the shared habits and beliefs that shape how people serve customers when no one is watching
Coaching: helping a person improve by asking questions and agreeing actions, rather than only giving instructions
Calibration: a session where reviewers score the same contact and agree how to apply the scorecard consistently
SBI: a feedback model that describes the Situation, the Behaviour and its Impact
Root cause: the underlying reason a problem happens, which, if removed, stops it happening again
:::

## 1. What builds a service culture

A service culture is how people serve when no one is watching. Posters and slogans do not build it. Supervisors build it every day through what they notice, what they reward and what they tolerate. If a supervisor ignores an agent who is rude on WhatsApp, the team learns that rudeness is acceptable. If a supervisor praises an agent who solved a difficult case, the team learns what good looks like.

This connects to the service-profit chain from lesson 1: staff who are coached, recognised and given the tools to solve problems serve customers better. The four tools in this lesson are coaching, call reviews, recognition and root-cause fixing.

## 2. Coaching with GROW

The GROW model, popularised by the coach John Whitmore, gives structure to a coaching conversation. Its strength is that the supervisor asks questions and the team member finds the answer, so they own the action.

::: slides
SLIDE G: Goal | "What would you like to improve in your WhatsApp replies?" | Agree one specific goal
SLIDE R: Reality | "What happens now? Let us look at three of your chats." | Facts, not opinions
SLIDE O: Options | "What could you do differently? What else?" | Let them suggest first
SLIDE W: Will | "What will you do, and when shall we review it?" | One action, one date
:::

Keep coaching short (15 to 20 minutes), regular (for example every two weeks) and focused on one skill at a time. Use real examples from the agent's own contacts.

::: quiz
? In a coaching session, the supervisor says: "Your hold times are too long. From tomorrow, check the knowledge base before you put customers on hold." What is wrong with this?
- Nothing, it is clear and direct || It is clear, but it is an instruction, not coaching.
* The supervisor tells instead of asking, so the agent does not explore the reality or own the solution || Correct. GROW asks questions so the agent helps find and commit to the action.
- The goal is not about customers || Long hold times affect customers directly.
:::

## 3. Call reviews and recognition

Call and chat reviews (often called quality monitoring) check contacts against your standards. Use a short scorecard of 6 to 10 items linked to what you have trained: greeting and name, active listening, empathy, correct information, positive language, compliance steps, and a clear close. Review a sample of contacts for each person every month, including good ones. Hold calibration sessions where supervisors score the same contact and agree on differences, so agents are scored fairly whoever reviews them.

Share the review with the agent within a few days, listen to the contact together and use it for coaching. If reviews are used only to punish, agents will hide problems.

Recognition is the other half. Use the SBI model, developed by the Center for Creative Leadership, for both praise and correction:

- Situation: "This morning, in the call with the shop owner from Cazenga..."
- Behaviour: "...you paraphrased his problem and gave him a clear time for the delivery..."
- Impact: "...and he thanked you and cancelled his complaint."

Specific, timely recognition teaches the whole team what good service looks like. "Good job" teaches nothing.

## 4. Using feedback to fix root causes

Front-line staff solve cases. Supervisors must also stop the same cases coming back. Two simple tools help.

Pareto analysis is based on the observation that a few causes usually create most of the problems. Count complaints by cause for a month, sort them from largest to smallest, and calculate each one's share of the total. Focus on the top two or three.

The 5 Whys, a technique associated with Toyota, asks "why?" repeatedly until you reach a cause you can fix. Five is a guide, not a rule.

Finally, close the loop. Tell the customer what changed, and tell staff what was fixed because of their feedback.

::: diagram cycle | The closed feedback loop
Collect: complaints, survey comments and staff observations
Analyse: group by cause and find the biggest with Pareto
Fix: find the root cause with the 5 Whys and change the process
Tell: inform customers and staff what changed
:::

::: quiz
? A branch has 200 complaints in a month: 90 about queues, 60 about card delivery, 30 about fees and 20 other. What share do the top two causes represent?
- 45% || That is queues only.
- 60% || That is not the total of the top two causes.
* 75% || Correct. Queues and card delivery are 150 of 200 complaints, which is 75%.
:::

## Worked example: a hotel in Lubango

A hotel in Lubango receives 80 complaints in two months. The front office supervisor sorts them: 36 about slow check-in, 20 about Wi-Fi, 14 about breakfast and 10 other. Check-in is 45% of the total.

She applies the 5 Whys. Why is check-in slow? Guests wait while rooms are finished. Why? Housekeeping does not know which rooms are needed first. Why? Arrival times are not shared. Why? Reservations and housekeeping use separate lists. The fix: a shared arrivals list on the team's WhatsApp group at 10:00 each day, with priority rooms marked.

::: case | Repeated complaints in Lubango
Two weeks later, check-in complaints fall. But receptionist Joana, who suggested the shared list, feels nobody noticed. Two other staff say their ideas are ignored.
? What should the supervisor do?
- Give Joana a bonus and say nothing to the team || Private reward does not show the team that feedback leads to change.
* Recognise Joana at the team briefing using SBI and ask the team for the next problem to fix || Correct. Public, specific recognition closes the loop and encourages more ideas.
- Wait for the monthly figures before saying anything || Recognition loses impact when delayed.
:::
