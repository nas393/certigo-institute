## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- explain what a financial market does for buyers and sellers;
- name the main types of market participants and their goals;
- explain why trading carries risk and what that means for a beginner;
- describe what a trading account requires before you deposit money.

## Key terms

::: terms
Market: a place or system where buyers and sellers agree prices for something
Asset: anything that can be bought or sold for value, such as a share, a currency pair or a commodity
Liquidity: how easily something can be bought or sold without moving its price much
Broker: a firm that carries out buy and sell orders for its clients
Speculator: a participant who aims to profit from price changes rather than from using the asset
Hedger: a participant who uses a market to reduce a risk they already carry
:::

## 1. What a market does

A market brings together people who want to buy and people who want to sell. Without a market, a shop owner in Luanda who needs a product has to find a supplier by chance, agree a price by negotiation every time, and hope the deal is fair. A market makes prices visible, comparable and quick to agree.

Financial markets work the same way, but for securities and contracts rather than goods on a shelf. Shares give a small ownership stake in a company. Currencies are traded in pairs, one against another. Commodities such as oil or wheat are traded in standard contracts. In each case the market shows a price that many people have agreed to, and that price changes as more buyers or sellers arrive.

Prices in a market are not set by one person. They come from the orders placed by everyone in it. When many people want to buy, the price tends to rise. When many want to sell, it tends to fall. This simple idea sits behind every chart you will ever see.

Some markets are organised exchanges with rules and central record keeping. Others, such as much of the currency market, work through networks of banks and brokers. The details differ, but the principle is the same: a buyer and a seller meet at a price.

## 2. Who trades in a market

Different participants have different goals, and it helps to name them.

Investors buy an asset to hold it for years, expecting its value or its income to grow. Speculators buy and sell to profit from short-term price moves. Hedgers use a market to protect themselves from a price change they cannot avoid, for example a business that buys fuel every month and wants to fix the cost in advance. Market makers quote both a buy price and a sell price, and earn a small difference on the trades they handle.

For a beginner, the important point is this. Most people you trade against are professionals with more information, faster systems and more experience. You do not need to be a professional to learn about trading, but you do need to know that the other side of your trade is often well prepared.

::: diagram steps | Typical roles in a market
Investor: buys to hold for the long term
Speculator: trades to profit from short-term moves
Hedger: uses the market to reduce an existing risk
Market maker: quotes buy and sell prices and earns the difference
:::

## 3. Trading is not the same as investing

People often use these words as if they meant the same thing, but they describe different activities. Investing usually means owning a share of a business or an asset with a long time horizon, and accepting that its value will move up and down along the way. Trading usually means buying and selling more often, over days or hours, with the aim of gaining from price moves.

Both activities carry risk, and neither is guaranteed to make money. A trader can lose the whole amount put at risk, and some lose more than they expected, especially when they use borrowed money. Any course, book, video or person that promises steady profits from trading is making a claim that no honest source can back up.

This course explains markets and orders. It does not tell you what to buy or sell, and nothing in it is financial advice.

::: tip | Warning
Trading can lead to losses, including losing the full amount you put at risk. Never trade money you cannot afford to lose, and never trade money you need for rent, food or school fees.
:::

## 4. What a trading account requires

To trade, you usually open an account with a broker. Before you deposit money, the broker normally asks for identity documents and information about your experience and financial situation. This is a normal check, and you should answer it truthfully.

A good broker is regulated, which means a financial regulator in its country licenses it and supervises how it treats clients. Check that the broker is licensed, that it holds client money separately from its own, and that it explains its costs clearly. Ask a qualified local professional which rules apply to you, because the rules depend on where you live and where the broker is based.

::: case | The first deposit
Carla works as an accountant in Benguela. She has saved a small amount for three years and wants to start trading. A friend tells her a broker will double her money in a month.
? What should Carla do first?
- Deposit her full savings straight away, because the friend has experience || A claim of doubled money in a month is a warning sign, not evidence.
* Check that the broker is regulated, read its cost schedule, and decide how much she can afford to lose before she deposits anything || This is the right order: check the broker, understand the costs, then set a limit.
- Borrow money to increase the amount she can trade with || Borrowing adds risk and can increase losses beyond the amount she put in.
:::

## 5. How to start learning without losing money

Start with a demo account if the broker offers one. A demo account uses fake money at real prices, so you can practise placing orders and watching what happens without risking anything. Treat the demo seriously: keep notes, follow your own rules and review the results. Many beginners do well on a demo and lose money on a live account, because real money changes behaviour.

Set a fixed amount for learning. It should be an amount whose loss would not change your life, and you should write it down before you start.

* The hedger, such as a business that fixes the cost of fuel in advance || A hedger uses the market to protect against a price change it cannot avoid.
- The investor, who holds an asset for years || Investors hold assets for long periods, but their goal is growth or income, not risk reduction.
:::

- Because markets never move in a predictable direction || Markets do move, but that is not the reason the claim is a warning sign.
* Because no honest source can guarantee trading profits, and such claims often hide the losses || Guaranteed profit claims are common in scams and in promotions that hide the risk.

::: terms

Demand: the quantity buyers are willing to take at a given price
Bid: the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay
Ask: the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept
Spread: the difference between the ask and the bid

## 1. Supply, demand and the meeting point

Imagine a market for a product sold in a single town. If many people want to buy and only a few sellers have stock, buyers compete for what is available, and the price rises until some of them decide it is too high. If many sellers have stock and few buyers want it, sellers lower their prices to attract customers. The price settles at the point where the amount buyers want matches the amount sellers offer.

Real markets are much bigger and faster, but the logic is the same. The price is where the willingness to buy meets the willingness to sell. It is not a fixed fact about the product. It changes as the balance changes.

The most useful habit to build early is to ask, of any price move: what changed in the balance between buyers and sellers? If you cannot answer, you are guessing.

::: slides
SLIDE Step 1: Find the balance | Ask how many people want to buy; Ask how many want to sell | Price follows the balance
SLIDE Step 2: Note what changed | Look for news, large orders or a shift in mood; Write it down with the date | Change explains movement
SLIDE Step 3: Test the reason | Does the explanation fit the size of the move?; Would it still make sense tomorrow? | Be honest about uncertainty
:::

## 2. Reading a two-way quote

Most markets show two prices at once. The bid is the price at which buyers are currently willing to buy. The ask, sometimes called the offer, is the price at which sellers are currently willing to sell. The ask is always higher than the bid. The gap between them is the spread.

If you buy at the ask and sell straight away at the bid, you lose the spread. This is why every trade starts slightly behind. A narrow spread means the price is close to the traders' agreed value. A wide spread means the market charges more to enter and leave, which is common in quiet markets or when news arrives.

::: diagram steps | Reading a quote
Bid: the price you receive when you sell at market
Ask: the price you pay when you buy at market
Spread: ask minus bid, a cost every trade starts with
Mid price: the point halfway between bid and ask, a reference only
:::

## 3. Three things that move the balance

The first is news. New information about a company, an economy or a commodity changes what people expect to happen next, and their expectations change their orders. Often the market moves before the news is fully understood.

The second is large orders. A single big order can use up the available sellers or buyers at a price and push the price to the next level. This is why very large trades sometimes move a market briefly.

The third is sentiment, the general mood of the people in the market. Sentiment can run ahead of facts, so prices sometimes move because traders expect others to react, not because of any new information. Recognising this is one of the hardest skills in trading.

## 4. Why some markets are harder to trade

A thin market has few buyers and few sellers at any moment. Orders have less to match against, so a small order can move the price and the spread tends to be wider. A thin market is not necessarily a bad market, but it punishes careless orders more than a busy one.

Markets are usually thinnest when a session opens or closes, during quiet hours, and around holidays. A beginner who trades in these periods pays more for the same trade than an experienced trader who waits.

::: case | The quiet morning
Joao watches the price of a commodity that his small import business uses. At 7 a.m., before most traders are active, the spread on his chosen instrument is much wider than it was the day before. He sees the price jump and wants to act at once.
? What is the most sensible first step for Joao?
- Buy immediately, because the price is moving and may keep rising || A thin market moves on small orders, and a fast move may reverse just as quickly.
* Wait until the market is busier, and check the spread before placing any order || A wider spread is a real cost, and waiting for normal trading conditions reduces it.
- Sell his stock of the commodity to lock in the current price || His business decision about stock is separate from his trading and should follow its own plan.
:::

## 5. What a price does and does not tell you

A price is the most recent agreement between buyers and sellers. It tells you what someone was willing to pay at that moment. It does not tell you the true value of the thing, and it does not tell you what will happen next. A high price is not necessarily a fair price, and a low price is not necessarily a bargain. The last trade is one data point among many.

Keep this in mind when you read headlines such as "the price has hit a record" or "the price has collapsed". These statements describe what happened. They do not explain why, and they do not tell you what to do.

::: quiz
? A market shows a bid of 100.00 and an ask of 100.20. What is the spread?
- 100.00, the bid itself || The spread is the difference between the two prices, not the bid.
* 0.20, the ask minus the bid || Ask minus bid gives the spread. Every trade starts with this cost.
- 100.10, the midpoint between the two prices || The midpoint is a reference point. The spread is the gap between the prices.
:::

::: quiz
? Which of these is the best description of what a price is?
- The true value of the asset, which the market eventually reveals || Price can differ from value for long periods, so it is not proof of true value.
* A record of the most recent agreement between buyers and sellers || This describes price as an agreement at a moment in time, which is what it is.
- A forecast of where the price will be next week || A price is not a forecast, although traders use it to form expectations.
:::

## Self-check

2. A bid is 50.00 and an ask is 50.40. If you buy and then sell immediately at the bid, what have you lost? (Answer: the spread of 0.40 per unit, before any other costs.)
3. Name one reason a market moves that is not new information. (Answer: sentiment, the general mood of traders, or a large order that shifts the balance.)

- The ask is higher than the bid, and the gap between them is a cost you pay on every trade.

## Key terms

::: terms
Order: an instruction to a broker to buy or sell an asset under stated conditions
Market order: an order to buy or sell immediately at the best available price
Limit order: an order to buy or sell only at a stated price or better
Stop order: an order that becomes a market order once a set price is reached
Fill: the execution of an order, in full or in part
Slippage: the difference between the price you expected and the price at which the order was filled
:::

## 1. The market order

A market order tells the broker to buy or sell now, at the best price available. In a busy market it fills quickly and close to the quoted price. That is its strength. Its weakness is that you do not control the exact price. If the market moves between your decision and the fill, you get the price that exists at the time, not the one you saw.

The gap between the expected price and the fill price is called slippage. It is usually small in liquid markets and larger when the market is thin or moving fast, for example during news. A market order is a reasonable choice when you value speed above the exact price, and when the market is busy enough that you can expect a close fill.

::: diagram steps | Using a market order
Check the market: is it busy, and is the spread normal?
Decide the size: how much you will buy or sell
Send the order: accept that the fill may differ slightly from the quote
Confirm the fill: check the price and size you actually received
:::

## 2. The limit order

A limit order sets the worst price you will accept. A buy limit fills at the limit price or lower. A sell limit fills at the limit price or higher. You control the price, but you give up certainty that the order will fill. If the market never reaches your limit, the order stays open and you do not trade.

Limit orders suit a trader who has a clear price in mind and can wait. They also let you avoid taking a wide spread in a quiet market.

::: case | The limit that never fills
Amelia wants to buy a currency pair if its price falls to a level she has chosen. She places a buy limit at that level and goes to bed. Overnight the price drops a little above her limit and then rises again without ever reaching it.
? What happened to her order, and what should she learn from it?
- It filled automatically at the nearest price, so she made the trade she wanted || A limit order fills only at the limit or better. The nearest price is not a fill.
* It stayed open and did not fill, so she did not trade, and she should check whether that outcome still suits her plan || A limit order protects the price but does not guarantee a trade. Missing a trade is a real outcome to plan for.
- It was cancelled by the broker because of the overnight move || Brokers do not cancel orders on their own for this reason. The order remained open and simply did not fill.
:::

## 3. The stop order

A stop order sits in the market and waits. When the price reaches the stop price, the order becomes a market order and is sent immediately. Traders use stops in two main ways. One is to limit a loss on an existing position, by placing a stop below a buy price or above a sell price. The other is to enter a trade when the price breaks through a level, which is sometimes called a breakout entry.

A stop is not a guarantee. Once triggered, it becomes a market order, so in a fast market it can fill far from the stop price. This is slippage in its harshest form, and it is most likely around news and at the opening of a session.

::: slides

SLIDE Stop order step 2 | Remember the stop becomes a market order; Expect a possible fill beyond the stop | A stop is not a guarantee
SLIDE Stop order step 3 | Review open stops daily; Cancel or move them only by your rules | Forgotten orders cause surprises

## 4. The stop-limit order

A stop-limit order combines the two. When the stop price is reached, it becomes a limit order rather than a market order. You control the price you will accept, but the order may not fill at all if the price moves past your limit quickly. It is useful when you care more about price than about getting out, and it carries the risk that you stay in a losing position because the order did not fill.

Many traders find that a stop-limit order gives a false sense of safety. Before using one, ask what you will do if it does not fill.

## 5. Choosing and checking an order

Start by asking what you want most. If the answer is speed, a market order may suit you. If it is the exact price, a limit order may suit you. If it is protection against a large loss, a stop order is the usual tool, with the understanding that it can fill beyond its stop.

Before you send any order, check four things: the instrument, the direction (buy or sell), the size, and the price or stop. Many costly mistakes come from one wrong digit or from a sell instead of a buy. Make the check a habit, and do not rush it.

::: tip | Practical tip
Read the order back to yourself before you confirm it. Say the instrument, the direction, the size and the price out loud. If anything sounds wrong, cancel and check again.
:::

::: quiz
? A trader wants to buy only if the price drops to a set level, and does not want to pay more. Which order fits best?
- A market order, because it fills fastest || Speed is not the goal here. The trader wants a specific maximum price.
* A buy limit order at that level, because it fills only at that price or lower || A buy limit sets the highest price the trader will accept.
- A sell stop order at that level, because it protects against a fall || A sell stop is used to exit a position, not to buy at a lower price.
:::

::: quiz
? Why can a stop order fill far from its stop price?
- Because brokers always add a fixed margin to stop orders || Brokers do not add a fixed margin to stop orders in this way.
* Because once triggered it becomes a market order, which fills at the best price available at that moment || A market order takes the available price, which can be far from the stop if the market moves fast.
- Because stop orders only work during the day || Some markets trade for long hours, and the reason for slippage is the market order, not the time of day.
:::

## Key terms

::: terms
Commission: a fee charged for executing a trade, sometimes shown separately from the spread
Financing: an interest-like charge for holding a leveraged position overnight, where the broker applies one
Currency conversion: exchanging money from one currency to another, usually at a rate that includes a margin
Requote: a broker offering a different price from the one you clicked, often in a fast market
Execution quality: how closely and how quickly your orders are filled at the prices you expected
Cost schedule: the broker's published list of fees, spreads and charges

## 1. Where the costs come from

Trading costs are easy to overlook because many of them are built into the price rather than charged as a separate line. The spread is the most important. Every trade starts with the spread, so a round trip, buying and then selling, costs at least one spread.

Other costs are often shown separately. A broker may charge a commission per trade. A broker may charge financing on positions held overnight, especially when the position is leveraged. Some brokers charge for inactivity, for deposits and for withdrawals. Currency conversion adds a cost whenever money moves between currencies. None of these costs is unusual, but together they can turn a small expected gain into a loss.

## 2. Slippage and the difference between quote and fill

Slippage is the gap between the price you expected and the price you received. It is most common in fast markets, around news, at the opening of a session and in thin markets. Slippage can work in your favour as well as against you. What matters is that you plan for it and do not assume that every fill will match the quote on your screen.

A simple way to plan for slippage is to add a margin to your cost estimate, based on how often you have seen it on your own demo or small live trades. Write the figure down and review it after a month.

::: diagram steps | Estimating the cost of one trade
Spread: the cost you pay to enter, which you see in the quote
Commission: any fee per trade, as stated in the cost schedule
Expected slippage: your own estimate, based on what you have observed
Total: add the three, then compare with the move you expect
:::

## 3. Currency conversion

If your account holds one currency and you trade assets priced in another, every conversion adds a cost. Brokers usually convert at a rate that includes a margin above the market rate. The difference may be small on a single trade but adds up over many trades and withdrawals.

Ask how conversions are priced before you deposit. A useful habit is to keep your trading money in the same currency as your daily expenses, so you do not convert money back and forth without need.

::: slides
SLIDE Conversion step 1 | Identify the currency of your account; Identify the currency of the asset | Know both currencies
SLIDE Conversion step 2 | Ask for the conversion margin in writing; Compare two brokers on one example | Compare before you trade
SLIDE Conversion step 3 | Limit conversions to what you need; Keep a record of each conversion | Small costs add up

## 4. Reading a cost schedule

A broker's cost schedule is the document that lists its charges. Read it before you open an account. Look first for the spread on the instruments you plan to trade, then for commissions, financing, currency conversion, inactivity fees, deposit and withdrawal fees. Ask whether any fee changes in fast or unusual markets.

Compare two brokers on the same trade. Take an example you could realistically place, and work out the total cost at each broker. A broker that looks cheap on one item may be expensive on another.

::: case | Two schedules
Miguel is choosing between two brokers. Broker X has a narrow spread but charges a commission on every trade. Broker Y has a slightly wider spread and no separate commission. He plans to make a few trades a month, each small.
? What is the most sensible next step for Miguel?
- Choose Broker X because a narrow spread is always cheaper || A narrow spread is only part of the cost. The commission can outweigh it on small trades.
* Work out the total cost of one of his planned trades at both brokers, including conversion and any inactivity fee, then decide || Comparing the full cost on his own trade is the only way to see which broker is cheaper for him.
- Choose Broker Y because a wider spread means a more honest broker || The width of the spread does not show a broker's honesty. Its regulation and its cost schedule do.
:::

## 5. Execution quality

Execution quality is part of the price. A broker that fills your orders slowly, rejects them often or requotes in fast markets is charging you something, even if its published costs look low. Ask how orders are filled, how often they are rejected and whether requotes occur. Test these on a demo account if you can, and watch the behaviour during news, which is when weak execution shows most clearly.

::: tip | Practical tip
Keep a simple log of each trade: the quoted price, the fill price, the commission and any conversion. After twenty trades you will know your real cost, which is more useful than any figure in a brochure.
:::

::: quiz
? A trade has a spread of 0.30, a commission of 0.10 per unit and expected slippage of 0.05 per unit. What is the total estimated cost per unit?
- 0.30, because only the spread matters || The spread is one cost. Commission and slippage also add to the total.
* 0.45, the sum of spread, commission and expected slippage || Adding the three gives the estimated cost per unit before any other charge.
- 0.10, because the commission is the largest single fee || Size of one fee does not give the total. Add all the costs.
:::

::: quiz
? Why does execution quality matter to the price you pay?
- Because a broker with good execution always has the lowest spread || Execution and spread are different things, although both affect cost.
* Because slow fills, rejections and requotes change the price you actually receive || A poor fill is a real cost even when the published spread looks low.
- Because execution quality only matters for professional traders || Every trader receives fills, so every trader is affected by execution quality.
:::

1. Why is the spread a cost even when no separate fee is charged? (Answer: every trade starts at the ask when buying and the bid when selling, so the gap is paid on entry and exit.)
2. What is slippage, and when is it most likely? (Answer: the gap between the expected and the filled price, most likely in fast markets, around news and in thin markets.)
3. What should you compare when choosing between two brokers? (Answer: the total cost of the same planned trade, including spread, commission, conversion and fees.)

## Practical assignment

Choose one broker you are considering, or use a demo account's cost information. Calculate the total cost of one trade you might realistically place, including spread, commission, conversion and expected slippage. Write the result on one page and keep it next to your trading plan.

## Summary

- Trading costs include the spread, commissions, financing, currency conversion and fees, and many are built into the price.
- Slippage and execution quality affect the price you actually receive, so plan for them rather than assuming the quote.
- Compare brokers on the total cost of the same trade, and keep a log of your own real costs.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- describe the difference between timeframes and why the choice matters;
- identify an uptrend, a downtrend and a sideways market on a chart;

## Key terms

::: terms
Candle: a visual summary of price over one period, showing the open, high, low and close
Timeframe: the length of time each candle represents, such as one minute, one hour or one day
Trend: a general direction of price over time, described by its highs and lows
Support: a price area where buying has often stopped a fall in the past
Resistance: a price area where selling has often stopped a rise in the past
Hindsight bias: the tendency to see a pattern clearly only after the outcome is known

## 1. What a candle shows

A candle summarises the price of an asset over one period. The body is the box between the open and the close. If the close is above the open, the body is usually shown filled or in one colour, and if the close is below the open, it is shown in another. The thin lines above and below the body, called wicks or shadows, show the highest and lowest prices reached during the period.

A candle is only a summary. Two candles can look almost identical and hide very different events. A long wick tells you that price went up or down sharply during the period and came back, but it does not tell you why.

::: diagram steps | Reading one candle
Open: the first price of the period
High: the highest price reached in the period
Low: the lowest price reached in the period
Close: the last price of the period, which decides the body colour
:::

## 2. Timeframes change the picture

The same market can look very different on different timeframes. A one-minute chart shows many small moves, most of them noise. A daily chart smooths those moves and shows the bigger direction. Neither is right or wrong. The question is which one fits the decision you are making.

A common beginner mistake is to switch timeframes until a pattern appears. This is a form of hindsight. Choose your timeframe first, for a reason you can state, and stick to it while you review your results.

::: slides
SLIDE Timeframe step 1 | Decide how long you will hold a position; Match the chart to that horizon | Horizon comes first
SLIDE Timeframe step 2 | Look at one longer chart for context; Use the shorter chart only for timing | Context before timing
SLIDE Timeframe step 3 | Write the timeframe in your notes; Do not change it after a loss | Stability keeps learning honest
:::

## 3. Trend: the direction of past prices

A trend describes the general direction of price. In an uptrend, each high is higher than the last, and each low is higher than the one before. In a downtrend, the highs and lows are both lower. A sideways market has no clear direction, with highs and lows staying within a range.

Trends are useful because they describe what has happened. They do not guarantee what will happen next. Markets can change direction, and a trend that looked clear can end suddenly. Treat a trend as a description to test, not as a promise.

## 4. Support and resistance

Support is a price area where buying has often stopped a fall in the past. Resistance is an area where selling has often stopped a rise. Traders mark these levels because prices sometimes pause or reverse near them. It is wise to treat them as zones rather than exact lines, because prices rarely stop at a single number.

A break of a level can be a useful signal to review your view, but it is not proof that the price will continue in the new direction. Many breaks reverse. Write down what would confirm or cancel your view before you act on a break.

::: case | The break that reversed
Nadia marked a resistance zone on a daily chart. The price moved above the zone one morning, and she was tempted to buy at once. Two days later the price had fallen back below the zone.
? What lesson should Nadia take from this?
- Breaks always continue, so she should have bought earlier || Breaks do not always continue. Assuming they will is the mistake.
* A break is a reason to review the view, and she should have written in advance what would confirm it before acting || Writing the rule before the move protects against chasing price.
- Levels are exact lines, so the price should not have moved above it || Levels are zones. Prices often move a little above or below them.
:::

## 5. The limits of chart reading

Charts describe past prices. Many patterns look obvious in hindsight, but are much harder to recognise as they form. Without records, you will remember the patterns that worked and forget the many that did not. This is why a trader who reads charts needs a written plan and a journal, and why reviewing results honestly matters more than finding a clever pattern.

Chart reading is a tool for describing a market and organising your thinking. It is not a way to predict the next move with certainty, and it cannot remove risk. Any claim of a chart pattern that always works should be treated with suspicion.

::: quiz
? A candle has a close above its open. How is its body usually shown?
- Filled in the colour for a falling price || A close above the open is a rising candle, so its body is shown in the rising colour.
* Shown in the colour used for a rising candle, because the close is above the open || The body colour shows whether the period closed above or below its open.
- Without a body, because only the wicks matter || The body is always shown, and it is the box between open and close.
:::

::: quiz
? A chart shows higher highs and higher lows. What does this describe?
- A guaranteed rise over the next month || A pattern describes past prices. It does not guarantee a future move.
* An uptrend, which describes the past direction of price || Higher highs and higher lows are the usual description of an uptrend.
- A sideways market with no direction || A sideways market has highs and lows that stay within a range.
:::

## Self-check

1. What do the wicks of a candle show? (Answer: the highest and lowest prices reached during the period.)
2. Why should you choose a timeframe before you look at the chart? (Answer: to avoid changing the timeframe until a pattern appears, which is hindsight.)
3. Why is a support or resistance level better treated as a zone? (Answer: prices rarely stop at one exact number, so the area near the level matters more.)

## Practical assignment

Choose one asset and one timeframe. For ten trading days, record the trend, the main support and resistance zones, and one sentence on what would prove your reading wrong. Do not trade. At the end, check how often your reading held.

## Summary

- A candle shows the open, high, low and close for one period, and the timeframe you choose changes what you see.
- Trends and levels describe past prices, and a break of a level is a reason to review, not a guarantee.
- Chart reading organises thinking and cannot remove risk, so keep written rules and an honest review.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- name the main kinds of events that move prices and explain how they differ;
- explain liquidity and why it changes during the day and around holidays;
- describe a gap and how it can affect an order or a stop;
- set out a simple plan for trading around scheduled news.

## Key terms

::: terms
Scheduled release: an economic or company announcement that appears on a published calendar in advance
Liquidity: the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold without moving its price much
Gap: a jump in price from one level to another with little or no trading in between
Volatility: how much and how quickly a price moves over a period
Event risk: the chance that a specific announcement moves a price sharply
Overreaction: a move that goes further than the new information justifies, often followed by a partial reversal
:::

## 1. What moves prices

Prices move when expectations change. The main sources are scheduled releases, such as official statistics or company results published on a known date. They are called scheduled because everyone knows they are coming, but nobody knows the result in advance. Unscheduled events, such as a sudden political development, a supply interruption or a rumour, move prices without warning. These can be the most dangerous, because there is no time to plan.

The size of a move depends on how much the result differs from what people expected. A figure that matches expectations often moves a market little. A surprise can move it a lot. This is why traders watch the expected figure as closely as the actual one.

## 2. Liquidity changes during the day

Liquidity is the ease of buying or selling without moving the price much. It tends to be highest when the main markets are open and most traders are active. It tends to fall during quiet hours, around public holidays and just before major events, when many participants wait on the sidelines.

Low liquidity does not mean the market is broken. It means each order has more effect on the price, and spreads are usually wider. A trade that looks fine on a busy afternoon can cost much more in a quiet hour.

::: diagram cycle | A day of changing liquidity
Opening: wider spreads and fast moves as orders arrive
Busy hours: tighter spreads and steadier prices
Quiet hours: thinner trading and more sensitivity to single orders
Close: positioning ahead of the next session or event
:::

## 3. Gaps

A gap is a jump in price from one level to the next, with little or no trading between them. Gaps often happen between sessions, over a weekend or after a surprise announcement. A gap can carry the price past a stop order. A stop triggered in a gap fills at the first available price, which may be far from the stop level.

This is a serious risk for anyone who relies on a stop to limit a loss. A stop is a useful tool, but it cannot protect against every gap. Position size matters here, because a smaller position limits the damage a gap can cause.

::: slides
SLIDE Gap step 1 | Know when the market closes and opens; Note events that fall outside normal hours | Gaps follow closed time
SLIDE Gap step 2 | Assume a stop may fill beyond its level; Size positions so a gap is survivable | Plan for the worst fill
SLIDE Gap step 3 | Review gaps after they happen; Adjust rules, not your emotions | Learn from each gap
:::

## 4. Planning around scheduled news

Scheduled news is the easiest kind of event to plan for, because you know the time. Before the event, decide whether you will trade it at all. Many experienced traders choose to stay out of major releases, because spreads widen, slippage increases and the first move is often reversed. If you do trade, decide your size, your maximum loss and your exit in advance.

Do not trade an event you have not studied. Reading the headline is not enough. Understand what the figure measures, what the market expected, and how the release has moved the market before.

::: case | The announcement at ten
Daniel has a small position in a commodity. An important economic figure is due at ten o'clock. The figure is a surprise, the price jumps and then reverses within a few minutes. He was not watching at the time, and his stop filled well below its level.
? What would have been the most useful step before the announcement?
- Add to the position, because the first move suggests the trend is strong || The first move is often reversed, and adding to a position before a release increases the risk.
* Decide in advance whether to hold through the release, and size the position so the worst fill is survivable || A planned choice about size and exposure protects against surprise and gaps.
- Ignore the calendar, because scheduled news cannot change prices || Scheduled news is one of the most common reasons prices move sharply.
:::

## 5. Avoid overreacting

The first move after news is not always the real one. Markets sometimes overreact, then partly reverse as traders think harder about what the news means. Headlines are not analysis. A headline tells you what happened, not what the market will do next, and it often arrives after the fastest traders have already acted.

Keep to your rules. If you did not plan to trade an event, do not start because a headline excites you. If you did plan it, follow the plan even when the move looks convincing.

::: quiz
? Why can a stop order fill far from its stop price after a gap?
- Because stops are only active during the day || Stops can be active whenever the market trades. The gap is the cause.
* Because the price jumps past the stop level, and the stop fills at the first available price || A gap skips over the stop level, so the fill comes at the next price available.
- Because brokers add a fixed delay to stop orders || Brokers do not add a fixed delay to explain gap fills.
:::

::: quiz
? What is the most reasonable response to a sudden headline on a market you hold?
- Trade at once, because the first move shows the real direction || The first move is often reversed. Acting at once without a plan is overreaction.
* Check your plan, your position size and your maximum loss before you decide anything || Following the plan you wrote before the headline is the disciplined response.
- Close all positions in every market, because headlines are always dangerous || Closing everything is not a plan either. Respond according to the plan you made.
:::
